Photo metadata explained

What a photo stores besides the picture: camera and date (EXIF), location (GPS), edits and credits (XMP and IPTC), and how to check or remove it before you share.

OverviewHow it worksFAQAI image metadataPhoto metadata

What photo metadata is

Every photo from a phone or camera carries more than the picture. The camera writes the date and time, its make and model, the lens and the exposure settings; with location turned on, a phone adds the exact place. Editing apps add their own name, the edits and sometimes your name. All of this is metadata: data about the photo, stored inside the same file.

The main standards are EXIF (camera data), GPS (a part of EXIF), XMP (an extensible XML packet used by editing apps) and IPTC (captions and credits from the news world). Pictures can also carry colour profiles, thumbnails, maker notes and even extra pictures or a short video.

GPS: where the photo was taken

With location tagging on, a phone stores latitude and longitude, often with altitude, direction and the time from the satellites. The position is usually accurate to a few metres, enough to find a house. Photos taken at home, at a school or at work can show those places to anyone who receives the files.

Turning location tags off stops new photos from getting a position. On an iPhone, go to Settings, then Privacy & Security, Location Services, Camera, and choose Never. On Android, open the Camera app's settings and turn off the location tag option (its name differs between phone makers). Photos that already have a position keep it until you remove it.

How exact is the position in a photo?

Phones combine satellite positioning with Wi-Fi and mobile network data, so outdoors the stored position is often accurate to within a few metres; indoors it can be off by tens of metres. Many photos also store the altitude, the direction the camera was facing and the speed, and some store how accurate the fix was.

Even a rough position matters when many photos are shared: a set of pictures taken in the evenings and at weekends usually points to a home, and pictures taken on weekday mornings point to a school or an office.

Dates, times and time zones

EXIF stores when a photo was taken, when it was digitised and when the file was last changed, usually to the second and sometimes with fractions. Newer cameras and phones also store the time zone offset, such as +05:00, and the GPS block keeps its own time in UTC.

Together these can show where in the world you were and when you travelled, even without a position. Many cameras keep their clock set to the home time zone while travelling, which can give away where you live. Note that most apps show the date taken from EXIF, not the file date, so editing the file's date on your computer does not change it.

XMP: edits, credits and history

XMP is a block of XML that editing apps such as Lightroom, Photoshop, phone gallery editors and many online tools write. It can hold the creator's name, copyright and contact details, ratings and keywords, the editing app, a record of the edits and the IDs of the original file (so copies can be linked back to it). AI tools use XMP for credit lines and for the digital source type.

Because XMP is extensible, apps can store almost anything in it. That is useful for photographers who manage large libraries, and it is also why an XMP block can contain more than you expect.

IPTC: captions and credits

IPTC fields come from news agencies and are used by stock sites and photo libraries: headline, caption, keywords, the photographer, credit and copyright, and the city, region and country where the photo was taken. They are stored in an older binary block and, in newer files, inside XMP. Place names in IPTC can reveal a location even when there is no GPS position.

Other hidden data

  • Colour profile (ICC): describes the colours, for example Display P3. Harmless and useful; cleaners usually keep it.
  • Embedded thumbnails: small preview copies. If a photo was cropped in an app that did not update the thumbnail, the thumbnail may still show the full picture.
  • Motion photos, depth maps and HDR gain maps: some phones add a short video or extra pictures after the main picture in the same file.
  • PNG text chunks: free text notes, used by many AI image tools for prompts and settings.
  • Content Credentials (C2PA): a signed record of how the picture was made or edited.

Screenshots, edited copies and RAW files

A screenshot of a photo does not carry the original photo's metadata, but it gets its own: the device, the date and sometimes the app it was taken in. An edited copy is different: many editors keep the original EXIF, including the GPS position, and add their own XMP on top, so an edited photo can hold more than the original.

RAW files from cameras (DNG, CR3, NEF, ARW and others) carry the most metadata of all, and photo apps often keep edits and keywords in a separate sidecar file with the same name and the .xmp extension. If you share a folder of photos, check what is inside before you send it.

Videos and motion photos

Phone videos store the location, the device and the recording time as well, in their own boxes inside the MP4 or MOV file. Live Photos and motion photos are a photo plus a few seconds of video, and both parts can carry the position. Clean videos with a tool made for them, because photo cleaners do not read video files.

What apps and websites do with it

Many chat apps and social networks remove most metadata when you send or post a photo, partly for your privacy and partly to make files smaller. But this is not guaranteed for every app, every setting or every way of sending: photos sent as documents or files, by email or as cloud links usually arrive with all their metadata. Websites you upload to may also read the metadata before they remove it from the copy they show.

The risks in plain words

  • Home and routine: GPS positions and dates can show where you live, where your children go to school and when you are away.
  • Identity: names in EXIF, XMP and IPTC, and serial numbers that tie photos to one camera.
  • More than you meant to share: thumbnails, motion photos and edit histories.
  • For AI pictures: prompts, file paths and account names.

Keep or remove?

Removing metadata is right before you share photos with strangers, sell items online, post pictures of your home or children, or send files to people you do not know well. Keeping it is useful in your own photo library, for your memories (dates and places) and for professional photos where the copyright and credit should stay attached. You can do both: keep the originals and share cleaned copies.

A short glossary

  • EXIF: the camera's data block in JPG, HEIC, WebP and many other files, organised in folders called IFDs.
  • IFD0, IFD1: the main folder of EXIF tags and the folder of the embedded thumbnail.
  • Maker note: a private block inside EXIF whose layout only the camera maker documents.
  • XMP: an XML block for editing data, credits and history; it can also live in a separate .xmp sidecar file.
  • IPTC IIM: the older binary block for captions and credits, still written by many apps next to XMP.
  • ICC profile: the description of the colours; it holds no personal data.
  • MPF: the Multi-Picture Format index that links extra pictures, such as depth maps or HDR gain maps, stored in one JPG.
  • HEIF: the container used by HEIC and AVIF files, which stores EXIF and XMP as separate items next to the picture.

A quick checklist before sharing

  • Is location tagging on? Check one photo with Photo Privacy Check.
  • Does the photo show your house number, car plate or school badge? Metadata tools cannot remove what the picture shows.
  • Sending as a document or file? Clean it first, because the file arrives exactly as it is.
  • Cleaned it? Open the clean copy in Photo Privacy Check: it should say no private details were found.
