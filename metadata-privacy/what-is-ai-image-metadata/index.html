What is AI image metadata?

Prompts, seeds, model names, the IPTC digital source type and Content Credentials: where AI image tools store them, how to read them, and what they can and cannot tell you.

OverviewHow it worksFAQAI image metadataPhoto metadata

The short answer

AI image metadata is the hidden information an AI image tool, an editing app or a website stores inside a picture file: the prompt you typed, the settings that were used, the name of the model and the app, and sometimes a signed record of how the picture was made. It sits next to the picture data, so you do not see it when you look at the image, but anyone who gets the file can read it.

Some of it is useful, for example to repeat a result or to show honestly how a picture was made. Some of it can be private: a prompt can contain names and places, a file path can contain your user name, and some generators store the name of your account.

Where the information is stored

Different tools use different places inside the file. A single picture can carry several of them at once.

Prompts and generation settings

Local Stable Diffusion tools usually write one block of text called "parameters". It starts with the prompt, may continue with "Negative prompt:" and ends with a line of settings, separated by commas. A typical settings line looks like "Steps: 28, Sampler: DPM++ 2M Karras, CFG scale: 6.5, Seed: 1234567890, Size: 768x512, Model: dreamshaper_8".

ComfyUI stores two JSON texts instead: "prompt", the graph of nodes that actually ran, with every input value, and "workflow", the editor layout. From the prompt graph you can read the sampler node's seed, steps, CFG scale, sampler and scheduler, the text of the prompt nodes connected to it, the checkpoint and any LoRA files.

Reading a settings line, step by step

Take a typical line: "Steps: 28, Sampler: DPM++ 2M Karras, CFG scale: 6.5, Seed: 1234567890, Size: 768x512, Model hash: 6ce0161689, Model: dreamshaper_8". It says the model refined the picture 28 times with the DPM++ 2M sampler and the Karras noise schedule, followed the prompt fairly closely (6.5 is a middle value), started from the random noise number 1234567890, made a picture 768 pixels wide and 512 high, and used a checkpoint called dreamshaper_8 whose file fingerprint starts with 6ce0161689.

Extra items often follow: "Lora hashes" lists the add-on models and their fingerprints, "Hires upscale" and "Denoising strength" describe a second pass that made the picture larger, and "Version" names the web UI release. Values that contain commas are written in quotes, so a reader must not simply split the line at every comma.

With the same model file, settings, seed and software version, the same picture can usually be made again. That is why people share these lines, and also why a picture can reveal which models and add-ons sit on your computer.

AI metadata in JPG and WebP files

PNG files have free text chunks, so most local tools write their settings there. When the same tools save a JPG or WebP, they put the settings text into the EXIF "user comment" field instead, and some add XMP. Online services that deliver JPG or WebP files more often use XMP, IPTC or Content Credentials.

This is also why prompts so often disappear: converting a PNG to JPG in most apps keeps only the picture, a screenshot makes a new file, and many websites remove all of it when you upload. If you want to keep the settings of your own pictures, keep the original files.

Generator names and the "digital source type"

Many apps write their own name into a software or creator tool field, and some services add a credit line such as "Made with" followed by their name. These fields are plain text: any app can write anything into them, and most editors overwrite them.

The IPTC "digital source type" is a standard way to state where a picture came from. It is stored in XMP or IPTC, and inside Content Credentials actions. Its value is a web address that ends in a short code:

  • trainedAlgorithmicMedia: made by a generative AI model from a prompt.
  • compositeWithTrainedAlgorithmicMedia: a picture that was partly made or edited with generative AI.
  • algorithmicMedia: made by an algorithm without trained data, for example a fractal.
  • digitalCapture: taken with a camera.
  • digitalArt: drawn or composed by a person with software.
  • composite: put together from several pictures.

Content Credentials (C2PA)

Content Credentials are the most structured kind of AI image metadata. The C2PA standard stores a "manifest store" inside the file: one or more manifests, each with a claim (which app made it, the title and format), assertions (actions such as created, edited or cropped, the digital source type, ingredients such as the original photo, sometimes a thumbnail) and a cryptographic signature with the signer's certificate. The last manifest describes the current state of the file.

A signature tells you that the manifest was not changed since it was signed by the holder of that certificate. It does not tell you that the picture is true, and a picture without credentials is not fake: most apps and websites simply do not add them, and many remove them on upload.

What AI image metadata cannot tell you

  • Whether a picture is AI-made. Metadata can be removed, changed or invented. Its absence proves nothing, and its presence only shows what some app wrote.
  • Anything about visible labels. Badges such as "AI info" or "Made with AI" that social platforms show are added by the platform, often from their own checks; they are not stored in your copy of the file.
  • Anything about watermarks in the pixels. Some AI services draw visible marks or hide invisible watermarks in the pixels themselves. They are part of the picture, not metadata, and metadata tools do not touch them.
  • What detectors decide. AI detectors analyse the picture itself. Removing metadata does not change that analysis.

Why people remove it, and why some keep it

Privacy is the usual reason. A prompt can contain a real person's name, a place, a client or a private idea. A ComfyUI workflow can include the names and folders of your model files. Some generators store your user name. Content Credentials can name the account that made the picture and list the files it was made from. Removing these details before you share a picture is reasonable, in the same way that people remove GPS data from holiday photos.

There are also good reasons to keep it: credentials help others see how a picture was made, prompts and seeds let you or a colleague reproduce a result, and a credit line gives credit. If you share AI pictures publicly, being open about how they were made builds trust.

  • This is privacy cleanup. Visible labels such as "AI info" or "Made with AI" and watermarks drawn into the pixels stay, and platforms may still recognise AI-made pictures by other means. Removing metadata does not remove a duty to label AI-made content where a law or a platform rule asks for it.

Labelling rules still apply

Removing metadata only changes the hidden data in your copy of the file. It does not change any duty you may have to say that content is AI-made. Rules such as the transparency duties of the EU AI Act and the policies of the major social platforms ask people, in many cases, to disclose realistic AI-made or AI-altered pictures, video and audio. Check the rules that apply to you and to the place where you publish.

A short glossary

  • Checkpoint (model): the main model file that makes the picture, for example an SDXL or FLUX checkpoint.
  • LoRA: a small add-on model that teaches a style, a person or an object, used with a strength such as 0.8.
  • VAE: the part that turns the model's internal result into visible pixels; some workflows name their own.
  • Hash: a short fingerprint of a file, used to name the exact model or add-on that was used.
  • Workflow: in ComfyUI, the graph of nodes that made the picture, saved as JSON inside the PNG.
  • Manifest: in Content Credentials, one signed record of how the file was made or changed.
  • Claim generator: the app that wrote and signed a manifest.
  • Assertion: one statement inside a manifest, such as the list of actions or the ingredients.
  • Ingredient: a file that was used to make this one, such as the original photo of an edit.
  • Digital source type: the IPTC code that says whether a picture was captured, drawn, composited or made by AI.
