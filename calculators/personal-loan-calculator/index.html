How it works

Enter the loan amount, the yearly interest rate and the term. The calculator finds the fixed monthly payment, the month the loan is paid off and the full schedule. Many lenders also charge an origination fee, either taken out of the loan (you get less cash but repay the whole amount) or paid upfront, and some add monthly loan insurance. Add them under Fees and insurance: the payment stays the same, but the cost of the loan and its real APR go up. The APR is the yearly rate at which your payments are worth exactly the cash you end up with.

The formula

  • Monthly rate r = yearly rate ÷ 100 ÷ 12; payments n = years × 12 + months
  • Monthly payment P = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n); at 0% interest P = L ÷ n
  • Fee = L × fee % ÷ 100, or the amount you type; cash received = L − fee when it is taken out of the loan
  • Cost of the loan = total interest + fee + insurance × n
  • Real APR = 12 × x, where x solves L − fee = (P + insurance) × (1 − (1 + x)^−n) ÷ x

A worked example

A 20,000 loan at 10% for 5 years: r = 0.10 ÷ 12 and n = 60, so P = 424.94 a month and 5,496.45 of interest in all. With a 5% fee taken out of the loan you receive 19,000 but still repay 60 × 424.94, so the real APR is the x that makes those payments worth 19,000: 12.239%.

Questions

Why is the APR higher than my interest rate?

The interest rate only prices the money you borrow. The APR also counts the fee (and any loan insurance), so it shows the full yearly cost. With a fee taken out of the loan you repay the whole amount but receive less cash, which is the same as borrowing at a higher rate.

Is it better to have the fee taken out of the loan or to pay it upfront?

For the same fee the APR is the same either way, because in both cases the cash you keep is the loan minus the fee. Taking it out of the loan means you need no cash on day one; paying upfront means the full amount reaches your account.

Why does loan insurance push the APR up so much?

A monthly insurance charge is paid on top of every installment, so over a few years it can add up to more than the interest. The APR here counts it as a cost of borrowing. If the insurance is optional, leave it at 0 to see the loan alone.

Can I use it for a loan in my own currency?

Yes. Pick the currency symbol at the top. The maths is the same everywhere; check that your lender quotes a yearly rate on the balance: a flat rate charged on the original amount costs much more than the same rate on the balance.
