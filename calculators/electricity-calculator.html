What an electricity cost calculator tells you

An electricity cost calculator shows how much energy one appliance uses and what that energy costs you per day, week, month and year. Power companies do not bill for watts; they bill for energy, counted in kilowatt-hours (kWh). One kWh is the energy used when 1,000 watts run for one hour, and it is the same thing as the "unit" printed on bills in Pakistan, India and many other countries.

Watts measure how fast an appliance uses energy, like the speed of a car. Kilowatt-hours measure how much it used in total, like the distance driven. A 2,000 W kettle that boils for 3 minutes uses only 0.1 kWh, while a 10 W LED bulb left on for 10 hours also uses 0.1 kWh. The price you pay depends on both numbers, which is why this calculator asks for the power and the time.

The answer is an estimate for one appliance. Your actual bill also covers every other device in the home, fixed monthly charges and taxes, and some tariffs charge more per unit once you pass a certain number of units.

How to use the calculator, step by step

  • Appliance: keep My own appliance (type its power), or pick one of 25 common appliances such as a refrigerator, ceiling fan, room air conditioner, water heater, microwave or laptop. Each preset shows its typical wattage.
  • Power rating: type the number from the label and choose the unit: W, kW, BTU/h, hp (mechanical), hp (metric) or tons of cooling power. With a preset, type your own wattage under Its power, if you know it, or leave it empty to use the typical value.
  • Share of the time at full power: leave 100% for things that run steadily, such as a fan or a TV. Lower it for appliances that cycle on and off under a thermostat; the refrigerator preset uses 33%.
  • Time in use: type a number and choose Per: minutes a day, hours a day, hours a week, hours a month, or whole days a week, a month or a year. With a preset, Hours used a day, if you know replaces the starting guess.
  • Price of electricity: the price of one kWh in your currency. Pick the currency from the currency menu, so the result shows Rs, $, £ or another symbol.

Understanding the results

The two headline figures are the Cost per month (with the kWh for a month underneath) and the Cost per year. Below them come the Cost per day, the Power when running, the Use converted to hours a day, and the Average power over a day, which is the steady wattage that would use the same energy if it ran around the clock. The Energy and cost table lists kWh and cost per day, week, month and year side by side.

A month here is 30.44 days and a year 365.25 days, so twelve months add up to a year. If your bill covers a different number of days, multiply the daily figure by the days on the bill.

Worked examples: a PC, a fridge and an electric car

Gaming PC. A computer drawing 350 W for 5 hours a day uses 1.75 kWh a day and 53.27 kWh a month. At 0.18 per kWh, close to the US average residential price of 18.16 cents per kWh reported by the US Energy Information Administration for August 2026, that is $9.59 a month and $115.05 a year.

Refrigerator. The fridge preset (725 W, running a third of the time, 24 hours a day) works out to 5.74 kWh a day, an average draw of 239.3 W, and about 174.79 kWh a month: $31.46 a month at 18 cents. Many newer, efficient fridges use far less, so read the yearly kWh figure on the energy label if it has one and compare.

Electric car. Charging at 7 kW for 1.5 hours a day adds 10.5 kWh a day and 319.62 kWh a month, which costs $57.53 a month at 18 cents. Some energy is lost as heat while charging, so the meter reads a little more than the battery receives.

Lighting. Swapping one 60 W incandescent bulb for a 10 W LED, both lit 6 hours a day, cuts that light's use from 10.96 to 1.83 kWh a month. At 18 cents that saves about $19.72 a year for each bulb.

Electricity bill in Pakistan: units, slabs and an AC example

On a Pakistani bill from a distribution company such as LESCO, GEPCO, MEPCO or IESCO, "units consumed" means kWh. Residential tariffs approved by the regulator NEPRA charge a different rate for different slabs of monthly units, and the bill adds fixed charges, fuel price adjustments, surcharges and taxes on top of the energy charge. Because those rates are revised often, this calculator does not store them: type your own price per unit.

For a realistic price, divide the total of a recent bill by the units on it. Suppose that comes to Rs 55 per unit (an example figure, not a tariff). An inverter air conditioner whose label lists an input power of 1,600 W, run 8 hours a day at full power, uses 12.8 units a day and 389.63 units a month, about Rs 21,429.76 a month at that price. In practice an inverter unit slows down once the room is cool, so set the share of the time at full power below 100% if you know its real average draw.

A 75 W ceiling fan running 12 hours a day uses 0.9 units a day and 27.4 units a month, about Rs 1,506.78 at the same example price. Adding up each appliance this way shows which ones push your monthly total into a higher slab.

Watts, BTU, horsepower and tons

Labels give power in several units, and the calculator converts them all to watts. Kilowatts are 1,000 W. Horsepower is used for motors and pumps: 1 mechanical hp is 745.70 W and 1 metric hp is 735.50 W. BTU per hour and tons describe heating or cooling capacity: 1 BTU/h is 0.29307 W, and 1 ton of refrigeration is 12,000 BTU/h, or 3,516.85 W.

Be careful with air conditioners. A 12,000 BTU/h (1 ton) or 18,000 BTU/h (1.5 ton) rating is the heat the unit removes, not the electricity it uses. Entered as BTU/h, 12,000 BTU/h for 8 hours a day would come to 856.42 kWh a month, more than three times the real use of a typical unit. Divide the BTU/h by the energy efficiency ratio (EER) on the label, or read the input watts, and type that instead: 12,000 ÷ 11 is about 1,100 W.

If the label shows only volts and amps, multiply them for an upper limit on the watts: 230 V × 2 A = 460 W. Motors usually draw less real power than volts × amps suggests, and a plug-in power meter gives the most accurate figure of all.

Tips for a lower electricity bill

  • Start with the biggest numbers in the table: heating water, heating or cooling rooms and old refrigerators use far more than lights and chargers.
  • Raise the cooling setpoint a little and clean air conditioner filters; a unit that reaches the setpoint spends more of its time idling.
  • Replace the remaining incandescent bulbs with LEDs, which give the same light for a fraction of the watts.
  • Switch off devices at the plug when they are not in use for long periods, and check standby power with a plug-in meter.
  • When buying, compare the yearly kWh on the energy labels, not only the price tag; multiply by your price per unit and by the years you expect to keep it.
  • Use heavy appliances (washer, iron, pump) outside peak hours if your tariff charges more at peak times.

How it works

An electricity bill counts energy in kilowatt-hours (kWh), the "units" on the meter: 1 kWh is 1,000 watts used for one hour. Type the appliance's power from its label, how long you use it and the price of one unit from your bill, and the calculator shows the energy and the cost per day, week, month and year. Some appliances switch themselves on and off, such as a fridge or an air conditioner with a thermostat, so they draw full power only part of the time: set that share below 100%. Or pick a common appliance from the list to start with a typical wattage, then type your own wattage or hours if you know them.

The formula

  • Energy per day (kWh) = power (kW) × share of the time at full power × hours used per day
  • Per week = per day × 7; per month = per day × 30.44; per year = per day × 365.25
  • Cost = energy (kWh) × price per kWh
  • Power units: 1 kW = 1,000 W; 1 BTU/h = 0.29307 W; 1 hp (mechanical) = 745.70 W; 1 hp (metric) = 735.50 W; 1 ton of cooling = 12,000 BTU/h = 3,516.85 W
  • Air conditioner input power = cooling capacity (BTU/h) ÷ its energy efficiency ratio (EER, on the label); 12,000 ÷ 11 ≈ 1,100 W

A worked example

A gaming PC that draws 350 W for 5 hours a day uses 0.35 kW × 5 h = 1.75 kWh a day. At 0.18 per kWh that is 0.32 a day; over a month it is 1.75 × 30.44 = 53.27 kWh, costing 9.59, and over a year 639.19 kWh, costing 115.05.

Questions

How do I calculate electricity cost from watts?

Divide the watts by 1,000 to get kilowatts, multiply by the hours used to get kWh, then multiply by the price per kWh. A 350 W PC used 5 hours is 0.35 × 5 = 1.75 kWh, which costs 0.32 at 0.18 per kWh.

How do I calculate electricity cost per unit?

Divide the total of your bill by the units (kWh) it charges for. That average includes fixed charges and taxes, which makes it a realistic price to type here; the energy rate alone is on your tariff.

How much do 500 units of electricity cost?

Multiply 500 by your price per unit: at an example price of Rs 55 a unit it would be Rs 27,500. In Pakistan the rate depends on the slab and the tariff in force, and taxes and adjustments are added, so check a recent bill for your real average.

Where do I find the wattage?

On the rating label or sticker (often on the back or under the base), on the charger, or in the manual. If it only shows volts and amps, multiply them: 230 V × 2 A = 460 W. A plug-in power meter gives the most accurate number, because many devices use less than their rating.

Why does a fridge use only part of its rated power?

Its compressor switches on and off to hold the temperature. The US Department of Energy suggests counting about a third of the time at full power, which is why the fridge preset uses 33%. Air conditioners and heaters with thermostats behave the same way.

My air conditioner says 12,000 BTU. Is that its power?

No, that is how much heat it removes per hour. The electricity it uses is that number divided by its efficiency ratio (EER) on the label, about 1,000 to 1,200 W for a 12,000 BTU/h unit. Type that wattage, not 12,000 BTU/h.

How do I calculate the electricity cost of charging an electric car?

Multiply the charger's power by the hours it charges, or use the kWh your car says it added, then multiply by the price per kWh. A 7 kW charger running 1.5 hours a day uses 10.5 kWh a day, about 319.62 kWh a month.

What is the formula for an electricity bill?

Energy charge = units (kWh) × price per unit, and units = watts × hours ÷ 1,000 for each appliance. A real bill then adds fixed charges, adjustments and taxes set by your supplier.

My bill has slabs (different prices per unit). What price do I use?

Use the price of the slab your monthly total falls in, or your bill's total amount divided by the units used for an average price. Taxes and fixed charges are not part of the price per unit.

What is the average electricity cost per kWh?

It depends on the country and changes over time. In the US the average residential price was 18.16 cents per kWh in August 2026 according to the Energy Information Administration; elsewhere, use the rate on your own bill.

How long is a month here?

An average month of 30.44 days (365.25 days ÷ 12, rounded), so twelve months add up to about a year.
