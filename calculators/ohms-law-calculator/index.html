What Ohm's law is, in simple terms

Ohm's law says that the current through a conductor is proportional to the voltage across it: double the voltage and the current doubles. Written as a formula, V = I × R, where V is the voltage in volts, I the current in amperes (amps) and R the resistance in ohms (Ω). The German physicist Georg Ohm published it in 1827 after experiments with wires of different lengths.

A water pipe is a handy picture. Voltage is the pressure pushing the water, current is how much flows each second, and resistance is how narrow the pipe is. More pressure pushes more water through; a narrower pipe lets less through at the same pressure.

Power ties in with a second rule, P = V × I, measured in watts. Combine the two and any two of the four quantities give you the other two. That is what this Ohm's law calculator does: type two values, leave two empty, and it works out the rest with the formulas shown.

Worked examples: charger, LED resistor, speaker and mains iron

Phone charger. A charger delivering 5 V at 2 A gives 10 W of power, and the load it is driving behaves like 2.5 Ω.

LED resistor. Say an LED's data sheet gives a forward voltage of 2 V at 20 mA, and you run it from 12 V. The resistor in series must take the other 10 V at 20 mA. Type 10 V and 20 mA: R = 10 ÷ 0.02 = 500 Ω, and the resistor turns 0.2 W into heat, so pick one rated above 0.2 W; a 0.5 W resistor runs cooler.

Speaker. An 8 Ω speaker playing at 50 W takes I = √(50 ÷ 8) = 2.5 A at V = √(50 × 8) = 20 V. A speaker's ohm rating is a nominal impedance, so treat this as a guide.

Mains iron. A 1,000 W iron on 230 V draws 4.35 A, and its heating element measures 52.9 Ω while hot. Cold, the element reads lower on a meter, because metal resistance rises with temperature.

Small battery and coil. A 3.7 V cell across a 0.5 Ω coil pushes 7.4 A and makes 27.38 W. Before running a low resistance from a battery, check that the current is within the battery's rated continuous discharge.

Ohm's law in series and parallel circuits

In a series circuit the parts sit one after another, so the same current flows through all of them and their resistances add: R = R₁ + R₂ + …. A 100 Ω and a 220 Ω resistor in series make 320 Ω. Across a 9 V battery that gives 0.028125 A (28.125 mA) and 0.253 W in total; the voltage splits in proportion, 2.81 V across the 100 Ω and 6.19 V across the 220 Ω.

In a parallel circuit each part has the full voltage across it, and the currents add. The total resistance is found from 1 ÷ R = 1 ÷ R₁ + 1 ÷ R₂ + …, and it is always smaller than the smallest branch. The same two resistors in parallel make 100 × 220 ÷ (100 + 220) = 68.75 Ω, so the 9 V battery supplies 0.1309 A (130.909 mA) and 1.18 W.

To solve a mixed circuit, reduce it step by step to one equivalent resistance, use this calculator for the total current, then work back through each branch. For resistor colour bands and standard values, see the Resistor Calculator below.

Where Ohm's law works, and where it does not

Ohm's law is an observed rule, not a law of nature for every material. It holds well for metal wires and resistors at a steady temperature, called ohmic devices, whose current-voltage graph is a straight line. A diode or LED is non-ohmic: almost no current flows until the voltage passes a threshold, then the current climbs steeply, which is why an LED always needs a resistor or a current-limited driver.

In AC circuits with capacitors and inductors the idea extends to impedance, V = Z × I, where Z depends on frequency. For heaters, kettles, irons and filament lamps on AC mains, use the RMS voltage (such as 230 V or 120 V) and the law works as usual. Motors and electronics draw current that is out of step with the voltage, so their real power is less than volts × amps; three-phase supplies add a further factor, so use a dedicated three-phase method for them.

Tips and common mistakes

  • Convert prefixes first: 4.7 kΩ is 4,700 Ω and 250 mA is 0.25 A. Most hand-calculation errors are a factor of 1,000.
  • Measure resistance with the power off. A multimeter on the ohms range can be damaged, and reads nonsense, in a live circuit.
  • Check power ratings. A resistor that must turn 0.2 W into heat needs a rating above that, not exactly that.
  • Hot and cold resistance differ for heaters and lamps; use the running values for running currents.
  • Respect mains voltage. Use these sums for planning, and leave work on household wiring to a qualified electrician.

How it works

Four quantities describe a simple circuit: voltage V (the push, in volts), current I (the flow, in amperes), resistance R (how hard the path resists, in ohms) and power P (the energy used each second, in watts). They are tied together by two rules, V = I × R and P = V × I, so as soon as you know any two of them the other two follow. Type two values, pick each one's unit, and leave the other two boxes empty. The answer is shown in volts, amperes, ohms and watts, with a shorter form such as 7.29 mA under it, and the steps show which formula was used.

The formula

  • V = I × R (Ohm's law)
  • P = V × I = I² × R = V² ÷ R
  • From V and I: R = V ÷ I, P = V × I. From V and R: I = V ÷ R, P = V² ÷ R. From V and P: I = P ÷ V, R = V² ÷ P
  • From I and R: V = I × R, P = I² × R. From I and P: V = P ÷ I, R = P ÷ I². From R and P: I = √(P ÷ R), V = √(P × R)
  • 1 hp (mechanical) = 745.69987 W; 1 BTU/h = 0.29307107 W; 1 kV = 1,000 V; 1 mA = 0.001 A; 1 kΩ = 1,000 Ω

A worked example

A 1,000 W iron on 230 V mains: the current is I = P ÷ V = 1,000 ÷ 230 = 4.35 A, and the hot element's resistance is R = V² ÷ P = 230² ÷ 1,000 = 52.9 Ω.

Questions

What is Ohm's law in simple words?

The current through a wire or resistor grows in step with the voltage across it: double the push and you double the flow. The formula is V = I × R.

How do I calculate resistance with Ohm's law?

Divide the voltage by the current: R = V ÷ I. 10 V driving 20 mA (0.02 A) means 500 Ω. If you know the power instead, R = V² ÷ P or R = P ÷ I².

How do I calculate current with Ohm's law?

Divide the voltage by the resistance: I = V ÷ R. 9 V across 320 Ω gives 0.028125 A. From power and voltage, I = P ÷ V.

What is the Ohm's law formula for power?

P = V × I, which can also be written P = I² × R or P = V² ÷ R. A 5 V, 2 A charger delivers 10 W.

How do I use Ohm's law in a parallel circuit?

Each branch has the full voltage, so find each branch current with I = V ÷ R and add them, or combine the resistances with 1 ÷ R = 1 ÷ R₁ + 1 ÷ R₂ first. 100 Ω and 220 Ω in parallel make 68.75 Ω.

How do I use Ohm's law in a series circuit?

Add the resistances to get the total, find the current with I = V ÷ R, then the voltage across each part with V = I × R. The same current flows through every part.

Why must I leave two boxes empty?

Two values fix the circuit completely, so the other two can only have one answer. If three or four boxes are filled, they might disagree with each other, so the calculator asks you to clear the ones you want worked out.

Does Ohm's law work for every device?

It works exactly for resistors and wires at a steady temperature. A filament lamp, a heater element or a motor changes its resistance as it warms up or turns, and a diode or LED does not follow it at all, so use the values measured while the device is running.

Can I use it for AC mains circuits?

For heaters, kettles and other resistive loads, yes: use the RMS voltage (for example 230 V or 120 V). Motors and electronics have a power factor below 1, so watts are less than volts times amps for them.

Which horsepower is used?

Mechanical horsepower, 745.7 watts. The result also shows the power in horsepower whenever power is worked out, so you can compare it with a motor rating.
