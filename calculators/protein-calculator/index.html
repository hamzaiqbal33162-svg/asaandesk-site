How much protein do you need a day?

Most healthy adults need roughly 0.8 to 1 g of protein for every kilogram they weigh, and more if they exercise. This protein calculator works out your daily protein in grams three ways: from your body weight and activity, as 10 to 35% of your daily calories, and as the World Health Organization's safe minimum of 0.83 g per kg.

Protein supplies the amino acids your body uses to build and repair muscle, skin, organs, enzymes and hormones. Unlike fat, the body does not store spare protein for later, so a steady daily intake matters. The US Institute of Medicine sets the adult Recommended Dietary Allowance at 0.8 g per kg, about 46 g a day for an average woman and 56 g for an average man.

The results are estimates for healthy adults aged 18 and over, not medical advice. Pregnancy, breastfeeding, illness and kidney disease change protein needs, and the page notes these cases.

How to use the protein calculator

  • Choose Metric (kg, cm) or US units (lb, feet and inches), then enter sex, age, weight and height.
  • Pick how active you are. Sedentary gives the 0.8 to 1.0 g per kg range; any level with regular exercise, from Light (1 to 3 times a week) up to Extra active, gives 1.0 to 1.8 g per kg.
  • Under Formula you can change the BMR formula used for the calorie-based range, including Katch-McArdle with your body fat percentage.
  • Read the headline range for your body weight, the 10 to 35% of calories range, the WHO safe minimum and the calories you need to keep your weight.

Understanding the three protein numbers

The three answers do different jobs, so a good daily target usually sits inside the first two and above the third.

Worked examples

  • Karachi, little exercise: a 28-year-old woman, 158 cm and 58 kg, gets 46 to 58 g a day for her body weight, 38 to 133 g as a share of her 1,520 kcal, and a WHO minimum of 48 g. About 55 g a day meets all three.
  • US units, active man: at 35, 6 ft and 185 lb with daily exercise, the body weight range is 84 to 151 g (0.45 to 0.82 g per lb). His 2,809 kcal allow 70 to 246 g, and his WHO minimum is 70 g.
  • Weight loss: a 45-year-old woman, 165 cm and 80 kg, who exercises 1 to 3 times a week gets 80 to 144 g for her weight and a WHO minimum of 66 g, with 1,987 kcal to keep her weight.
  • Muscle gain: a very active 22-year-old man, 175 cm and 68 kg, gets 68 to 122 g a day. The research figure of 1.62 g per kg described below would be about 110 g for him.
  • Older adult: a sedentary man of 65, 170 cm and 72 kg, gets 58 to 72 g a day, with a WHO minimum of 60 g.

Protein for muscle gain and weight loss

For building muscle, a 2018 meta-analysis of 49 trials with 1,863 people (Morton and colleagues, British Journal of Sports Medicine) found that extra protein stopped adding to muscle gains from strength training at a total intake of about 1.62 g per kg a day, though the true figure could lie anywhere from about 1.0 to 2.2 g per kg. The International Society of Sports Nutrition (2017) advises 1.4 to 2.0 g per kg a day for most people who exercise, in doses of about 20 to 40 g every 3 to 4 hours.

For weight loss, many people aim for the upper part of their range while eating fewer calories; the ISSN notes that higher intakes may help people who train while cutting calories to keep muscle. Because the calorie-share range shrinks when you eat less, check that your target still sits inside 10 to 35% of your new calorie figure; the Calorie Calculator gives that number.

Training matters as much as protein: it is strength work that drives muscle gain, and protein supports it.

Protein in common foods, including Pakistani meals

The Pakistan Dietary Guidelines for Better Nutrition (2018) suggest about 1 g of protein per kg of body weight a day for adults and give average protein per serving of each food group. The USDA FoodData Central database gives values for single foods.

Special cases: pregnancy, older age and kidney disease

  • Pregnancy: about 25 g a day more is advised in the second half of pregnancy, and breastfeeding mothers need extra too. Your midwife or doctor can give a figure for you.
  • Older adults: muscle is lost more easily with age, so meeting at least the upper end of the sedentary range and staying active is sensible; ask a doctor if you have a health condition.
  • Kidney disease: people with reduced kidney function are often told to eat less protein, so follow your doctor's or dietitian's advice instead of this page.
  • Very high intakes: the Institute of Medicine's upper end for protein is 35% of calories. Going far above your range adds cost and can push fibre-rich foods off the plate without adding benefit.

Tips for meeting your protein target

  • Spread protein over the day: 20 to 40 g at each main meal is used better than one very large serving.
  • Mix sources: meat, fish, eggs and dairy give complete protein, and pulses with cereals (daal with roti or rice) complement each other.
  • Read labels: protein is listed in grams per serving and per 100 g, so you can add it up quickly.
  • Use supplements only to fill a gap; food first is cheaper and brings other nutrients.
  • Recalculate when your weight or training changes, since both move your range.

How it works

Protein needs are usually given per kilogram of body weight. People who do little exercise need about 0.8 to 1.0 g per kg a day; people who exercise regularly, and especially those building strength, do well on 1.0 to 1.8 g per kg, as sports nutrition guidelines advise. A second way is to take 10 to 35% of your daily calories from protein: the calculator estimates your calories (BMR from the Mifflin-St Jeor formula times your activity factor) and divides by 4 kcal per gram. The World Health Organization's safe level, enough for nearly all healthy adults, is 0.83 g per kg.

The formula

  • Body weight guide: 0.8 to 1.0 g × weight (kg) if sedentary; 1.0 to 1.8 g × weight (kg) with regular exercise
  • Share of calories: calories × 10% ÷ 4 to calories × 35% ÷ 4 (4 kcal per gram of protein)
  • WHO safe minimum: 0.83 g × weight (kg)
  • Calories: BMR × activity factor, rounded to whole kcal

A worked example

A 28-year-old woman, 158 cm and 58 kg, who does little exercise: 0.8 × 58 ≈ 46 g to 1.0 × 58 = 58 g a day. Her calories are about 1,520 a day, so 10 to 35% of them is 1,520 × 0.10 ÷ 4 = 38 g to 1,520 × 0.35 ÷ 4 = 133 g. The WHO safe minimum is 0.83 × 58 ≈ 48 g.

Questions

How do I calculate my protein intake?

Multiply your weight in kg by a figure for your activity: 0.8 to 1.0 g if you rarely exercise, 1.0 to 1.8 g if you train regularly. A 58 kg woman who does little exercise needs about 46 to 58 g a day. The calculator also checks the range against your calories.

How much protein do I need according to my weight?

At least 0.83 g per kg, the WHO safe level, and 0.8 g per kg by the US Recommended Dietary Allowance. With regular exercise, 1.0 to 1.8 g per kg is a sensible range, or about 0.45 to 0.82 g per lb.

How much protein do I need to build muscle?

A 2018 meta-analysis found that gains from strength training levelled off at about 1.6 g per kg of body weight a day, and sports nutrition groups suggest 1.4 to 2.0 g per kg. Spreading it over 3 or 4 meals with 20 to 40 g each helps.

How much protein should I eat to lose weight?

Use the range for your activity, aiming towards the upper part while you eat fewer calories, especially if you also strength train. An 80 kg woman who exercises gets 80 to 144 g a day here.

Why are there three different answers?

They answer slightly different questions. The body weight guide is for your activity level, the calorie share shows the healthy range for your diet as a whole, and the WHO figure is the minimum that keeps nearly everyone healthy. A good target sits inside the first two and above the third.

How much protein is in common foods?

By the Pakistan Dietary Guidelines, one egg, 28 g of lean meat or fish, or half a cup of cooked pulses gives about 7 g, and one chapati about 6 g. USDA data put 100 g of boiled lentils at 9 g of protein.

Can I eat too much protein?

The Institute of Medicine's healthy range tops out at 35% of calories, and eating far more than your range brings no extra benefit. People with kidney disease are often told to eat less, so they should follow their doctor's advice.

Do I need more protein when pregnant?

Yes. In the second half of pregnancy about 25 g a day more is advised, and breastfeeding mothers also need extra. Ask your doctor or midwife for advice that fits you.

Is the protein calculator for men and women the same?

The per-kg ranges are the same for both, so the main difference is body weight. Sex changes only the calorie estimate, and so the 10 to 35% of calories range.
