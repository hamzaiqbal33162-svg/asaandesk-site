What does a period calculator do?

A period calculator predicts when your next period should start, from the date your last one began and the usual length of your cycle. This one also shows how many days your period is likely to last, which day of your cycle you are on today, your next likely ovulation days, a table of the next 6 cycles and a calendar you can page through for the coming months.

It works on a simple idea: a menstrual cycle starts on day 1 of bleeding and ends the day before the next period begins, so each period is expected one cycle length after the last. The NHS says a cycle is typically around 28 days, but anywhere from 21 to 35 days is normal, and a period lasts 2 to 7 days, usually about 5.

Real cycles move by a few days from month to month, so treat the dates as a guide for planning, not a promise. The calculator is not a method of contraception and does not give medical advice.

How to use the period calculator

  • First day of your last period: the first day of proper bleeding, not spotting before it. It starts on today's date until you change it.
  • Period length: how many days your bleeding usually lasts, from 1 to 10 days.
  • Usual cycle length: from 22 to 44 days. If you are not sure, count the days between the start dates of your last few periods and take the average.
  • Read your next period date and its expected days, the next likely ovulation days, today's cycle day and the period after that. The table lists six cycles, and the calendar marks period days and ovulation days three months at a time, with Earlier and Later buttons.

Understanding your results

  • Your next period: the first expected start date after today, with Lasting about N days from your period length.
  • Expected period days: the start date plus the following days of bleeding, for example Dec 10 to Dec 15 for a 6-day period.
  • Next likely ovulation days: five days centred on 14 days before the next period. Ovulation is when an egg is released, and the days around it are when pregnancy is most likely.
  • Cycle day today: how far into the current cycle you are, counting the first day of bleeding as day 1.
  • Earlier months on the calendar show where periods would have fallen at the same cycle length; comparing them with dates you remember is a quick check that the cycle length you entered is right.

Irregular periods, first periods and late periods

  • First periods: cycles are often irregular at first. The Office on Women's Health says cycles longer than 38 days are common for a few years after the first period and usually become more regular within three years, so predictions for young teens are rough.
  • Irregular cycles: enter your average cycle length and expect the real date to fall a few days either side. Tracking for three to six months gives a better average.
  • Late period: if you could be pregnant, the NHS says home tests are most reliable from the first day of a missed period. The Conception Calculator shows when a test is worth doing.
  • See a doctor if your cycles are often shorter than 24 or longer than 38 days, if you go 90 days without a period and are not pregnant or breastfeeding, if bleeding soaks a pad or tampon every one to two hours, or if you bleed between periods or after sex.

Periods, ovulation and pregnancy

The ovulation days on this page are a calendar estimate: about 14 days before the next period, with two days either side. The NHS notes that ovulation usually happens 10 to 16 days before a period, which is why the days are shown as a range.

Because ovulation moves, counting days is not a reliable way to avoid pregnancy, and the calculator should not be used as contraception. If you are trying for a baby, the Ovulation Calculator and the Conception Calculator focus on the fertile days in more detail.

Tips for tracking your period

  • Write down the first day of every period, how long it lasted and how heavy it was. A notes app or a paper calendar works as well as a dedicated app.
  • Note pain, mood changes and spotting too; patterns help your doctor if anything changes.
  • Update the cycle length here after a few months of records so the predictions stay accurate.
  • Stress, illness, travel, weight change and some medicines can shift a period, so one odd month is not usually a worry.

How it works

Enter the first day of your last period, how many days your period usually lasts and how long your cycle usually is. Each new period is expected one cycle length after the one before, so the calculator counts forward in steps of your cycle to find the next period after today and the ones after it. Ovulation usually happens about 14 days before a period starts, so the five days around that point are marked as likely ovulation days. The calendar shows three months at a time; use Earlier and Later to page through.

The formula

  • Period k starts on: first day of last period + k × cycle length
  • Period days: that start date and the following (period length − 1) days
  • Likely ovulation days: start + (cycle length − 16) to start + (cycle length − 12) days
  • Next period = the first expected start after today; cycle day today = days since the last expected start + 1

A worked example

A period that started on November 10, 2026, lasts 6 days and comes every 30 days. The next periods start on December 10, January 9 and February 8. Ovulation days for the first cycle are November 10 + 14 to November 10 + 18, that is November 24 to 28. On November 22 you are on day 13 of the cycle.

Questions

How do I calculate my next period date?

Add your usual cycle length to the first day of your last period. With a 30-day cycle, a period that started on November 10 means the next one is due on December 10. The calculator does this for the next six cycles and shows them on a calendar.

How do I calculate my period cycle length?

Count the days between the start dates of two periods in a row, not from the end of bleeding. Do this for a few months and use the average. If your periods started on October 12 and November 7, that cycle was 26 days.

What is a normal cycle length?

A cycle is measured between the start dates of two periods in a row. Anything from about 21 to 35 days is common in adults, and periods usually last 2 to 7 days. A cycle that changes by a few days from month to month is normal.

Can I use a period calculator for irregular periods?

Yes, but the dates will be rough. Enter your average cycle length and expect your period a few days either side. If your cycles are very irregular, often shorter than 24 or longer than 38 days, see a doctor.

Does it work for a first period?

It can only predict from a period that has already happened, and cycles are often irregular for the first few years. Periods usually start around age 12, anywhere from 8 to 15, so for a young teen the predictions are a rough guide.

How long does a period usually last?

Between 2 and 7 days, usually about 5, according to the NHS. Bleeding that often lasts longer than 8 days, or needs a new pad or tampon every one to two hours, counts as heavy and is worth checking with a doctor.

My period is late. What should I do?

A period a few days late is common, and stress, illness, travel or weight changes can shift it. If you could be pregnant, take a home pregnancy test. If periods are often very irregular, stop for three months or more, or come with heavy bleeding or pain, see a doctor.

When should I take a pregnancy test?

Home tests are most reliable from the first day of a missed period. Your expected period date is shown at the top of the results, so a late period is easy to spot.

Can I use this to avoid pregnancy?

No. Ovulation can come earlier or later than the calendar suggests, so counting days is not reliable contraception. Talk to a doctor or a family planning service about a method that suits you.

Why do the earlier months show periods too?

They show where your periods would have fallen if your cycle stayed the same length. Comparing them with the dates you remember is a quick way to check that the cycle length you entered is right.
