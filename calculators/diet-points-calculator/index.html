What a diet points calculator does

A diet points calculator turns the numbers on a nutrition label into one simple score. Instead of adding up calories, sugar and fat separately, a points diet gives every food a whole number of points, and you eat within a daily points budget. Foods that are dense in calories, sugar or saturated fat cost more points; protein and fiber bring the score down, depending on the formula.

This food points calculator offers the three formulas that were published for earlier points programs: 2015, 2010 and 1997. It shows the points per serving, the exact value before rounding and every step of the sum. It is free, needs no account and is not linked to any diet company.

How to use this calculator, step by step

  • Pick a tab: 2015 formula, 2010 formula or 1997 formula. Use the one that matches the points list or budget you follow.
  • Find the nutrition label and decide on one serving, the amount you will really eat.
  • 2015 formula: enter Calories, Sugar, Saturated fat and Protein for that serving.
  • 2010 formula: enter Protein, Carbohydrate, Total fat and Fiber.
  • 1997 formula: enter Calories, Total fat and Fiber. Fiber counts up to 4 g.
  • Energy can be typed in kcal, kJ or J, and every nutrient in g, kg, oz or lb; the calculator converts them.
  • Read the points per serving at the top and the worked sum underneath.

Understanding your results

The calculator always shows three things:

  • Points per serving: the formula's total rounded to the nearest whole point (halves round up), never below 0.
  • Before rounding: the exact value, useful when you add several foods together and want to round only once.
  • Steps: each nutrient's amount times its factor, the total, and an energy conversion line when you typed kJ or J.

With the default label (200 kcal, 10 g sugar, 3 g saturated fat and 6 g protein) the 2015 formula gives 6.1 + 0.825 + 1.2 − 0.588 = 7.537, so 8 points. A note appears if the formula goes below zero, because a food cannot add points back to your budget, and if fiber above 4 g was left out of the 1997 sum.

The three points formulas compared

Each formula rewards and penalises different nutrients. Dividing calories by the points per calorie shows roughly how many calories one point stands for.

The 1997 formula was patented in the United States as a 'process for controlling body weight': calories ÷ 50, plus about one point per 12 g of fat, minus about one per 5 g of fiber. The 2010 version dropped calories and scored the macronutrients directly, which made fat the most expensive (45 per 175) and fiber a discount. The 2015 version added sugar and saturated fat and let protein lower the score, steering people towards lean protein. Since late 2021 the leading program has used personalised formulas that are not public, so no calculator can reproduce them exactly.

Worked examples

One food, three formulas. The default label also fits the 2010 and 1997 tabs: 6 g protein, 25 g carbohydrate, 8 g fat and 2 g fiber in 200 kcal. The 2010 formula gives (96 + 475 + 360 − 28) ÷ 175 = 5.16, so 5 points; the 1997 formula gives 4 + 0.667 − 0.4 = 4.27, so 4 points; the 2015 formula gives 8. Point values are only comparable within one formula.

A label per 100 g. A packet of biscuits bought in Karachi lists, per 100 g, 480 kcal, 30 g sugar, 11 g saturated fat and 6 g protein, and you eat 40 g. Multiply each number by 0.4 to get 192 kcal, 12 g sugar, 4.4 g saturated fat and 2.4 g protein. The 2015 formula gives 5.856 + 1.21 + 1.44 − 0.235 = 8.27, so 8 points for those few biscuits.

Energy in kilojoules. A label that shows 840 kJ per serving is 840 ÷ 4.1868 = 200.6 kcal. Choose kJ next to Calories and type 840; the calculator adds this conversion as the first step.

Does a points diet work?

Points are a shortcut for eating fewer calories while nudging you towards more filling, less sugary food. Weight loss still comes from the calorie gap: points only make it easier to see.

The tracking itself seems to help. A systematic review by Burke and colleagues (2011) found a consistent link between regularly recording what you eat and losing weight, though the studies were of mixed quality. In a 2015 review of commercial programs in Annals of Internal Medicine, the best-studied points-based program led to about 2.6% more weight loss after 12 months than control groups.

Points have limits. They ignore vitamins, minerals and salt, they can make some healthy foods such as nuts and oils look expensive, and a low score does not make a food nutritious. People with diabetes, kidney disease or a history of eating disorders should plan any diet with a doctor or dietitian.

Tips and common mistakes

  • Count the portion you actually eat, not the label's suggested serving if yours is bigger.
  • Check whether the label is per 100 g or per serving before you type the numbers.
  • Use total sugar for the 2015 formula and total fat for the 2010 and 1997 formulas, not just saturated fat.
  • Stay with one formula. Mixing points from different formulas in one day gives a meaningless total.
  • For a home-cooked dish, add up the nutrients of all ingredients, enter the totals, then divide the points by the number of servings.
  • Your daily budget comes from the program you follow. To relate it to calories, find your needs in the Calorie Calculator and divide by the calories per point in the table above.

How it works

Points-based diets give each food a whole number of points from its nutrition label, so you can track a daily budget instead of counting every calorie. Pick a formula, copy the numbers for one serving from the label, and the calculator adds up each nutrient's share and rounds to the nearest whole point. The 2015 formula uses calories, sugar, saturated fat and protein, so sugary and fatty foods cost more and protein-rich foods cost less. The 2010 formula uses protein, carbohydrate, fat and fiber. The original 1997 formula uses calories, fat and fiber (up to 4 g). Amounts can be typed in grams, kilograms, ounces or pounds, and energy in kcal, kJ or J.

The formula

  • 2015 formula: points = calories × 0.0305 + saturated fat (g) × 0.275 + sugar (g) × 0.12 − protein (g) × 0.098
  • 2010 formula: points = (16 × protein + 19 × carbohydrate + 45 × fat − 14 × fiber) ÷ 175, all in grams
  • 1997 formula: points = calories ÷ 50 + fat (g) ÷ 12 − fiber (g, at most 4) ÷ 5
  • The total is rounded to the nearest whole point and never goes below 0
  • Kilojoules to calories: kcal = kJ ÷ 4.1868

A worked example

A snack bar with 250 kcal, 10 g sugar, 5 g saturated fat and 8 g protein, with the 2015 formula: 250 × 0.0305 = 7.625, 5 × 0.275 = 1.375, 10 × 0.12 = 1.2, and 8 × 0.098 = 0.784 is taken off. 7.625 + 1.375 + 1.2 − 0.784 = 9.416, which rounds to 9 points.

Questions

How do I calculate food points?

Take the numbers for one serving from the nutrition label and put them into a points formula. With the 2015 formula that is calories × 0.0305 + saturated fat × 0.275 + sugar × 0.12 − protein × 0.098, rounded to a whole number. This calculator does the sum and shows each step.

What is the points diet?

It is a way of dieting where each food has a points value based on its nutrients and you stay within a daily points budget. The idea is to make calorie control simpler and to push choices towards foods with less sugar and saturated fat and more protein or fiber.

Are these the points used by diet programs today?

No. These are the formulas that were published for earlier points programs. Current commercial programs use newer formulas that are not public, and some foods count as zero points there, so their numbers can differ. This page is not linked to any diet company.

Which formula should I use?

Use the one that matches the points list or daily budget you follow. If you are starting fresh, the 2015 formula reflects newer advice best, because it charges more for sugar and saturated fat and less for protein.

How many calories is one point?

It depends on the formula and the food. As a rough guide one point is about 50 kcal in the 1997 formula, 35 to 44 kcal in the 2010 formula and about 33 kcal in the 2015 formula, before fat, sugar, protein or fiber adjust it.

Why can a food come out at 0 points?

In the 2015 and 2010 formulas, protein or fiber lowers the total. A very lean, high-protein or high-fiber food can come out at or below zero. A food never takes points away from your budget, so anything below zero is shown as 0.

Why does fiber stop counting after 4 g in the 1997 formula?

That formula only lets fiber lower the points by up to 0.8 (4 g ÷ 5), so adding lots of fiber cannot turn a rich food into a free one. Fiber above 4 g is ignored.

Should I enter the numbers per 100 g or per serving?

Per serving, the amount you actually eat. If the label lists only per 100 g, multiply each number by the grams you eat and divide by 100 before you enter them.

Can I use kilojoules from the label?

Yes. Choose kJ (or J) next to Calories and type the number from the label. The calculator divides kilojoules by 4.1868 to get kcal and shows that step.
