What a BTU calculator works out

A BTU calculator estimates how much heat an air conditioner or heater has to move to keep a room comfortable. The answer is given in BTU per hour (BTU/h or BTU/hr), the rating printed on most air conditioners, and also in watts, kilowatts and tons, so you can compare it with any spec sheet.

BTU stands for British thermal unit. The U.S. Energy Information Administration defines one BTU as the heat needed to raise one pound of water by 1 °F, and NIST puts it at about 1,055.06 joules. An appliance rated in BTU/h tells you how many BTU it can add or remove every hour, so it is a measure of power, not of energy used.

How to use the BTU calculator for a room

The first tab, "Air conditioner size", is the one most people need. The US / Metric switch chooses feet or metres, and each box also has its own unit menu.

  • Room floor area: the floor of the room in square feet or square metres (length × width).
  • Ceiling height: the rule is set for an 8 ft (2.44 m) ceiling. A taller ceiling raises the result; a lower one is counted as 8 ft.
  • Room: Bedroom, Living room, Kitchen, Whole house, Whole first (ground) floor or Whole second (upper) floor. A kitchen adds 4,000 BTU/h for the cooking heat, a living room 1,000, and an upper floor 10%.
  • Climate: Average, Hot (long, hot summers) or Cold (mild summers, long winters).
  • Under "More about the room": People usually in the room, Insulation (Average, Good, Poor) and Sun on the room (Average, Heavily shaded, Very sunny).

The second tab, "Heating or cooling load", is for heaters, garages and workshops. Enter the Width, Length and Ceiling height, the Insulation, and the "Temperature change wanted" in °F or °C: the indoor temperature you want minus the outdoor temperature in winter, or the other way round in summer.

Understanding the BTU result: watts, kW and tons

Capacity needed is the estimate in BTU/h. In watts divides it by 3.412, because one watt is 3.412 BTU/h, and Kilowatts is the same figure divided by 1,000. In tons divides by 12,000, the size of one ton of cooling. On the first tab you also get the smallest common unit that is big enough, picked from the usual sizes between 5,000 and 60,000 BTU/h; above that the page suggests several units or a central system.

Worked examples: a living room and a bedroom in Pakistan

Living room in feet: 300 ft², a 9 ft ceiling, very sunny, average insulation and climate, 3 people. The floor part is 20 × 300 × (9 ÷ 8) = 6,750 BTU/h; with the 2,500 base it is 9,250. Very sunny adds 10% to give 10,175, one extra person adds 600 and the living room adds 1,000, so the room needs 11,775 BTU/h (3.45 kW). The smallest common unit that covers it is 12,000 BTU/h, a 1 ton air conditioner.

Bedroom in Lahore, in metres: 18 m² of floor with a 3 m ceiling on a sunny top floor with poor insulation, a hot climate and 2 people. The calculator converts the room to 193.75 ft² and a 9.84 ft ceiling, so the floor part is 4,767 BTU/h and 7,267 with the base. Poor insulation (× 1.2), strong sun (× 1.1) and the hot climate (× 1.2) bring it to 11,512 BTU/h, about 3.37 kW, so again a 1 ton (12,000 BTU/h) split unit is the smallest size that is big enough.

In hot places the climate setting matters a lot. A 500 ft² room on Hot and Very sunny needs 16,500 BTU/h, which points to an 18,000 BTU/h (1.5 ton) unit, while the same room on average settings needs 12,500.

Heating and cooling load: garages, shops and radiators

The "Heating or cooling load" tab estimates the heat that leaks through the walls and ceiling: the wall area (2 × (width + length) × height) plus the ceiling area, times the temperature change in °F, times a factor for the insulation (0.24 good, 0.4 average, 0.84 poor). A change typed in °C is multiplied by 1.8 first; no 32 is added, because it is a difference, not a temperature.

Garage heater example: a 20 × 22 ft garage with a 9 ft ceiling and poor insulation, to be kept 30 °F warmer than outside. The walls are 756 ft² and the ceiling 440 ft², so the load is 1,196 × 30 × 0.84 = 30,139 BTU/h, about 8.83 kW. Insulating the walls and door would cut that figure a lot.

Radiator example in metric: a 4 × 3.5 m room with a 2.4 m ceiling and good insulation, heated 20 °C above the outdoor temperature. The calculator gives 4,650 BTU/h, or 1,363 W, which is the combined output the radiators in that room should reach. Radiator makers quote output in watts or BTU/h, so the two figures can be compared directly.

This is a quick wall-and-ceiling estimate. Windows, air leaks and the floor are not counted separately, so for a whole house, a furnace or a central system use a full load calculation such as ACCA Manual J.

What changes the BTU a room needs

  • Sun and windows: a west-facing room with large windows can need far more cooling in the afternoon than a shaded room of the same size.
  • Insulation and roof: a top-floor room under a flat concrete roof, or a room with thin walls, gains heat quickly; good insulation and a light-coloured roof lower the load.
  • Ceiling height: more height means more air to cool, which is why the floor part is scaled up for ceilings above 8 ft.
  • People and appliances: every person gives off heat, and cookers, ovens and computers add more, hence the extra for kitchens and for each person above two.
  • Climate: the Hot setting adds 20% and the Cold setting takes off 15%, because summer temperatures and humidity set how hard the unit must work.
  • Room shape and air leaks: a long narrow room has more outside wall than a square one of the same area, and gaps around doors and windows let hot air in.

BTU in fuels, electricity and water

BTU is also used to compare fuels. The EIA gives these approximate heat contents: 1 kilowatt-hour of electricity is about 3,412 BTU, 1 cubic foot of natural gas about 1,036 BTU, a therm exactly 100,000 BTU, and a gallon of motor gasoline about 120,214 BTU. The EIA recalculates such factors every year, so treat them as rounded figures.

For heating water the definition gives the formula directly: BTU = pounds of water × temperature rise in °F. A water heater's BTU/h rating then tells you how quickly it can deliver that heat.

Tips and common mistakes when sizing an air conditioner

  • Do not buy far too big. An oversized unit cools the air in short bursts and switches off before it has removed the moisture, so the room feels cold and damp and the compressor wears faster.
  • Do not buy too small either: the unit will run without stopping on the hottest days and still not reach the set temperature.
  • Measure the room, not the house plan. Use the actual floor area of the space the unit will cool, and choose one unit per room or zone for mini splits.
  • Check whether a figure is BTU/h or watts of power drawn. A 12,000 BTU/h air conditioner moves 3.5 kW of heat but uses much less electricity than that.
  • For a whole house, the Whole house and floor options give a first idea only; have a technician run a Manual J calculation before buying central air or a furnace.

How it works

For an air conditioner, give the room's floor area, ceiling height, what the room is and your climate; under More about the room you can add the number of people, the insulation and how sunny the room is. The capacity appears as you type in BTU per hour, watts, kilowatts and tons, with the smallest common unit size that is big enough. For heating or cooling, the second tab estimates the heat that flows through the walls and ceiling for the temperature change you want. Rooms typed in square meters and meters are converted exactly, so the same room gives the same answer in either unit.

The formula

  • Room: 20 BTU/h per ft² of floor × (ceiling height ÷ 8 ft, when taller than 8 ft) + 2,500
  • × insulation (good 0.8, poor 1.2) × sun (heavily shaded 0.9, very sunny 1.1) × climate (cold 0.85, hot 1.2) × 1.1 for a whole upper floor
  • + 600 for each person above two, + 4,000 for a kitchen, + 1,000 for a living room
  • Load: (walls 2 × (width + length) × height + ceiling width × length) ft² × temperature change °F × 0.24 good, 0.4 average or 0.84 poor insulation
  • 1 W = 3.412141633 BTU/h; 1 ton of cooling = 12,000 BTU/h; a change of 1 °C = 1.8 °F

A worked example

A 400 ft² kitchen with an 8 ft ceiling, poor insulation, lots of sun, a hot climate and 4 people: (20 × 400 + 2,500) × 1.2 × 1.1 × 1.2 = 16,632, plus 2 × 600 for the extra people and 4,000 for the kitchen = 21,832 BTU/h, about 6,398 W or 1.8 tons, so a 24,000 BTU/h (2 ton) unit.

Questions

How do I calculate BTU for a room?

Multiply the floor area in square feet by 20 and add 2,500, then adjust: more for sun, a hot climate, poor insulation, a tall ceiling, extra people or a kitchen, and less for shade or a cold climate. A 150 ft² bedroom on average settings comes to 5,500 BTU/h. The calculator does every step and shows them.

How many BTU do I need per square foot?

For a room with an 8 ft ceiling, about 20 BTU per hour for each square foot, plus a base amount, so a 150 ft² bedroom needs about 5,500 BTU/h and a 300 ft² room about 8,500. Sunny rooms, kitchens, hot climates and more people push the figure up.

How do I calculate BTU for a room in meters?

Switch to Metric and type the floor area in square metres and the ceiling height in metres. The calculator converts them exactly to square feet and feet, so a 20 m² bedroom with a 2.5 m ceiling gives 6,914 BTU/h, the same as typing the room in feet.

What does BTU mean in an AC?

It is the cooling capacity: how many British thermal units of heat the air conditioner can remove from the room each hour. A 12,000 BTU unit removes 12,000 BTU per hour, which is 3.5 kW or 1 ton of cooling. A higher number means a more powerful unit, not a more efficient one.

What does an AC ton mean?

One ton of cooling is 12,000 BTU per hour, the heat needed to melt a ton of ice in a day. A 1.5 ton split air conditioner moves about 18,000 BTU/h (5.3 kW). The calculator shows the tons next to the BTU figure.

What is BTU/hr?

BTU per hour, a rate of heat flow. It is a unit of power, like watts: 1 W is about 3.412 BTU/hr. Air conditioners, heaters and furnaces are rated in BTU/hr even when the label just says BTU.

What is the BTU formula for heating?

For a quick estimate, add the wall area and ceiling area in square feet, multiply by the temperature rise in °F, then by 0.24 for good insulation, 0.4 for average or 0.84 for poor. That is what the "Heating or cooling load" tab does, and it converts °C and metres for you.

What size heater do I need for a garage?

Use the load tab with the garage's width, length and ceiling height and how many degrees warmer you want it than outside. A poorly insulated 20 × 22 ft garage with a 9 ft ceiling, kept 30 °F above the outdoor temperature, needs about 30,139 BTU/h. Better insulation reduces that sharply.

Why does ceiling height matter?

The rule of thumb is set for an 8 ft (2.44 m) ceiling. A taller room holds more air to cool, so the floor part of the estimate is scaled by the ceiling height divided by 8 ft; for example a 10 ft ceiling adds a quarter. Lower ceilings are counted as 8 ft so the size is never cut too far.

Is a bigger air conditioner better?

Not much bigger. An oversized unit cools the air quickly and switches off before it has removed the moisture, so the room feels cold and damp and the unit wears faster. Choose the size shown or the next common size up.

Can I use this to size central air or a furnace?

Only as a first idea. Whole-house systems should be sized with a full load calculation (ACCA Manual J) that counts windows, air leaks, ducts and the local design temperatures.
