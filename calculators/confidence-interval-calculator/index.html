What a confidence interval is, in simple words

A confidence interval calculator turns a sample into a range of plausible values for the true average of the whole group. You measured some of the people, parts or days, not all of them, so your sample mean is only an estimate. The interval puts honest error bars around it: "the average is about 20.6, give or take 0.887".

The confidence level, usually 95%, describes the method rather than one answer. If you repeated the sampling many times and built an interval each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true mean. A single interval either contains it or misses it; you just do not know which, and the 95% tells you how often the method succeeds.

This page finds the interval for a population mean. It needs three numbers you already have from your data (the sample size, the mean and the standard deviation) and returns the margin of error, the lower and upper limits and the steps.

How to use the confidence interval calculator

  • Sample size (n): how many values you measured. It starts at 50.
  • Sample mean (x̄) and Standard deviation: type the average of your values and their spread. Use the population σ if it is known, otherwise the sample standard deviation s. The defaults are 20.6 and 3.2.
  • Confidence level: pick 90%, 95% or 99%, or choose Other and type any level between 0% and 100%, such as 80 or 99.9.
  • More options, Use the t distribution: tick it when the sample is small and the standard deviation was worked out from the same sample. The page then uses Student's t with n − 1 degrees of freedom, and needs at least 2 values.
  • Read the interval in the headline, the steps below it and the collapsed Other confidence levels table, which repeats the margin for 80%, 90%, 95%, 98%, 99%, 99.5% and 99.9%.

Confidence interval formula with worked examples

The formula is CI = x̄ ± z × σ ÷ √n. The steps are always the same: find the standard error, multiply it by the critical value, then add and subtract the result from the mean.

Example 1, the defaults: SE = 3.2 ÷ √50 = 0.452548. For 95% the z value is 1.9600, so the margin is 1.9600 × 0.452548 = 0.887. The interval runs from 20.6 − 0.887 = 19.713 to 20.6 + 0.887 = 21.487.

Example 2, test marks in Lahore: a teacher samples 36 students whose average mark is 62 with a standard deviation of 12. SE = 12 ÷ √36 = 2, the 95% margin is 1.9600 × 2 = 3.920, and the class average is likely between 58.080 and 65.920 (± 6.3% of the mean).

Example 3, shop bills in PKR: a Karachi shop checks 200 receipts with a mean of PKR 1,250 and a standard deviation of PKR 400. SE = 400 ÷ √200 = 28.2843. At 99% confidence z = 2.5758, so the margin is PKR 72.86 and the average bill lies between about PKR 1,177.14 and PKR 1,322.86.

Example 4, a small sample with t: 10 values, mean 5, standard deviation 1, 90% confidence. With z the margin is 1.6449 × 0.316228 = 0.520. With t (9 degrees of freedom) the critical value is 1.8331 and the margin grows to 0.580, giving 4.420 to 5.580.

Moving from 95% to 99% raises z from 1.96 to 2.58, so the interval becomes about 31% wider for the same data. There is no free lunch: more confidence always costs width unless you collect more values.

z or t: confidence interval with the t distribution

The z interval assumes you know the population standard deviation. In real studies you usually estimate it from the same sample, which adds uncertainty. Student's t distribution allows for that with heavier tails, so its critical values are larger, especially for tiny samples.

As the sample grows, t moves toward z. By about 30 values the difference is small, and by 100 it is under 2%. The table shows the 95% t values the calculator produces for several sample sizes.

Other kinds of intervals and doing it in Excel or R

For a proportion, such as 120 yes answers out of 400, the usual large-sample formula is p̂ ± z × √(p̂(1 − p̂) ÷ n). Here p̂ = 0.30, the standard error is √(0.30 × 0.70 ÷ 400) = 0.0229, and the 95% interval is 0.30 ± 0.045, or 25.5% to 34.5%. The NIST handbook notes that this simple form can give a negative lower limit for rare events and recommends the Wilson or exact binomial method there.

For the difference between two means, the standard error becomes √(s₁² ÷ n₁ + s₂² ÷ n₂), and the interval is (x̄₁ − x̄₂) ± critical value × that standard error. This page handles one mean at a time.

In Excel, CONFIDENCE.NORM(alpha, standard_dev, size) and CONFIDENCE.T(alpha, standard_dev, size) return the margin of error, not the interval; alpha is 1 minus the confidence level, so 0.05 for 95%. CONFIDENCE.T(0.05, 1, 50) gives 0.2842, which matches 2.0096 × 1 ÷ √50 from the t option here. In R, t.test(x)$conf.int returns a 95% t interval for the mean by default.

Common mistakes and tips

  • Do not read a 95% interval as a 95% chance for this one interval. The 95% belongs to the method, as explained above.
  • Do not use the interval to describe individual values. It is about the mean; single measurements spread far wider, roughly by the standard deviation itself.
  • Use the standard deviation, not the standard error, in the Standard deviation box. If a report gives only the standard error, multiply it by √n first.
  • Random sampling matters more than formulas. A biased sample gives a precise interval around the wrong value.
  • To halve the margin of error, collect four times as many values. The Sample Size Calculator finds the n needed for a target margin.
  • Related tools: Standard Deviation Calculator for s from raw data, Z-score Calculator and P-value Calculator for tests.

How it works

Type how many values are in your sample, their mean and their standard deviation, then pick how confident you want to be (95% is the usual choice). The page works out the standard error (the standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size), multiplies it by the z value for your confidence level to get the margin of error, and shows the interval as mean ± margin, as a share of the mean and as a low-to-high range, with an error-bar picture. For small samples whose standard deviation comes from the sample itself, switch on the t distribution under More options.

The formula

  • Standard error SE = σ ÷ √n
  • Margin of error = z × SE, where z = Φ⁻¹(1 − (1 − C) ÷ 2) for confidence level C (1.6449 for 90%, 1.9600 for 95%, 2.5758 for 99%)
  • Interval = x̄ − margin to x̄ + margin
  • With the t distribution: margin = t × SE, with t from Student's t with n − 1 degrees of freedom

A worked example

50 values with a mean of 20.6 and a standard deviation of 3.2: SE = 3.2 ÷ √50 = 0.45255. At 95% confidence z = 1.9600, so the margin is 1.9600 × 0.45255 = 0.887 and the interval is 20.6 ± 0.887, from 19.713 to 21.487 (± 4.3% of the mean).

Questions

What does a 95% confidence interval mean?

If you took many samples and built an interval from each in the same way, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true mean. It does not mean there is a 95% chance that this one interval holds it: it either does or it does not.

How do I calculate a confidence interval by hand?

Divide the standard deviation by the square root of the sample size to get the standard error. Multiply that by the z value for your level (1.96 for 95%) to get the margin of error. Subtract the margin from the mean for the lower limit and add it for the upper limit.

What is the 95% confidence interval formula?

For a mean it is x̄ ± 1.96 × σ ÷ √n. With 50 values, a mean of 20.6 and a standard deviation of 3.2 that gives 20.6 ± 0.887. For small samples swap 1.96 for the t value with n − 1 degrees of freedom.

How do I get a confidence interval from the standard error?

If you already have the standard error, skip the first step: the margin is the critical value times the standard error. A standard error of 2 at 95% gives a margin of 1.96 × 2 = 3.92. To use this page, type the standard deviation, which is the standard error times √n.

Should I use z or t?

Use z when you know the population standard deviation, or when the sample is large (30 or more values), where z and t are almost the same. Use t when the sample is small and its standard deviation was worked out from the sample. The t interval is a little wider to allow for that extra uncertainty.

What is the difference between a confidence interval and the margin of error?

The margin of error is the half-width, the part after the ± sign. The confidence interval is the whole range, the mean minus the margin to the mean plus the margin. A poll result of 52% ± 3% has a margin of 3 points and an interval of 49% to 55%.

Can I find a confidence interval without the standard deviation?

You need some measure of spread. If you have the raw data, work out the sample standard deviation first, for example with our standard deviation calculator, then use the t option. For a yes or no proportion the spread comes from the proportion itself, through √(p̂(1 − p̂) ÷ n).

How do I calculate a confidence interval in Excel?

Use =CONFIDENCE.NORM(0.05, sd, n) for a z margin or =CONFIDENCE.T(0.05, sd, n) for a t margin at 95%. Both return the margin only, so the interval is the mean minus that result to the mean plus it.

Why does a higher confidence level give a wider interval?

To be more certain of catching the true mean you need a wider net. Going from 95% to 99% raises z from 1.96 to 2.58, so the margin of error grows by about a third.

How can I make the interval narrower?

Measure more values. The margin of error shrinks with the square root of the sample size, so four times as many values halves it. Lowering the confidence level also narrows it, but makes the result less certain.

How is a confidence interval used in hypothesis testing?

A two-sided 95% interval contains exactly the values that a two-sided test at the 5% level would not reject. If a claimed mean of 18 falls outside 19.713 to 21.487, the data are significant evidence against it at the 5% level.
