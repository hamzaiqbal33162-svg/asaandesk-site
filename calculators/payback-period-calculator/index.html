What the payback period is

The payback period is how long an investment takes to earn back what it cost. A payback period calculator adds up the cash an investment brings in, year by year, and tells you the point at which the total equals the money you put in: the break-even point in time. A machine, a shop fit-out, solar panels or a new product line can all be judged this way.

It is one of the oldest tools in capital budgeting because it answers a practical question in plain words: how long is my money at risk? Many businesses set a maximum, such as three or five years, and reject projects that take longer. This calculator also gives the discounted payback period, which counts the time value of money, together with the net present value (NPV) and the internal rate of return (IRR), so you can judge the whole project rather than the payback alone.

How to use the payback period calculator

  • Cost of the investment: the money spent at the start (year 0), 100,000 by default.
  • Different amount each year: type the Cash flow in year 1, 2, 3 ... separated by commas, such as 20,000, 30,000, 35,000, 30,000, 25,000. Use a minus sign for a year that costs money, for example a major repair.
  • Same amount, growing or shrinking: enter the Cash flow in year 1, whether it then Grows or Shrinks, By what % a year (0 for the same amount every year) and the Number of years.
  • Discount rate: the yearly return you need, often your cost of borrowing or what the money could earn elsewhere. Enter 0 to skip the discounted payback.
  • Choose the currency at the top; it changes only the symbol.

Payback period formula

When the cash flow is the same every year, the formula is simply: payback period = initial investment ÷ yearly cash flow. A 50,000 investment that returns 12,500 a year pays back in 50,000 ÷ 12,500 = 4 years.

With uneven cash flows, add them up year by year until the total passes the cost, then work out the fraction of the last year: payback = years before the full recovery + amount still unrecovered at the start of that year ÷ cash flow in that year. With the defaults, 85,000 is back after 3 years and 15,000 is still owed; year 4 brings 30,000, so payback = 3 + 15,000 ÷ 30,000 = 3.5 years.

To give the answer in years and months, multiply the fraction by 12: 0.5 × 12 = 6 months, so 3 years 6 months. This assumes the cash comes in evenly through the year, which is the usual textbook assumption, including in A-level and IB business courses.

Discounted payback period

The plain payback treats 1 received in year 5 the same as 1 received today. The discounted payback period corrects that: each cash flow is divided by (1 + r)^year before it is added up, where r is your discount rate. The answer is always longer than the plain payback when the rate is above zero.

With the defaults at 10%, the year-1 cash flow of 20,000 is worth 18,181.82 today, year 2's 30,000 is worth 24,793.39, and so on. The discounted running total is still −10,238.37 after year 4 and reaches +5,284.66 after year 5, so the discounted payback is 4 + 10,238.37 ÷ 15,523.03 = 4.660 years. The final discounted total is the project's NPV.

For the same cash flow C every year there is a direct formula: discounted payback = −ln(1 − cost × r ÷ C) ÷ ln(1 + r). If cost × r is at least C, the discounted cash flows never cover the cost and there is no discounted payback.

Worked examples

Solar panels in Pakistan. A home system costs Rs 1,200,000 and saves Rs 300,000 of electricity bills a year. With flat savings, the payback is 1,200,000 ÷ 300,000 = 4 years; at a 12% discount rate the discounted payback is 5.770 years. If you expect the savings to grow 10% a year as tariffs rise, choose Grows by 10% for 10 years: the payback falls to 3.518 years, the discounted payback to 4.630 years, and the NPV over 10 years is Rs 1,273,265. Your own savings depend on your usage, tariff and any net-metering arrangement, so enter your actual bills.

A growing cash flow. The fixed mode's defaults, 100,000 cost and 30,000 in year 1 growing 5% a year for 5 years, pay back in 3.156 years, with a discounted payback of 3.921 years at 10%, an IRR of 18.86% and an NPV of 24,517.74.

Why payback is not enough. Project A costs 80,000 and returns 40,000 in each of the next two years and nothing after. Project B costs the same and returns 20,000, 25,000, 30,000, 35,000 and 40,000. A pays back in 2 years and B in 3.143, so A looks better on payback. But at 10%, A never earns back its cost in today's money, while B has a discounted payback of 3.779 years and an NPV of 30,124.74. B is clearly the better investment.

Strengths and limits of the payback method

  • Simple: easy to work out and to explain to people who are not finance specialists.
  • Shows risk and liquidity: a short payback means your money is exposed for less time, which matters for small firms with limited cash and for fast-changing technology.
  • Ignores what happens after payback: cash after the break-even year does not count, so a project with large later returns can look worse than a short-lived one.
  • Ignores the time value of money: the plain payback treats all years alike; the discounted payback fixes this.
  • No built-in target: the acceptable payback is a judgement, so pair it with NPV and IRR for the real decision.

Payback uses cash flows, not accounting profit. Depreciation is not a cash payment, so if you start from profit after tax, add the depreciation back to get each year's cash flow. Include any money spent later, such as replacing an inverter in a solar system, as a negative cash flow in that year.

How to calculate payback period in Excel

Put the years in column A (0, 1, 2 ...) and the cash flows in column B, with the cost as a negative number in year 0. In column C, keep a running total: =B2 in C2, then =C2+B3 in C3, copied down. Find the last year where the running total is still negative, and payback = that year + ABS(its running total) ÷ the next year's cash flow.

For the discounted payback, add a column with =B3/(1+rate)^A3 and keep a running total of that column instead. Excel's =NPV(rate, B3:B7)+B2 gives the net present value and =IRR(B2:B7) the internal rate of return, the same figures this page shows.

Tips and related tools

  • Use realistic, after-tax cash flows, including maintenance and running costs.
  • Set your maximum acceptable payback before you look at the numbers.
  • Run the discounted payback at your real cost of money; at 0% it equals the plain payback.
  • Compare projects on NPV when they differ in size or in how long they last.

How it works

The calculator keeps a running total that starts at minus the cost and adds each year's cash flow. The payback period is the point where that total reaches zero: the whole years before it, plus the part of the next year needed, counted as if that year's money came in evenly. The discounted payback does the same with each cash flow first divided by (1 + discount rate) to the power of its year, so money that comes later counts for less. When the yearly amount is the same every year, the discounted payback comes straight from the annuity formula.

The formula

  • Running total after year n = −cost + CF1 + CF2 + … + CFn
  • Payback = (n − 1) + amount still owed after year n − 1 ÷ CFn, for the first year n where the total reaches 0
  • Discounted cash flow in year k = CFk ÷ (1 + r)^k; discounted payback uses these in the same way
  • Same amount C every year: discounted payback = −ln(1 − cost × r ÷ C) ÷ ln(1 + r)
  • Growing or shrinking: CFk = CF1 × (1 ± g)^(k − 1)

A worked example

A $100,000 machine brings in $5,000, $25,000, $35,000, $40,000 and then $30,000. After three years $65,000 is back and $35,000 is still owed; year 4 brings $40,000, so the payback is 3 + 35,000 ÷ 40,000 = 3.875 years, about 3 years 11 months.

Questions

How do I calculate the payback period?

For equal yearly cash flows, divide the cost by the yearly cash flow. For uneven cash flows, add them up until the total passes the cost, then add the fraction of the last year: amount still owed ÷ that year's cash flow. The calculator does both and shows the running total.

How do I calculate payback period with uneven cash flows?

Keep a running total starting at minus the cost. Find the last year it is still negative, then add the remaining amount divided by the next year's cash flow. With 100,000 cost and 20,000, 30,000, 35,000 and 30,000, that is 3 + 15,000 ÷ 30,000 = 3.5 years.

How do I calculate the payback period in months?

Multiply the fraction of a year by 12. A payback of 3.5 years is 3 years and 6 months; 3.875 years is 3 years and about 10.5 months, which the calculator rounds to 3 years 11 months.

What is the discounted payback period formula?

Discount each cash flow by dividing it by (1 + r)^year, then find when the running total of those discounted values reaches the cost. With the same cash flow C every year, discounted payback = −ln(1 − cost × r ÷ C) ÷ ln(1 + r).

Plain or discounted payback: which should I use?

The plain payback is quick and easy to explain. The discounted payback is stricter because it counts the time value of money: a dollar in year 5 is worth less than a dollar today, so it always takes a little longer.

What discount rate should I enter?

Use the yearly return you need to make the investment worthwhile, often your cost of borrowing or what you could earn elsewhere. Enter 0 to see only the plain payback.

What is the payback period for solar panels?

Divide the system cost by the yearly saving on your electricity bills. A Rs 1,200,000 system saving Rs 300,000 a year pays back in 4 years, sooner if tariffs rise. Use the growing cash flow mode to include rising bills.

What if the cost is never earned back?

The result says so and shows the average yearly cash flow, with a rough estimate of the payback if that average carried on beyond the years you entered.

What is the cash flow return rate?

It is the internal rate of return of the cost and the cash flows you entered: the yearly rate at which they balance. If it is above your discount rate, the net present value is positive.

Does the payback period tell me whether an investment is good?

Only partly. It ignores everything after the payback year, so a project that pays back fast but stops early can look better than one that earns much more over time. Check the net present value as well.
