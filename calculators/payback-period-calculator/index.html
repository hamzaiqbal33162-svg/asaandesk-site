How it works

The calculator keeps a running total that starts at minus the cost and adds each year's cash flow. The payback period is the point where that total reaches zero: the whole years before it, plus the part of the next year needed, counted as if that year's money came in evenly. The discounted payback does the same with each cash flow first divided by (1 + discount rate) to the power of its year, so money that comes later counts for less. When the yearly amount is the same every year, the discounted payback comes straight from the annuity formula.

The formula

  • Running total after year n = −cost + CF1 + CF2 + … + CFn
  • Payback = (n − 1) + amount still owed after year n − 1 ÷ CFn, for the first year n where the total reaches 0
  • Discounted cash flow in year k = CFk ÷ (1 + r)^k; discounted payback uses these in the same way
  • Same amount C every year: discounted payback = −ln(1 − cost × r ÷ C) ÷ ln(1 + r)
  • Growing or shrinking: CFk = CF1 × (1 ± g)^(k − 1)

A worked example

A $100,000 machine brings in $5,000, $25,000, $35,000, $40,000 and then $30,000. After three years $65,000 is back and $35,000 is still owed; year 4 brings $40,000, so the payback is 3 + 35,000 ÷ 40,000 = 3.875 years, about 3 years 11 months.

Questions

Plain or discounted payback: which should I use?

The plain payback is quick and easy to explain. The discounted payback is stricter because it counts the time value of money: a dollar in year 5 is worth less than a dollar today, so it always takes a little longer.

What discount rate should I enter?

Use the yearly return you need to make the investment worthwhile, often your cost of borrowing or what you could earn elsewhere. Enter 0 to see only the plain payback.

What if the cost is never earned back?

The result says so and shows the average yearly cash flow, with a rough estimate of the payback if that average carried on beyond the years you entered.

What is the cash flow return rate?

It is the internal rate of return of the cost and the cash flows you entered: the yearly rate at which they balance. If it is above your discount rate, the net present value is positive.

Does the payback period tell me whether an investment is good?

Only partly. It ignores everything after the payback year, so a project that pays back fast but stops early can look better than one that earns much more over time. Check the net present value as well.
