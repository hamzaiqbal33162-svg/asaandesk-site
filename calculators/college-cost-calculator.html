What a college cost calculator tells you

A college cost calculator estimates what a degree will cost by the time your child starts, when prices have kept rising, and how much you need to save each month to pay for it. This one works out the cost of each year of college, the total for the whole degree, that total in today's money, and the monthly saving for the full cost and for the share you plan to cover from savings.

The result is a planning number, not a bill. What a family really pays depends on grants, scholarships and the aid formula, so the calculator lets you choose a share, such as 50%, to pay from savings and leave the rest to aid, loans or income at the time. The guide below explains the averages behind the menu, the difference between sticker price and net price, and the ways US families save and pay.

How to use the college cost calculator

  • Type of college: choose a 4-year private college, a 4-year public college (in-state or out-of-state), a 2-year public college, or My own yearly cost. The averages are College Board total budgets for 2025-26.
  • Yearly cost today (only with My own yearly cost): everything for one year at today's prices, including housing, food and books, not just tuition. A college's own cost of attendance is the best figure to use.
  • Years until college starts (0 if it starts now) and Years of study (4 for a bachelor's degree, 2 for an associate degree, more for a longer program).
  • Share you will pay from savings: the part of the cost you want savings to cover. The rest is expected from grants, scholarships, loans or income while your child is in college.
  • Saved so far: money already put aside for college.
  • Assumptions (optional): College costs rise by (5% a year to start), Return on your savings (5%) and Tax on that return (25%; use 0% for a tax-free plan such as a US 529 plan).

How much does college cost in 2025-26?

The College Board's Trends in College Pricing 2025 gives the average published prices for full-time undergraduates. Tuition and fees are only part of the bill: the total budget also includes housing and food, books and supplies, transportation and other personal costs, which are the figures in the calculator's menu.

Prices vary a lot by state and by school. The same report puts in-state tuition and fees at public four-year colleges between $6,360 in Florida and $18,090 in Vermont in 2025-26. Housing, food and the other costs vary less across types of college than tuition does, so a 2-year public college with tuition at about a third of a public university's still has a total budget of about 69% of it. For a single school, such as Harvard, Stanford, UCLA or your state university, use its published cost of attendance with My own yearly cost.

Sticker price, net price and the Student Aid Index

The published price is the sticker price. The net price is what a family pays after grants and scholarships, and it is often far lower. The College Board estimates that first-time, full-time in-state students at public four-year colleges paid about $2,300 of net tuition and fees on average in 2025-26, and students at private nonprofit four-year colleges about $16,910, against sticker prices of $11,950 and $45,000.

To see a net price for a particular school, use its net price calculator. Under the Higher Education Opportunity Act of 2008, every college that takes part in federal student aid programs had to post one on its website by 29 October 2011. They ask about income, assets and family size, and some about grades and test scores, so the results are estimates rather than offers.

Federal aid now starts from the Student Aid Index (SAI), which replaced the Expected Family Contribution from the 2024-25 FAFSA. It is worked out from the information on the FAFSA and ranges from −1,500 to 999,999; a negative number shows greater need. A school finds financial need by subtracting the SAI from its cost of attendance. The SAI is an eligibility number, not the amount a family will be asked to pay.

Worked examples

Public university, starting in 5 years. The starting figures (in-state public, $30,990 today, 5% yearly rise, 4 years, 5% return taxed 25%) give a first-year cost of $39,552, rising to $45,786 in the fourth year, and $170,474 in total. In today's money that is $134,007. Saving it all needs $1,464 a month for 9 years; saving half needs $732.

Private college in 10 years with a 529 plan. Choose the 4-year private average ($65,470), 10 years until college starts, 60% from savings, $15,000 saved so far, costs rising 4%, a 6% return and 0% tax on the return. The total cost is $411,532 and $210,410 in today's money. The monthly saving for your 60% is $980, and $1,722 a month would cover everything. With the same plan in a taxable account (25% tax on the return) your 60% would need $1,074 a month.

Starting early makes the biggest difference. For the same public university starting in 15 years instead of 5, the total rises to $277,684, but the monthly saving for half falls from $732 to $464, because the money has 19 years to grow instead of 9. In a tax-free plan it is $422.

How fast do college costs rise?

Sticker prices still go up every year, but in recent years they have risen at about the pace of general inflation. From 2024-25 to 2025-26, average published tuition and fees rose 2.9% at public four-year colleges for in-state students and 4.0% at private nonprofit four-year colleges, before inflation, according to the College Board. With inflation of 2.6% over the same period, the real increase was under 1% in the public sectors and 1.4% at private nonprofits. Housing and food rose faster: at public four-year colleges the average went from $13,310 in 2024-25 to $13,900 in 2025-26, an increase of about 4.4%.

Over the decade from 2015-16 to 2025-26, inflation-adjusted tuition and fees fell 10% at public two-year colleges and 7% at public four-year colleges, and rose 2% at private nonprofit four-year colleges. Over 30 years the picture is different: in 2025 dollars, public four-year in-state tuition and fees roughly doubled, from $5,940 in 1995-96 to $11,950.

The 5% default is deliberately cautious. If you expect prices to track inflation, 3% to 4% is reasonable; if the college you have in mind has raised prices faster, use its own history. The table above shows how much the choice matters over 15 years.

Ways to save and pay for college

  • 529 plans. Contributions are not deductible on the federal return, but growth is tax-free when spent on qualified education costs, so set the tax on the return to 0%. For college, the IRS counts tuition and required fees, books, supplies and equipment, computers and internet access used mainly by the student, and housing and food for students enrolled at least half-time (up to the school's allowance). From 2026, up to $20,000 a year can also go to K-12 costs, and up to $10,000 in a lifetime toward the beneficiary's or a sibling's student loans.
  • Leftover 529 money. Since 2024, up to $35,000 in a lifetime can be moved directly to the beneficiary's Roth IRA if the account has been open at least 15 years, within the yearly Roth IRA limit. Money taken out for other purposes has its earnings taxed, generally with a 10% additional tax, with exceptions such as a tax-free scholarship.
  • Grants and scholarships do not have to be repaid. The maximum Federal Pell Grant for 2026-27 is $7,395, for undergraduates with financial need; states, colleges and private groups offer many more.
  • Federal work-study pays students with financial need for part-time jobs, often on campus.
  • Federal student loans. Limits for undergraduate Direct Loans did not change on 1 July 2026, but new Parent PLUS loans are now capped at $20,000 a year and $65,000 in total per dependent student, and Grad PLUS loans ended for new borrowers. Use the student loan calculator to see what a loan would cost after graduation.

Is it worth it? US Bureau of Labor Statistics figures for 2025 show median weekly earnings of $1,578 for full-time workers with a bachelor's degree against $966 with a high school diploma, and unemployment of 2.8% against 4.3%. That gap is why many families treat college saving as an investment, while still weighing the debt a degree might need.

How to calculate college costs yourself, including per semester

To estimate a future year's cost, multiply today's cost by (1 + yearly rise) raised to the number of years until that year. A $30,990 year rising 5% for 5 years costs 30,990 × 1.05^5 = $39,552. Add the years of study to get the total. For a cost per semester, divide the yearly cost by two; most schools bill tuition and housing per term, so a $30,990 year is about $15,495 a semester.

To find what to save, bring each year's cost back to today by dividing by (1 + your return after tax) for each year it is away, add them up, take off what you have saved, and spread the rest into equal monthly amounts with the loan payment formula. That is exactly what the calculator does, and the Cost of each year of college table shows every step.

  • Compare net prices, not sticker prices: average in-state net tuition and fees at public four-year colleges (about $2,300 in 2025-26) were under a fifth of the $11,950 sticker price.
  • Use the full cost of attendance, not just tuition: housing and food are often as large as tuition at public colleges.
  • Count the realistic number of years. Some students take longer than four years, and each extra year adds a full year of costs.
  • Revisit the plan once a year with real balances and the college's latest prices.
  • Do not skip retirement saving to fund college; loans exist for college but not for retirement.
  • Fill in the FAFSA every year your child is in college, even if you think you will not qualify, because many colleges use it for their own aid.

How it works

Pick a US average or type your own yearly cost, then say when college starts, how long it lasts and how much of it you plan to pay from savings. Each year of college costs today's price grown by the yearly rise until that year, and adding the years gives the total. Because your savings earn a return, each year's cost is also brought back to today's money at your return after tax. The monthly saving is the level amount that, saved from now until the last year of college, is worth that much today. Money you have already saved is taken off first. Under Assumptions you can change the yearly cost rise, the return on your savings and the tax on that return.

The formula

  • Cost of college year k (k = 0 for the first year) = today's cost × (1 + rise)^(years until start + k)
  • Return after tax ra = return × (1 − tax on the return)
  • In today's money = sum of each year's cost ÷ (1 + ra)^(years until start + k)
  • Still to save = today's money × your share − savings so far
  • Monthly saving = still to save × i ÷ (1 − (1 + i)^−N), with i = ra ÷ 12 and N = (years until start + years of study) × 12; at 0%, still to save ÷ N

A worked example

With the starting figures, a 4-year public college for an in-state student costs $30,990 a year today. Rising 5% a year and starting in 5 years, the first year costs $39,552 and the 4 years $170,474 in total. At a 5% return taxed 25% (3.75% after tax) that is $134,007 in today's money, or $1,464 a month for 9 years to pay it all. Paying 50% from savings needs $732 a month.

Questions

How do I calculate future college costs?

Multiply today's yearly cost by (1 + the yearly price rise) for each year until college starts, then do the same for each later year of study and add them up. The calculator does this for every year and shows the total at future prices and in today's money.

How much should I save for college each month?

It depends on the cost, how many years you have and your return. For half the cost of an average in-state public university starting in 5 years, the calculator gives about $732 a month at a 3.75% return after tax; starting 15 years ahead cuts that to about $464.

Where do the average costs come from?

They are the College Board's average total budgets for 2025-26: tuition and fees plus housing, food, books, transportation and other costs for one year. Your own college may cost more or less; choose My own yearly cost to type it.

Can I use this college cost calculator by school?

Yes. Look up the school's cost of attendance on its website, choose My own yearly cost and type it. For what you would really pay after aid at that school, run its own net price calculator, then enter the net figure as the yearly cost.

What is the SAI, and how does it affect college cost?

The Student Aid Index is a number from the FAFSA, from −1,500 to 999,999, that schools use to decide federal aid. Your financial need is the cost of attendance minus the SAI. A lower SAI usually means more need-based aid and a lower net price.

Why do I keep saving while my child is in college?

The plan spreads the saving evenly from today until the last year of college. Money saved in the later years pays for the later years, so the monthly amount is lower than if you had to have it all by the first day.

What does in today's money mean?

It is how much you would need to put aside right now, earning your return after tax, to pay every year's bill when it comes. It is lower than the total because the money grows while it waits.

What rate should I use for the cost rise?

Tuition and fees at four-year colleges rose 2.9% to 4.0% before inflation from 2024-25 to 2025-26, close to general inflation. 5% a year is a cautious guess; use 3% to 4% if you expect prices to track inflation.

How do I use it with a 529 plan?

Set Tax on that return to 0%, because 529 growth is free of federal tax when spent on qualified costs, and put the plan's balance under Saved so far. Use a return that fits the plan's investments, which often become more cautious as college gets closer.

How do I calculate college cost per semester?

Divide the yearly cost of attendance by two for a school with two semesters, or by three for a school on quarters. The calculator works in whole years, so add up the terms to get a yearly figure first.

Is starting at a 2-year college much cheaper?

Usually, yes. Average in-district tuition and fees at public two-year colleges were $4,150 in 2025-26 against $11,950 at public four-year colleges, and the College Board finds that, on average, grant aid has covered tuition and fees for first-time full-time two-year students since 2009-10. Living costs still apply, so the total budget gap is smaller than the tuition gap.

Can I use it for a university outside the US?

Yes. Choose My own yearly cost and type the full yearly cost of that university. The figures are labelled in dollars, but the maths works the same in any currency, so you can read the results in rupees, pounds or euros as long as you type every amount in that currency.
