What the LCM (least common multiple) is

The LCM, or least common multiple, of two or more whole numbers is the smallest positive number that each of them divides into exactly. The multiples of 4 are 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24 … and the multiples of 6 are 6, 12, 18, 24 …; the first number on both lists is 12, so LCM(4, 6) = 12. The Cambridge O Level syllabus calls it the lowest common multiple; it is the same thing.

This LCM calculator finds the answer for any list of whole numbers and shows it worked out two ways, with prime factors and with the greatest common divisor, so you can follow the method your teacher uses. It also gives the greatest common factor, the LCM written in prime powers and the next few common multiples.

How to use the LCM calculator

  • Type the numbers in the Whole numbers box, separated by commas, spaces or semicolons. The page opens with 12, 18, 30.
  • Type large numbers without thousands separators: 1,000 is read as the two numbers 1 and 000. Write 1000.
  • Use up to 100 positive whole numbers of up to 100 digits each. Zero, negatives and decimals are refused (see below for decimals and fractions).
  • The answer updates as you type. Open the steps for both methods and the table of prime factors.

Understanding the results

  • Least common multiple (LCM): the answer, with the numbers it belongs to. Very long answers get a Copy button.
  • Greatest common factor (GCF): the largest number that divides all of them, 6 for 12, 18 and 30. When it is 1 the page says the numbers are co-prime.
  • LCM in prime factors: the answer in prime powers, such as 2² × 3² × 5 for 180.
  • Common multiples: the LCM and its next multiples (180, 360, 540, 720 …), because every common multiple is a multiple of the LCM.
  • Prime factors of each number: a table with each number split into primes, plain and with powers, and the LCM on the last row. Numbers over about 20 digits are not split; then only the GCD method is shown.

Three ways to find the LCM, with steps

Listing multiples works for small numbers: write out the multiples of each until one appears in every list. For 6 and 8: 6, 12, 18, 24 and 8, 16, 24, so the LCM is 24. It gets slow quickly, so the calculator uses the two methods below.

  • Prime factor method, for 9, 12 and 15: write 9 = 3 × 3, 12 = 2 × 2 × 3 and 15 = 3 × 5. Take every prime the most times it appears in any one number: 2 twice (from 12), 3 twice (from 9) and 5 once (from 15). LCM = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5 = 180.
  • GCD method, for the same numbers: GCD(9, 12) = 3, so LCM(9, 12) = 9 × 12 ÷ 3 = 108 ÷ 3 = 36. Then GCD(36, 15) = 3, so LCM(36, 15) = 36 × 15 ÷ 3 = 540 ÷ 3 = 180. For more numbers, keep folding the running answer into the next number.
  • Division (ladder) method, common in school books: write the numbers in a row and divide by a prime that goes into at least one of them, bringing the others down unchanged. 9, 12, 15 ÷ 2 gives 9, 6, 15; ÷ 2 gives 9, 3, 15; ÷ 3 gives 3, 1, 5; ÷ 3 gives 1, 1, 5; ÷ 5 gives 1, 1, 1. Multiply the divisors: 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5 = 180.

All three give the same answer. The GCD method is the fastest for very large numbers, because Euclid's algorithm finds the GCD in a few divisions without splitting anything into primes.

Where the LCM is used

Adding and subtracting fractions. The lowest common denominator is the LCM of the denominators: for 5/12 + 7/18, LCM(12, 18) = 36, so 5/12 = 15/36 and 7/18 = 14/36, and the sum is 29/36. The Fraction Calculator does the whole sum.

Things that repeat. If one bus leaves a Lahore terminal every 45 minutes and another every 60 minutes, and both leave at 7:00 am, they next leave together after LCM(45, 60) = 180 minutes, at 10:00 am. Two traffic lights or two machines on different cycles line up in the same way.

Buying in packs. If juice comes in packs of 8 and cups in packs of 10, the smallest equal number of each you can buy is LCM(8, 10) = 40: 5 packs of juice and 4 packs of cups.

Gears and rotations. Two gears with 12 and 18 teeth return to the same starting position after 36 teeth have passed, which is 3 turns of the small gear and 2 of the large one.

LCM of fractions, decimals and algebraic expressions

Fractions. The LCM of fractions in lowest terms is the LCM of the numerators over the HCF of the denominators. For 2/3 and 4/9 that is LCM(2, 4) ÷ HCF(3, 9) = 4/3, and indeed 4/3 ÷ 2/3 = 2 and 4/3 ÷ 4/9 = 3, both whole numbers. Use this calculator for the numerators and the GCF Calculator for the denominators.

Decimals. Multiply all of them by the same power of 10 to make whole numbers, find the LCM, then divide back. For 0.6 and 0.8: LCM(6, 8) = 24, so the LCM is 2.4 (2.4 ÷ 0.6 = 4 and 2.4 ÷ 0.8 = 3).

Algebra. Take the LCM of the number parts and each variable at its highest power. For 4x²y and 6xy³: LCM(4, 6) = 12, x², y³, so the LCM is 12x²y³. This is the common denominator for adding algebraic fractions.

Spreadsheets and code. In Excel and Google Sheets, =LCM(12, 18, 30) returns 180; Excel cuts decimals down to whole numbers and gives an error for negatives. In Python 3.9 and later, math.lcm(12, 18, 30) gives the same.

LCM and HCF for Matric and O Level

Finding the highest common factor (HCF) and lowest common multiple (LCM) of two numbers, and writing a number as a product of its prime factors, are listed together in the Number section of the Cambridge O Level Mathematics syllabus (4024, exams in 2025 to 2027). Its Paper 1 is a non-calculator paper, so learn the prime factor method; this page is for checking.

Two facts are worth memorising for exams: for any two numbers, HCF × LCM = the product of the numbers, and the LCM is always a multiple of the HCF. If a question gives HCF(a, b) = 6, LCM(a, b) = 72 and a = 18, then b = 6 × 72 ÷ 18 = 24.

Common mistakes and related calculators

  • Multiplying the numbers together: 6 × 8 = 48 is a common multiple, but the least one is 24. The product is the LCM only when the numbers are co-prime.
  • Taking the lowest power of each prime: that gives the HCF. For the LCM take the highest.
  • Leaving out a prime that appears in only one number, such as the 5 in 15.
  • Mixing up LCM and HCF in word problems: "when do they meet again" or "smallest number of items" needs the LCM; "largest equal groups" or "longest equal pieces" needs the HCF.

How it works

Type two or more positive whole numbers, separated by commas or spaces. The least common multiple (LCM) is the smallest number that every one of them divides into exactly. The answer updates as you type and is worked out two ways: by splitting each number into primes, and by the greatest common divisor (GCD). Numbers of any length are handled exactly.

The formula

  • LCM(a, b) = a × b ÷ GCD(a, b)
  • LCM(a, b, c) = LCM(LCM(a, b), c), and so on for longer lists
  • With prime factors: LCM = the product of every prime, each taken the most times it appears in any one number
  • GCD(a, b) by Euclid's algorithm: GCD(a, b) = GCD(b, a mod b), until the remainder is 0

A worked example

LCM of 12, 18 and 30: 12 = 2 × 2 × 3, 18 = 2 × 3 × 3 and 30 = 2 × 3 × 5. Take 2 twice (from 12), 3 twice (from 18) and 5 once (from 30): 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5 = 180. Check with the GCD method: LCM(12, 18) = 216 ÷ 6 = 36, then LCM(36, 30) = 1080 ÷ 6 = 180.

Questions

How do I calculate the LCM of two numbers?

Divide their product by their greatest common factor: LCM(a, b) = a × b ÷ GCF(a, b). For 12 and 18 the GCF is 6, so the LCM is 216 ÷ 6 = 36. You can also split both into primes and take each prime at its highest power: 2² × 3² = 36.

How do I calculate the LCM of 3 numbers?

Find the LCM of the first two, then the LCM of that answer and the third. For 9, 12 and 15: LCM(9, 12) = 36 and LCM(36, 15) = 180. With prime factors, take every prime the most times it appears in any one of the three.

What is the LCM formula using the GCD?

LCM(a, b) = |a × b| ÷ GCD(a, b). It works because the product counts the shared prime factors twice, and dividing by the GCD removes one copy. For 8 and 12: 96 ÷ 4 = 24.

What is the LCM of 4 and 6?

12. The multiples of 4 are 4, 8, 12 … and of 6 are 6, 12 …, and 12 is the first they share. In primes, 4 = 2² and 6 = 2 × 3, so the LCM is 2² × 3 = 12.

What is the LCM of 6 and 8?

24. 6 = 2 × 3 and 8 = 2³, so take 2³ and 3: 8 × 3 = 24. Their GCF is 2, and 24 × 2 = 48 = 6 × 8.

What is the LCM of 12 and 18?

36. 12 = 2² × 3 and 18 = 2 × 3², so the LCM is 2² × 3² = 36, and the GCF is 2 × 3 = 6.

How do I find the LCM of fractions?

Write each fraction in lowest terms, then divide the LCM of the numerators by the HCF of the denominators. For 3/4 and 5/6: LCM(3, 5) = 15 and HCF(4, 6) = 2, so the LCM is 15/2. If you need a common denominator for adding fractions instead, take the LCM of the denominators.

What is the least common multiple used for?

Mostly for adding and subtracting fractions: the LCM of the bottom numbers is the lowest common denominator. It also answers timing questions, such as when two buses that leave every 12 and 18 minutes next leave together (after 36 minutes).

How are the LCM and the GCF related?

For two numbers, LCM × GCF = the product of the numbers. For 4 and 6: the GCF is 2, so the LCM is 4 × 6 ÷ 2 = 12. That is the GCD method shown in the steps.

Can I use 0, negative numbers or decimals?

No. The least common multiple is defined here for positive whole numbers, so 0, negative numbers and decimals such as 1.5 are refused with a message. For decimals, scale them to whole numbers first as explained in the guide.

How big can the numbers be?

Up to 100 numbers of up to 100 digits each. The answer is always exact. The prime factor method is shown when the numbers can be split into primes quickly (up to about 20 digits); for longer numbers only the GCD method is shown.
