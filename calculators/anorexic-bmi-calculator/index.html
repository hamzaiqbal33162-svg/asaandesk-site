What is an anorexic BMI?

People often ask what is considered an anorexia BMI. For adults, the World Health Organization's ICD-10 diagnostic guidelines use a BMI of 17.5 or less as one of the signs of anorexia nervosa. This anorexic BMI calculator checks a weight against that cut-off for adults, and against the expected weight for height and age for children and teens.

A number on its own is never a diagnosis. ICD-10 describes anorexia nervosa as deliberate weight loss that the person brings about and keeps up, driven by a lasting fear of fatness. A doctor looks at thoughts, habits and physical health together, and some people are seriously ill at a weight this check would call normal.

Please treat the result as an estimate that can start a conversation, not as medical advice. If food, weight or body shape are causing worry, the support links in the results and in the last section below are a good place to begin.

How to use the anorexic BMI calculator

  • Choose Metric (kg and cm) or US units (lb, feet and inches). The answers use the same units.
  • Pick Female or Male. For adults this changes nothing in the sum; for ages 2 to 18 the growth charts differ by sex.
  • Enter the age in whole years. From 19 the adult rule is used; for ages 2 to 18 an Extra months box appears so a 14-year-old who is nearly 15 is compared fairly.
  • Enter height and weight. Use a recent weight taken on a reliable scale, without heavy clothes or shoes.
  • Read the BMI and the check result, then the rows underneath: how far the weight is from the warning line and from the healthy range, and the healthy weight range for that height.

Understanding the results for adults

For an adult the calculator shows the BMI, whether it is at or under 17.5, the weight to gain to reach 17.5 (only when the BMI is at or under it), the weight to gain to reach 18.5, the WHO weight class and the healthy weight for the height (BMI 18.5 to 25). The thinness grades come from WHO Technical Report 894. Any BMI under 18.5 is called underweight, and being underweight has many possible causes, from illness and stress to genes and poverty. Only some of them are eating disorders.

Children and teens: why height matters

A child's weight should rise every year, so a single BMI line cannot work. The CDC classes a child aged 2 through 19 as underweight below the 5th percentile of BMI for age and sex, and a healthy weight from the 5th up to the 85th percentile. For eating disorders, adolescent medicine also compares a young person's BMI with the median (50th percentile) for their age, called percent of median BMI.

This calculator takes the median BMI for the exact age in years and months, multiplies it by the height squared to get an expected weight, and flags a weight under 85% of it. Because height is in the sum, a tall teen gets a higher expected weight than a short teen of the same age, which is fairer than a target based on age alone.

Worked examples in kg and lb

  • Karachi, adult: a woman of 24, 158 cm and 41 kg, has a BMI of 16.42 (moderate thinness). The check may suggest anorexia; reaching BMI 17.5 takes 2.7 kg and the healthy range starts 5.2 kg higher, at 46.2 kg.
  • US units, adult: a man of 32, 5 ft 11 in and 118 lb, has a BMI of 16.46. He is 7.5 lb under the 17.5 line and 14.6 lb under the healthy range, which for his height runs from 132.6 to 179.2 lb.
  • Just above the line: a woman of 27, 163 cm and 47 kg, has a BMI of 17.69. The check does not suggest anorexia, yet she is in mild thinness and 2.2 kg below BMI 18.5, which is worth raising with a doctor if the weight was lost recently or without trying.
  • A girl of 14 years 3 months, 157 cm and 38 kg: the median BMI for her age is 19.5, so her expected weight is 48.0 kg and 85% of it is 40.8 kg. Her BMI of 15.42 is 79% of the median, so the check flags it; she is 2.8 kg under the 85% line.
  • Two boys of 16, both 52 kg: at 172 cm the expected weight is 60.7 kg and he is at 86% of the median (not flagged); at 182 cm the expected weight is 68.0 kg and he is at 76% (flagged, 5.8 kg under the 85% line). Same weight, very different meaning.

What BMI is considered severe anorexia?

There is no safe low BMI, and danger does not depend on BMI alone. The MSD Manual for professionals (2025 review) treats an adult BMI under 17 as significantly low, and 17 to under 18.5 as possibly significantly low depending on where the person started. It adds that hospital care should be considered once weight falls below about 75% of the recommended weight, or sooner when there is fainting from low blood pressure, a very slow heart rate or an irregular heartbeat.

Anorexia is serious: the same source cites a pooled analysis of 35 studies that found 5.1 deaths per 1,000 person-years. Vomiting, laxatives or diuretics can lower potassium and sodium and disturb the heart rhythm, so they are dangerous whatever the BMI. Severity is a medical judgement, so please do not use this page to rank how ill someone is.

Can you have anorexia at a normal or higher weight?

Yes. Atypical anorexia is the term for having the thoughts and behaviours of anorexia, often after a large weight loss, while weight stays within or above the normal range. The UK charity Beat and Australia's National Eating Disorders Collaboration both stress that it is as serious as typical anorexia, and ICD-10 lists atypical anorexia nervosa as its own code (F50.1).

So a person with obesity can still have a restrictive eating disorder, and a BMI over 17.5 does not mean someone is fine. A fast drop in weight, missed periods, dizziness or a fear of eating matter more than the number on this page.

Warning signs and getting help

  • Common signs listed by the NHS: eating very little or skipping meals, strict food rules, lying about eating, avoiding meals with others, exercising a lot, weighing often, wearing baggy clothes, periods stopping, feeling cold, dizzy or tired, and in children slower growth or delayed puberty.
  • Anorexia most often starts in the mid-teens and is most common in young women, but men and women of any age can be affected (NHS). The MSD Manual puts lifetime prevalence at about 1.5% of women and 0.1% of men.
  • Treatment works best early. In the NHS, young people are usually offered family therapy first, and adults one of several talking therapies alongside a meal plan; most people are treated as outpatients.
  • In Pakistan, findahelpline.com lists the Rozan Counseling Helpline (0304 111 1741) for emotional and mental health support, and 1122 for emergencies (checked November 2026). In the UK, Beat's helpline is 0808 801 0677.

How it works

This is a screening check on body weight only. For adults 19 and over it follows the World Health Organization ICD-10 guideline, which treats a BMI of 17.5 or less as one possible sign of anorexia nervosa, and it shows the weight that would bring the BMI to 17.5 and to the healthy range from 18.5. Children and teens grow, so for ages 2 to 18 the weight is compared with the expected weight for their height and age: the median (middle) BMI for their age and sex on the CDC growth charts, times their height squared. Weighing less than 85% of that is a warning sign. Because it uses height, a tall and a short teen of the same age get different expected weights, which is how adolescent medicine works it out.

The formula

  • BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)²
  • Adults (19 and over): a BMI of 17.5 or less is a warning sign (WHO ICD-10); weight to reach BMI 17.5 = 17.5 × height² − weight
  • Children and teens (2 to 18): expected weight = median BMI for age and sex (CDC 2000) × height²; warning sign below 85% of it
  • Percent of median BMI = BMI ÷ median BMI for age × 100
  • Healthy range: adults 18.5 to 25 × height²; children the 5th to 85th percentile BMI for age × height²

A worked example

A woman of 24 who is 158 cm (1.58 m) tall and weighs 41 kg has a BMI of 41 ÷ 1.58² ≈ 16.42, which is under 17.5. A BMI of 17.5 at her height is 17.5 × 1.58² ≈ 43.7 kg, so the calculator shows 2.7 kg to reach that line and 5.2 kg to reach the start of the healthy range (46.2 kg, BMI 18.5).

Questions

What BMI is considered anorexic?

For adults, the WHO ICD-10 guidelines use a BMI of 17.5 or less as one sign of anorexia nervosa, and that is the line this calculator uses. For children and teens there is no fixed number: a weight under 85% of the expected weight for height and age is the warning sign here. A low BMI alone does not mean someone has anorexia.

What is severe anorexia BMI?

Doctors judge severity from BMI together with heart rate, blood pressure, blood tests and how fast weight was lost. As a guide, the WHO calls an adult BMI under 16 severe thinness, and the MSD Manual says hospital care should be considered below about 75% of the recommended weight. Anyone near those levels needs medical care, not a calculator.

Is there a lowest BMI that is safe with anorexia?

No. Serious heart and salt problems can happen at many BMIs, especially with fast weight loss, vomiting or laxatives, so there is no point at which low weight is safe. If you are losing weight on purpose and feel unable to stop, please talk to a doctor soon.

Can you be anorexic and be obese?

Yes. Atypical anorexia means having the thoughts and habits of anorexia while weight is in or above the normal range, often after a large weight loss. Beat and other eating disorder services say it is just as serious, so a high or normal BMI does not rule out an eating disorder.

Can this tell me if I have anorexia?

No. Anorexia is diagnosed by a doctor or mental health professional from how a person thinks and feels about food, weight and body shape, not from a number. A low BMI is only one possible sign, and people can be very unwell at a normal weight. If you are worried, please talk to someone you trust and a doctor.

What is the difference between being underweight and having anorexia?

Underweight simply means a BMI under 18.5 for an adult, or under the 5th percentile for a child, and it has many causes, including illness, stress and natural build. Anorexia is a mental illness with deliberate restriction and a strong fear of gaining weight. A doctor can check which applies.

Why is the rule different for children and teens?

Their weight should rise as they grow, so a fixed BMI line does not work. Their weight is compared with the expected weight for their height and age from the CDC growth charts; less than 85% of it is a warning sign that a doctor should look at, and a child who stops gaining weight while growing needs a check too.

How is the expected weight for a teen worked out?

It is the median BMI for the teen's age and sex, times their height squared. Using the BMI keeps height in the sum, so a tall and a short teen of the same age get different expected weights, which a target based on age alone would miss.

Where can I get support, including in Pakistan?

In Pakistan, findahelpline.com lists the Rozan Counseling Helpline (0304 111 1741) for emotional and mental health, and 1122 for emergencies. In the UK, Beat runs helplines and web chat for anyone affected, including families. For other countries, findahelpline.com lists free and confidential helplines; the links are under Sources on this page.

What if I am worried about a friend or my child?

Choose a calm, private moment, talk about what you have noticed and how you feel rather than about weight or food rules, and offer to help them see a doctor. Support services such as Beat also help families and friends.
