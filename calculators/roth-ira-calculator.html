What is a Roth IRA?

A Roth IRA is a US individual retirement account that you fund with money you have already paid income tax on. You get no deduction when you put money in, but the investments grow tax-free and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free too. You open one yourself at a bank, broker or fund company and choose the investments.

This Roth IRA calculator projects how much your account could hold at retirement, using the IRS yearly contribution limits for your age. It also puts the same deposits into an ordinary taxable account that pays tax on its growth every year, so you can see in dollars what the Roth's tax-free growth is worth. An optional income check tells you whether your income lets you contribute directly for 2026 or 2027.

Because the tax break comes at the end, a Roth IRA tends to suit people who expect the same or a higher tax rate in retirement, and younger savers with many years of growth ahead.

How to use the Roth IRA calculator

  • Enter the Roth IRA balance today (default 20,000; use 0 for a new account).
  • Enter the Amount you add each year (default 7,500), or tick Put in the most the IRS allows every year to use the limit for each age.
  • Enter Your age (default 30) and your Retirement age (default 65).
  • Set the Expected yearly return (default 6%). This is an assumption; real returns vary from year to year.
  • Set Your tax rate for the taxable comparison (default 22%), the rate the taxable account pays on each year's growth.
  • Open Limits and income check to choose the Tax year of the limits (2026 or 2027) and, if you want, pick your filing status under Check whether my income allows a Roth and enter your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI).

Deposits are counted at the end of each year, from your current age up to the year before retirement. If you type more than the limit, the calculator uses the limit and tells you.

Roth IRA contribution and income limits for 2026 and 2027

The yearly limit is shared by all your traditional and Roth IRAs, and you cannot put in more than your taxable compensation for the year. Above the phase-out range for your filing status you cannot contribute directly at all.

The calculator keeps the chosen year's limit for every future year; the IRS raises it with inflation over time, so a maxed-out account may end up larger.

How the Roth IRA phase-out is calculated

Inside the phase-out range the limit shrinks in a straight line. The IRS worksheet in Publication 590-A takes the share of the range your MAGI has passed, removes that share of the limit, rounds the result up to the next 10 and allows at least 200 if anything is allowed. Conversion income from a traditional IRA is left out of MAGI for this test.

Single in 2026 with a MAGI of 160,000: you are 7,000 into the 15,000-wide range, so you lose 7/15 of 7,500 and may put in 4,000. In 2027 the range starts at 158,000, so the same MAGI allows 6,500. A married couple filing jointly with a MAGI of 245,000 in 2026 is 3,000 into a 10,000 range and may put in 5,250 each. A single filer with a MAGI of 170,000 is above the 2026 range and gets 0.

If you put in more than you are allowed, the IRS charges a 6% excise tax each year the excess stays in the account, so check your income before you contribute, or wait until you know it.

A Roth IRA for kids, teens and 18-year-olds

There is no minimum age for a Roth IRA, and contributions can be made at any age, but the money must come from taxable compensation, such as wages from a part-time job. The limit is the smaller of the yearly limit and what the child earned, so a teen who earns 3,000 can put in up to 3,000. For a minor, a parent or guardian usually opens a custodial account.

Starting young is powerful. An 18-year-old who puts 3,000 a year into a Roth IRA until 65, at 6%, would have 723,296, from 141,000 of deposits. The same money in a taxable account at a 22% tax rate would reach 486,033.

Withdrawals, conversions and other Roth IRA rules

  • Your own contributions come out first and can be withdrawn at any time without tax or penalty; earnings come out last.
  • A qualified distribution is tax-free: it must come at least five years after the first tax year you contributed to a Roth IRA, and be made at 59½ or later, because of disability, to a beneficiary after death, or for a first home (up to 10,000 in a lifetime).
  • Taking earnings out before those conditions are met can mean income tax and a 10% additional tax.
  • Roth IRAs have no required minimum distributions while you are alive: you can leave the money in as long as you live. Beneficiaries do have to take distributions.
  • You can convert a traditional IRA to a Roth IRA. The converted amount is taxed as income the way a traditional IRA withdrawal would be, which is why some people whose income is above the Roth range put money in a traditional IRA first and move it to a Roth later.
  • Contributions for a tax year can be made until the tax filing deadline for that year, usually in April of the following year.

How it works

Enter what is in your Roth IRA today, how much you add each year (or tick the box to always put in the most allowed), your age, retirement age and the return you expect. Each year the deposit is capped at the IRS limit for your age, which rises by the catch-up amount from 50. The Roth balance grows untaxed. To show what the tax shelter is worth, the same deposits also go into an ordinary taxable account that pays your tax rate on each year's growth. Under Limits and income check you can pick the tax year and check whether your income lets you contribute directly.

The formula

  • Deposit each year = the smaller of your amount and the IRS limit for your age (limit + catch-up from age 50)
  • Roth: B ← B × (1 + R) + deposit (the deposit goes in at the end of the year)
  • Taxable: T ← T × (1 + R × (1 − tax rate)) + deposit; tax that year = T × R × tax rate
  • Roth growth = Roth balance − money put in; Roth advantage = Roth balance − taxable balance
  • Income check (IRS worksheet): allowed = limit − limit × (MAGI − start of range) ÷ width of range, rounded up to $10, at least $200

A worked example

With $30,000 today and $7,500 a year from age 30 to 65 at 6%, the Roth grows to $1,066,343. You put in $292,500, so $773,843 is tax-free growth. In a taxable account at a 25% tax rate the money grows at 6% × 0.75 = 4.5% a year and ends at $751,245 after $152,915 of tax, so the Roth is $315,098 ahead.

Questions

How much can I put in a Roth IRA in 2026?

For 2026 and 2027 the limit is 7,500 a year, or 8,600 if you are 50 or older. It is one limit shared by all your traditional and Roth IRAs, you cannot put in more than you earned from work that year, and a high income reduces or removes it.

What is the Roth IRA income limit for 2026?

For 2026 the limit shrinks for single filers with a MAGI between 153,000 and 168,000 and for married couples filing jointly between 242,000 and 252,000; above the range you cannot contribute directly. The 2027 ranges are 158,000 to 173,000 and 250,000 to 260,000.

How do I calculate my Roth IRA contribution in the phase-out?

Find how far your MAGI is into your range, take that share of the limit off, and round up to the next 10. Single with a 2026 MAGI of 160,000 is 7,000 into a 15,000 range, so the limit drops by 7/15 to 4,000. The calculator's income check does this for you.

How do I calculate Roth IRA growth?

Each year, multiply the balance by 1 plus the return and add that year's deposit. At 6%, 20,000 today plus 7,500 a year from 30 to 65 grows to 989,483. The calculator runs this year by year and shows the result as a table and a chart.

How much will my Roth IRA be worth at retirement?

It depends on how much you add, for how long and the return. Starting from 0 at 25 with 7,500 a year at 6%, the account reaches 1,160,715 at 65; starting at 45 it reaches 275,892. Enter your own numbers to see your figure.

Can a child or teenager have a Roth IRA?

Yes, if they have earned income such as wages. They can contribute up to the smaller of the yearly limit and what they earned, and a parent usually opens a custodial account for a minor. Starting at 18 with 3,000 a year at 6% gives 723,296 by 65.

Can married couples each have a Roth IRA?

Yes. Each spouse has their own IRA and their own limit, and a spouse with little or no income can still contribute based on the couple's joint income when filing jointly. The income limit uses your joint MAGI, so at 245,000 in 2026 each spouse may put in 5,250.

Why compare with a taxable account?

A Roth's benefit is that its growth is never taxed. Putting the same deposits in an ordinary account that pays tax on each year's interest or gains shows how much that is worth to you in dollars.

Can I take my money out early?

You can take out what you put in at any time, tax- and penalty-free. Growth is tax-free once you are 59½ and five years have passed since your first Roth contribution; taking growth out earlier can mean tax plus a 10% penalty.

What is the difference between a Roth IRA and a 401(k)?

A Roth IRA is an account you open yourself, with a 7,500 limit in 2027 and an income limit. A 401(k) is an employer plan with a much higher limit, 25,500 of deferrals in 2027, often with an employer match. Many people use both.

Will the limit stay the same in future years?

The IRS raises the limit with inflation in 500 steps, so it will probably grow. This projection keeps the chosen year's limit for every future year, which may slightly understate what a maxed-out account can reach.
