How much house can I afford?

A house affordability calculator answers the question every buyer starts with: what is the most I can pay for a home? It works backwards from your income and debts, using the same ratios mortgage lenders use, to the highest price whose monthly payment, tax and insurance still fit. Or, in the second mode, it starts from the monthly amount you choose to spend.

The answer depends on four things: your gross income, your other monthly debt payments, your down payment and the interest rate. A higher rate or a bigger car loan lowers the price you can afford; a larger down payment raises it. The calculator shows each of these effects instantly, so you can test a scenario before you talk to a lender.

How to use the house affordability calculator

  • From my income: enter your Household income a year before tax (everyone on the loan) and Other debt payments a month, such as car and student loans, card minimums and child support.
  • From a monthly budget: enter the Monthly amount for the home and tick whether that budget also pays tax, insurance, HOA and upkeep.
  • Down payment: as a % of the price or a dollar amount; 20% by default.
  • Loan term (30 years) and Interest rate (6.5%).
  • Loan type: Conventional (28% / 36%), FHA (31% / 43%), VA (41%) or My own debt limit, where you set the highest share of income for all debts. For VA, say whether this is your first VA loan and whether the funding fee goes on the loan or is paid at closing.
  • Home costs: Property tax, Home insurance, HOA or building fee and Upkeep and repairs, each as a % of the price or a dollar amount a year; Closing costs as a % of the price; and the PMI rate for conventional loans with less than 20% down.

Front-end and back-end ratios, and the 28/36 rule

The front-end ratio, also called the housing ratio, is your monthly housing cost (principal, interest, property tax, home insurance, mortgage insurance and HOA dues) divided by your gross monthly income. The back-end ratio, better known as the debt-to-income ratio or DTI, adds every other recurring debt payment.

The 28/36 rule of thumb says housing should take no more than 28% of gross income and all debts no more than 36%. It is a guideline, not a law. Fannie Mae, for example, caps DTI at 36% for loans underwritten by hand, allows up to 45% when the borrower meets its credit score and reserve requirements, and accepts up to 50% when its automated Desktop Underwriter system approves the loan.

Worked examples

Debts change the answer. With the same $100,000 income but a $900 car and student loan payment each month, the back-end limit becomes the binding one: 36% of $8,333 is $3,000, minus $900 leaves $2,100 for housing. The affordable price falls from $360,460 to $324,414, about $36,000 less for every $900 of monthly debt.

An FHA loan with 3.5% down. With $100,000 of income and no debts, the FHA 31% limit allows $2,583 a month for housing. The affordable price is $324,604 with $11,361 down. You pay an upfront premium of $5,482 (1.75% of the loan) at closing and an annual premium of 0.55% of the loan, $144 a month, so the cash needed at closing is only $26,581, against $82,906 for the conventional loan with 20% down.

A VA loan with nothing down. The VA looks only at the 41% back-end ratio, so the same income supports $3,417 a month for housing and a price of $433,959. A first-time VA borrower's funding fee of 2.15%, $9,330, is added to the loan, and the cash needed at closing is the $13,019 of closing costs.

A fixed budget. If you decide $2,500 a month is your limit for everything, including tax, insurance and upkeep, the budget mode gives a price of $323,700 with 20% down.

Costs beyond the mortgage payment

Property tax and insurance can add hundreds of dollars a month, and both vary widely by state and county, so use the figures for the area you are looking in. HOA or condo fees are part of the housing ratio too. Upkeep is easy to forget; the calculator assumes 1.5% of the price a year for repairs by default, and older homes usually need more.

Private mortgage insurance on a conventional loan with less than 20% down is not permanent. You can ask your servicer to cancel it when the loan balance is scheduled to fall to 80% of the home's original value, and the servicer must end it automatically when the balance is scheduled to reach 78%, provided you are current on payments.

Then there is the cash at closing: the down payment, closing costs of a few percent of the price for the lender's fees, title, appraisal and taxes, and any upfront FHA premium. Some loans also require reserves, money still in savings after closing, so do not plan to spend every dollar you have.

Housing affordability outside the US

The ratio method works anywhere; only the limits change. In Canada, CMHC caps the gross debt service ratio at 39% and the total debt service ratio at 44% for insured mortgages, and borrowers must qualify at a stress-test rate; see our Canadian Mortgage Calculator.

In Pakistan, banks set their own limits for home finance under State Bank of Pakistan rules, so ask your bank for its debt-burden limit and current rate, then choose My own debt limit and switch the currency to PKR. For example, with a household income of Rs 3,600,000 a year (Rs 300,000 a month), a 40% limit, 30% down and a 20-year loan at a rate of 13%, the calculator gives a price of about Rs 14.5 million, with a loan of about Rs 10.1 million at Rs 118,793 a month. Set property tax and closing costs to your city's actual figures for a sharper answer.

How to afford more house, and related tools

  • Pay down other debts first. Every $100 of monthly debt you clear frees about $100 a month for housing under the back-end limit.
  • Raise your credit score; a better score usually earns a lower rate, and each percentage point matters a lot over 30 years.
  • Save a bigger down payment. It lowers the loan, may remove PMI, and raises the price you can afford.
  • Shop for the rate. Compare several lenders' offers on the same day.
  • Consider whether an FHA or VA loan fits your situation, but count the full cost of their premiums and fees.

Remember that a lender's maximum is not a target. Buying below it leaves room for savings, emergencies and the costs no ratio captures. The housing affordability index used by the National Association of Realtors makes the same point at a national level: a value of 100 means a median-income family has exactly enough income to qualify for a median-priced home with 20% down, when principal and interest take no more than 25% of income.

How it works

Lenders cap two ratios. The front-end ratio is your monthly housing cost (loan payment, mortgage insurance, property tax, home insurance and HOA) divided by your gross monthly income. The back-end ratio adds your other debt payments. Conventional loans usually stop at 28% and 36%, FHA loans at 31% and 43%, and VA loans look at the back-end 41% only. The calculator takes the lower of the two limits as the most you can spend on housing each month, then finds the home price whose monthly costs add up to exactly that, with your down payment. Mortgage insurance follows the loan type: PMI on a conventional loan with less than 20% down, HUD's annual premium on an FHA loan (its upfront premium is paid at closing), and the one-time VA funding fee, which is added to the loan. In the budget mode you name the monthly amount yourself. Upkeep and closing costs are shown so you see the full cost, but lenders leave them out of the ratios.

The formula

  • Monthly income I = yearly income ÷ 12
  • Housing limit H = the lower of front% × I and back% × I − other debts (VA and your own limit: back-end only)
  • Loan L = price − down payment; payment = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), r = rate ÷ 12, n = years × 12
  • Solve price P: payment + mortgage insurance + (tax + insurance + HOA) ÷ 12 = H; every part is linear in P, so P = (H − fixed costs + payment factor × down) ÷ (payment factor + costs per dollar of price)
  • PMI = 0.5% of the loan a year with under 20% down; FHA annual premium from HUD's table; VA fee = table rate × loan, added to the loan
  • Cash at closing = down payment + closing costs (+ FHA upfront premium 1.75% of the loan)

A worked example

With $100,000 a year ($8,333 a month), no other debts, 20% down and a 30-year loan at 6.5%, with property tax at 1.2% and insurance at 0.5% of the price, the 28% front-end limit allows $2,333 a month. The price that costs exactly that is $360,460: a $288,368 loan at $1,823 a month plus $360 of tax and $150 of insurance.

Questions

How do I calculate how much house I can afford based on income?

Take your gross monthly income, multiply by 28% for the housing limit and by 36% minus your other debt payments for the total limit, and use the lower figure. Then find the price whose payment, tax and insurance add up to it. This calculator does the last step for you; $100,000 a year with no debts and 20% down at 6.5% gives about $360,000.

What is the 28/36 rule?

A guide many conventional lenders use: spend no more than 28% of your gross monthly income on housing (payment, insurance, tax and HOA) and no more than 36% on all debts together, housing included. The lower of the two decides what you can borrow.

How much house can I afford on a $100,000 salary?

With no other debts, 20% down, a 30-year loan at 6.5% and typical tax and insurance, about $360,000 under the 28/36 rule. With $900 of monthly debts it drops to about $324,000, and with an FHA or VA loan it can be higher, at the cost of mortgage insurance or a funding fee.

Why can I afford more with an FHA loan?

FHA allows higher ratios, 31% for housing and 43% for all debts, and needs only 3.5% down. You pay for it with mortgage insurance: 1.75% of the loan upfront and an annual premium, usually 0.55% with under 5% down.

Is the VA funding fee a monthly cost?

No. It is a one-time fee, a share of the loan that depends on your down payment and whether you used a VA loan before. Most borrowers add it to the loan, which raises the monthly payment a little. Here it is added to the loan unless you choose to pay it at closing.

Which number should I trust, the income rule or my own budget?

The income rule shows roughly what a lender may approve. Your own budget shows what feels comfortable after savings and other spending. Try both modes; buying below the lender's maximum leaves room for repairs and surprises.

Does a house payment include taxes and insurance?

Lenders count them: the housing ratio uses principal, interest, property tax, home insurance, mortgage insurance and HOA dues. The result shows the loan payment on its own and all home costs a month, including upkeep.

What else do I need cash for?

Besides the down payment, closing costs of about 2% to 5% of the price, any upfront mortgage insurance, moving costs and a cash reserve. The result shows the cash needed at closing.

What is the housing affordability index?

It is a national measure published by the National Association of Realtors. A value of 100 means a median-income family earns exactly enough to qualify for a median-priced existing home with 20% down; higher values mean homes are more affordable.
