What statistics is, and what this calculator does

A statistics calculator turns a list of numbers into the handful of figures that describe it. Statistics is the study of collecting, summarising and drawing conclusions from data. It has two broad branches: descriptive statistics, which summarises the data you have, and inferential statistics, which uses a sample to say something about a bigger population.

This page does descriptive statistics in one go. It reports where the data are centred (mean, median, mode, geometric mean), how big the list is (count, sum, smallest, largest) and how spread out the values are (range, variance and standard deviation, for both a population and a sample), plus the sums of squares behind them.

These are the same numbers a handheld scientific or graphing calculator shows in its one-variable statistics mode, so you can use this page to check homework, lab results, sales figures or survey scores.

How to use the statistics calculator

  • Paste a list: type or paste your numbers separated by commas, spaces or new lines. A column copied from a spreadsheet works. The sample list starts with 10, 2, 38, 23, 38, 23, 21, 23.
  • One number per box: switch tabs to type each value in its own box, as on a handheld calculator. Twelve boxes are shown and twelve more are under More boxes. Empty boxes are skipped.
  • Negative numbers and decimals are fine. Every result updates as you type.
  • For data given as a frequency table, type each value as many times as it occurs, or use Paste a list with the values repeated.

Two related figures follow from these. The coefficient of variation is s ÷ x̄, here 3.1623 ÷ 15 = 21%, which compares spread between data sets on different scales. A z-score, (x − x̄) ÷ s, says how many standard deviations a value is from the mean; the Z-score Calculator works it out.

Worked example step by step

  • Data: seven students in a Lahore classroom score 12, 15, 11, 18, 14, 20 and 15 on a quiz marked out of 20.
  • Mean: the sum is 105, so x̄ = 105 ÷ 7 = 15.
  • Median: sorted, the scores are 11, 12, 14, 15, 15, 18, 20. The 4th of 7 is 15.
  • Mode: 15 appears twice, every other score once, so the mode is 15.
  • Range: 20 − 11 = 9.
  • Deviations from the mean: −3, 0, −4, 3, −1, 5, 0. Their squares are 9, 0, 16, 9, 1, 25, 0, which add up to 60.
  • Sample variance: 60 ÷ (7 − 1) = 10, so the sample standard deviation is √10 = 3.1622776601684.
  • Population variance: 60 ÷ 7 = 8.5714285714286, so σ = 2.9277002188456. Use this one only if these seven students are the whole class you care about.

Population or sample: why divide by N − 1?

If your numbers are every member of the group you are describing, such as all seven students in a small class, use the population columns, which divide by N. If they are a sample from a bigger group, such as seven students picked from a school of 700, use the sample columns, which divide by N − 1.

The reason is that a sample's own mean sits closer to its values than the true population mean does, so squared distances from it come out a little small. Dividing by N − 1 instead of N (Bessel's correction) makes the sample variance an unbiased estimate of the population variance. The difference matters for small lists and fades for large ones.

Most research, surveys, quality checks and lab measurements are samples, so the sample standard deviation is the one to report unless you really have the whole group.

Statistics in Excel and on a scientific calculator

  • Mean, median and mode: =AVERAGE(A1:A7), =MEDIAN(A1:A7), =MODE.SNGL(A1:A7).
  • Standard deviation: =STDEV.S(A1:A7) for a sample (the n − 1 method) and =STDEV.P(A1:A7) for a whole population. For the quiz scores they give 3.1623 and 2.9277.
  • Variance: =VAR.S(A1:A7) and =VAR.P(A1:A7).
  • Sums of squares: =SUMSQ(A1:A7) for Σx² and =DEVSQ(A1:A7) for Σ(x − x̄)².
  • Other: =GEOMEAN(A1:A7), =COUNT(A1:A7), =SUM(A1:A7), =MAX(A1:A7)-MIN(A1:A7).
  • On scientific and graphing calculators, the statistics or one-variable mode lists the same values. The population standard deviation is usually labelled σx and the sample one sx or Sx; check which one your teacher or report wants before copying it down.

Common mistakes and tips

  • Picking the wrong standard deviation. Samples use s (N − 1), whole populations use σ (N).
  • Forgetting to square before adding. The sum of plain deviations from the mean is always 0, which is why variance squares them.
  • Comparing standard deviations of data in different units. Use the coefficient of variation instead.
  • Trusting the mean for skewed data. Report the median alongside it when a few values are far from the rest.
  • Typing a frequency table as two columns. Enter each value as many times as it occurs.

How it works

Paste your numbers into the box (commas, spaces and new lines all work as separators), or switch to "One number per box" and type them in one at a time, the way you would on a handheld statistics calculator. Every result updates as you type: the averages first (mean, median and mode), then the size of the list (count, sum, smallest, largest, range), the geometric mean, and the spread as both the whole-population and the sample standard deviation and variance. The sums of squares are there too, for anyone checking a formula by hand.

The formula

  • Mean = Σx ÷ N
  • Median = the middle number of the sorted list, or the average of the two middle numbers when N is even
  • Mode = the number or numbers that appear most often (when one appears more than once)
  • Range = largest − smallest
  • Geometric mean = (x₁ × x₂ × ... × x_N)^(1/N), for numbers that are 0 or more
  • Population variance σ² = Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ N; sample variance s² = Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ (N − 1); standard deviation = √variance

A worked example

For 3, 7, 7, 19 the sum is 36, so the mean is 36 ÷ 4 = 9. Sorted, the middle two numbers are 7 and 7, so the median is 7, and 7 is also the mode because it appears twice. The range is 19 − 3 = 16. The squared distances from 9 are 36, 4, 4 and 100, which add up to 144: the population variance is 144 ÷ 4 = 36 (standard deviation 6) and the sample variance is 144 ÷ 3 = 48 (standard deviation 6.9282032302755).

Questions

What is statistics in maths?

It is the branch of mathematics that collects, organises and interprets data. Descriptive statistics, such as the mean and standard deviation on this page, summarise a data set, while inferential statistics use a sample to draw conclusions about a larger group.

How do I calculate statistics for a list of numbers?

Paste the list into the box and every common statistic appears at once. By hand, start with the count and sum, divide for the mean, sort for the median and range, count repeats for the mode, then square the distances from the mean for the variance.

Which standard deviation should I use?

Use the sample column when your numbers are some of a bigger group (most surveys, tests and measurements). Use the whole-population column only when your list holds every member of the group you are describing.

What is the difference between variance and standard deviation?

Variance is the average squared distance from the mean, so its units are squared. The standard deviation is its square root, back in the units of the data, which makes it easier to read: a quiz with s = 3.16 marks spreads about 3 marks from the mean.

What is the sum of squares used for?

Σ(x − x̄)², the sum of squared differences from the mean, is the top line of both variance formulas and of many tests and regressions. Σx² is used in the shortcut Σx² − (Σx)² ÷ N, which gives the same 60 for the quiz scores.

How do I calculate statistics in Excel?

Use AVERAGE, MEDIAN and MODE.SNGL for the averages, STDEV.S and VAR.S for a sample, STDEV.P and VAR.P for a population, and DEVSQ for the sum of squared deviations. The answers match the ones on this page.

Why does the geometric mean say "Not defined"?

The geometric mean multiplies the numbers together and takes a root, which only works for numbers that are 0 or more. If your list has a negative number there is no geometric mean, so we say so instead of showing a made-up value.

What if no number repeats?

Then the list has no mode. When two or more numbers tie for the most appearances, all of them are listed as modes, smallest first.

How do I use the one-number-per-box mode?

Type each number in its own box; empty boxes are skipped, and clearing a box removes that number. Twelve boxes are shown, and twelve more are under "More boxes". For longer lists, paste them into "Paste a list" instead.
