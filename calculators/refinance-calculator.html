What a refinance calculator tells you

A refinance calculator shows whether replacing your current loan with a new one saves money, and when the switch pays for itself. Refinancing means taking a new loan, usually at a different rate or term, and using it to pay off the old one. People refinance to get a lower rate, cut the monthly payment, pay the loan off sooner, switch from a variable to a fixed rate, or take cash out of their home's equity.

A lower rate alone does not make a refinance worthwhile. Points and closing costs are paid upfront, and a longer new term can add more interest than the lower rate saves. This page counts all of it: every payment left on your current loan against every payment on the new one, plus the costs and any cash out, so the verdict follows the total cost.

How to use the refinance calculator, step by step

  • Choose your currency and how to describe your current loan. Balance and payment: enter the Balance you still owe and the Current monthly payment (principal and interest only). Original loan: enter the Original loan amount, Original term and Time left, and the calculator works out the balance.
  • Enter the Current interest rate.
  • Describe the new loan: New loan term in years and New interest rate.
  • Under More options, enter Points (1 point = 1% of the new loan, paid upfront for a lower rate), Closing costs and fees, and any Cash out you want on top of your balance.
  • Read the lifetime saving or extra cost at the top, the new payment, the verdict, the break-even month and the side-by-side table.

How to calculate refinance savings and the break-even point

Start with what the current loan still costs: every payment left. At $1,900 a month, $250,000 at 7.25% takes 264 more payments, $499,811.91 in all. The new 20-year loan at 6.25% has a payment of 250,000 × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−240) with r = 0.0625 ÷ 12, which is $1,827.32, or $438,556.92 over 240 months. Subtract that and the $5,500 of costs: $499,811.91 − $438,556.92 − $5,500 = $55,754.99 saved.

The refinance break-even formula you often see is upfront costs ÷ monthly saving. Here that is $5,500 ÷ $72.68 = about 76 months. But the payment barely moved because the new loan is also two years shorter; much of each payment now pays principal faster. Interest is the true cost, so this calculator counts the interest saved month by month on the same balance and finds that it covers the $5,500 after 26 months.

The two methods agree when the term stays the same. When the term changes, the simple formula can mislead in either direction: a 30-year refinance shows a big monthly saving and a quick payback while costing more in total.

Points are a trade-off between upfront cost and rate. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) explains that one point equals 1% of the loan, points lower your rate in exchange for paying more at closing, and they tend to pay off only if you keep the loan for a long time. Lender credits work the other way: lower closing costs for a higher rate. In the table, 2 points save the most over the full term but take the longest to pay back.

Good and bad reasons to refinance

  • A lower rate you will keep long enough: the saving beats the costs well before you expect to move.
  • A shorter term: moving from 22 years left to 15 at a lower rate raises the payment but, in the table above, saves $108,471.62.
  • From a variable to a fixed rate: you may pay a little more today in exchange for a payment that cannot rise.
  • Removing mortgage insurance: under the CFPB's description of US PMI rules, after a refinance the original value used for PMI is the appraised value at the time of refinancing, so a home that has risen in value may need no PMI on the new loan.
  • Taking cash out for something that pays back, such as a needed repair or replacing far dearer debt.
  • Weak reason: only to lower the payment by stretching the term. It eases the budget now, but usually costs more in total, as the 30-year line shows.
  • Weak reason: chasing a small rate cut with high costs. At 6.75%, half a point below today's rate, the default costs take 48 months to recover.

Cash-out refinance

A cash-out refinance borrows more than you owe and pays you the difference. With $40,000 cash out on the default loan, the new loan is $290,000, the payment $2,119.69 and the costs $5,900, because points are charged on the bigger loan; you keep $34,100 after costs. The lifetime figure adds the cash back so the comparison is fair, but you still repay that money with interest over 20 years.

Cash out makes sense mostly for spending that adds value, such as home improvements, or to replace much more expensive debt. Lenders limit how much of your home's value you can borrow; the HELOC Calculator estimates your equity limit, and a line of credit or home equity loan can be cheaper than refinancing a low-rate first mortgage.

Refinancing a car loan or other loans

The same maths works for any fixed-payment loan. A car loan with $20,000 left at 9% and $500 a month has 48 payments to go. Refinanced over 4 years at 6.5% with a $200 fee, the payment drops to $474.30 and you save $901.40 in total, with the fee paid back after 5 months. Enter it with Balance and payment, and leave points at 0.

Student loans, personal loans and credit card balances can be refinanced too, but check what you give up: a new loan from a different lender may not keep the repayment options or protections of the old one. Taxes, rules and timing in the US

The APR shown follows the idea in Regulation Z's Appendix J: the yearly rate at which the payments are worth the loan minus the points and fees you pay. It lets you compare a new loan with costs against your current rate on equal terms.

Points paid to refinance are generally not deducted all at once. IRS Topic No. 504 (updated November 2026) says points on a refinance are deducted ratably over the term of the loan, unlike points on a loan to buy or build your main home, which may be deductible in the year paid if you meet the conditions.

When you refinance your main home with a new lender, Regulation Z (12 CFR 1026.23) generally gives you until midnight of the third business day after closing to cancel. A refinance with your current lender is exempt, except for any new money borrowed beyond the old balance and the refinancing costs.

Think about how long you will keep the loan. If you might sell or refinance again before the break-even month, the upfront costs may never be recovered. Ask each lender for a Loan Estimate with the same number of points so you can compare offers directly, as the CFPB suggests.

Tips, common mistakes and related calculators

  • Compare the lifetime result, not just the new payment; a lower payment over a longer term often costs more.
  • Use only principal and interest as your current payment; tax and insurance do not change with a refinance.
  • Count every cost: application, appraisal, title, points and any prepayment penalty on the old loan.
  • Do not restart the clock without a reason. If you have 22 years left, a new 20-year loan keeps you on track; a new 30-year loan adds 8 years of payments.
  • Keep paying your old payment after refinancing if you can; the extra goes to principal and widens the saving.
  • Rate offers change often. Re-run the numbers on the day you lock a rate.

How it works

Describe your current loan either by its balance and monthly payment or by the original amount, term and time left. The calculator follows it to the end, counting every payment still to make, including a smaller last one. The new loan is your balance plus any cash out, repaid over the new term at the new rate. Points and fees are paid upfront. The lifetime result is what you still pay on the current loan, minus what you would pay on the new one, plus the cash you take out, minus the upfront costs.

The formula

  • New loan L₁ = balance + cash out; new payment M₁ = L₁ × r₁ ÷ (1 − (1 + r₁)^−n₁), r₁ = new rate ÷ 1200
  • Upfront costs U = points ÷ 100 × L₁ + fees
  • Lifetime saving = (payments left on the current loan − M₁ × n₁) + cash out − U
  • APR a: the yearly rate at which n₁ payments of M₁ are worth L₁ − U today
  • Break-even: the first month in which the interest saved since refinancing covers U (both loans start from today's balance)
  • Simple break-even often quoted elsewhere: U ÷ monthly payment saving (misleading when the term changes)

A worked example

The defaults: you owe $250,000 at 7.25% and pay $1,900 a month, which takes 264 more payments ($499,811.91). A new 20-year loan at 6.25% costs $1,827.32 a month, $438,556.92 in all. With 1 point ($2,500) and $3,000 of fees, refinancing saves $499,811.91 − $438,556.92 − $5,500 = $55,754.99, the APR is 6.531%, and the lower interest has paid back the $5,500 after 26 months.

Questions

How do I calculate refinance savings?

Add up every payment left on your current loan, subtract every payment on the new loan and the points and fees, and add back any cash out. With the defaults that is $499,811.91 − $438,556.92 − $5,500 = $55,754.99 saved.

How do I calculate the refinance break-even point?

The quick version is upfront costs ÷ monthly saving. It is accurate only when the term stays the same, so this calculator instead finds the month when the interest saved covers the costs: 26 months for the defaults, against about 76 months by the quick formula.

When is refinancing worth it?

When the money you save over the years you will keep the loan is larger than the upfront costs. Look at the break-even month: if you expect to sell or refinance again before it, refinancing probably will not pay off.

Why can a lower rate still cost more?

Starting a new 30-year loan on a balance that had 20 years left spreads it over more payments, so you can pay more interest in total even at a lower rate. Points, fees and cash out add to that. The verdict here looks at the total cost, not only the rate.

How do I calculate a refinance mortgage payment?

Use the loan payment formula on the new loan amount (balance plus any cash out): M = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), with r the new monthly rate and n the new number of months. $250,000 over 20 years at 6.25% gives $1,827.32.

What are points?

A point is a fee of 1% of the new loan, paid at closing to get a lower rate. One point on a $250,000 loan costs $2,500. They pay off only if you keep the loan long enough.

What is the APR of the new loan?

The APR spreads the points and fees over the loan as if they were extra interest. Comparing it with your current rate shows whether the new loan is cheaper per year once its costs are counted.

Does cash out count as a saving?

No. Cash out is more money borrowed. The lifetime figure adds it back only so the two loans compare fairly; you still repay it, with interest, as part of the new loan.

Can I use this as a car loan refinance calculator?

Yes. Enter the car loan's balance and payment, its rate, the new term and rate, and any fee, with points at 0. A $20,000 balance at 9% refinanced over 4 years at 6.5% with a $200 fee saves $901.40.

Should I refinance to a 15-year mortgage?

If you can afford the higher payment, a shorter term usually saves the most. On the default loan, 15 years at 6.25% raises the payment by $243.56 a month but saves $108,471.62 overall and ends the loan 7 years sooner. Keep enough cash aside so the higher payment does not strain your budget.

Are refinance points tax-deductible?

In the US, points paid to refinance are generally deducted a little each year over the life of the loan, according to IRS Topic No. 504, rather than all in the year you pay them.

What is refinancing a home loan?

Replacing your current home loan with a new one, usually from a new lender or at a new rate or term. The new loan pays off the old one, and from then on you make the new payments.
