How it works

Resistors are too small to print a number on, so their value is painted as coloured rings. Hold the resistor with the tolerance ring (often gold or silver, a little apart from the rest) on the right and read from the left: the first two rings (three on 5 and 6 band parts) are digits, the next one says how many zeros to add (or, for gold, silver and pink, to divide by 10, 100 or 1,000), then comes the tolerance, and on 6 band parts a last ring for how much the value drifts with temperature. Pick the colours and the value appears with its tolerance range, its printed letter code and whether it is a standard value. The other tabs go the other way (value to colours), add resistors in series or in parallel, and work out the resistance of a length of wire from its size and metal.

The formula

  • Value = (digits as one number) × multiplier; 3 and 4 band resistors have 2 digits, 5 and 6 band resistors have 3
  • Digit colours: black 0, brown 1, red 2, orange 3, yellow 4, green 5, blue 6, violet 7, grey 8, white 9; multiplier = 10 to the digit, gold × 0.1, silver × 0.01, pink × 0.001
  • Tolerance: brown ±1% (F), red ±2% (G), orange ±0.05% (W), yellow ±0.02% (P), green ±0.5% (D), blue ±0.25% (C), violet ±0.1% (B), grey ±0.01% (L), gold ±5% (J), silver ±10% (K), no band ±20% (M)
  • Temperature coefficient: black 250 (U), brown 100 (S), red 50 (R), orange 15 (P), yellow 25 (Q), green 20 (Z), blue 10 (Z), violet 5 (M), grey 1 (K) parts per million per kelvin
  • Series: R = R₁ + R₂ + … ; parallel: 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …
  • Wire: R = L ÷ (σ × A), with A = π × (d ÷ 2)² and σ the conductivity of the metal

A worked example

Yellow, violet, red, gold: the digits are 4 and 7, red multiplies by 100, so the value is 47 × 100 = 4,700 Ω = 4.7 kΩ. Gold means ±5%, so a good part measures between 4.465 kΩ and 4.935 kΩ. Its printed code is 4K7, and 4.7 kΩ is a value in every standard series from E3 to E24.

Questions

Which end do I start reading from?

Start at the end where the rings are bunched together. The tolerance ring, usually gold or silver on common parts, sits on the right and is often a little wider or set apart. If both ends look the same, try both readings: only one usually gives a standard value.

What do the letters such as J or F mean?

They are the IEC 60062 letter codes printed on parts and in parts lists: F is ±1%, G ±2%, J ±5%, K ±10% and M ±20%. The temperature letters work the same way, for example R means 50 ppm per kelvin.

What is a standard (E-series) value?

Resistors are made in a fixed set of values per decade, chosen so that neighbouring values overlap within their tolerance. E12 has 12 values (1.0, 1.2, 1.5 … 8.2), E24 has 24 for ±5% parts, and E96 has 96 for ±1% parts. If the value you need is not in the list, the calculator shows the nearest ones.

Why does a 6th band sometimes mean something else?

On most 6 band resistors the last ring is the temperature coefficient, as here. Some military parts use a 5th or 6th ring for reliability (failure rate) instead, so check the maker's data sheet for those.

Does the wire resistance change with temperature?

Yes. Metals conduct less well when warm: copper and aluminium gain about 0.4% resistance per °C. The conductivity presets are for 20 °C; type your own conductivity for another temperature or metal.
