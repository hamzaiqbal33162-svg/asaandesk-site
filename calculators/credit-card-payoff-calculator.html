What the credit cards payoff calculator does

This credit cards payoff calculator builds a repayment plan for several cards at once. You tell it the total you can pay each month and list your cards; it works out when each card is cleared, the date you become debt-free, the interest you pay along the way and exactly how much to send to each card in each month.

The plan follows one simple rule. Every card gets its minimum payment so nothing falls behind, and every spare amount goes to a single target card. When that card is paid off, its whole payment rolls on to the next target. Your monthly total stays the same, so each card is attacked with more money than the one before it, which is why this approach is often called a debt avalanche or a debt snowball.

You choose the order. Highest rate first sends the extra to the most expensive card and saves the most interest. Smallest balance first clears whole cards sooner. The results always show the other order too, so you can see the price of the quicker wins in money.

How to use the credit cards payoff calculator

  • Currency: choose it at the top; only the symbol changes.
  • Total you can pay each month: one figure for all cards together. It must be at least the minimum payments added up. The default is 600.
  • Pay extra to: Highest rate first (avalanche) or Smallest balance first (snowball).
  • Number of cards: from 1 to 20. Three example cards are filled in to start with.
  • For each card, type a Name (optional), the Balance owed, the Minimum payment from your statement and the Interest rate (APR). A row with no balance is left out of the plan.

If your budget is even 1 short of the minimums, the calculator stops and tells you the amount needed, because a missed minimum brings late fees and can trigger a penalty rate. It also warns you when a card's minimum is too small to cover its own interest; that card's balance only starts falling once the extra money reaches it.

Worked example: three cards, 600 a month

The default plan uses three cards: an Everyday card with 4,500 at 26.99% (minimum 110), a Store card with 2,800 at 21.99% (minimum 75) and a Travel card with 6,200 at 18.49% (minimum 150). Together they owe 13,500, and the minimums add up to 335. In the first month the cards add 248.05 of interest between them.

  • Months 1 to 14: the Everyday card has the highest rate, so it gets its 110 minimum plus the spare 265, which is 375 a month. The other two cards get their minimums. A final 54.11 in month 15 clears it, after 804.11 of interest.
  • Months 15 to 20: the Everyday card's money rolls on to the Store card, which now gets 450 a month and is clear in month 20, after 833.41 of interest.
  • Months 21 to 29: all 600 goes to the Travel card. It is clear in month 29 with a last payment of 446.81, after 2,109.29 of interest.
  • Total: 29 payments, 3,746.81 of interest and 17,246.81 paid in all.

With Smallest balance first, the Store card goes first and is clear by month 10, the Everyday card by month 21 and the Travel card by month 30. That plan costs 3,928.24 of interest, 181.43 more, and takes one month longer. The first card is gone five months sooner, which is the trade-off the snowball method makes.

Avalanche or snowball: what the choice costs

Highest rate first always costs the least interest, because every spare amount goes where interest grows fastest. Smallest balance first can cost more, but closing an account early is a visible win, and some people find it easier to keep going that way. The bigger your budget, the smaller the gap between the two, because the cards are cleared quickly either way.

Example in rupees: two cards in Pakistan

Pick PKR as the currency and enter yearly rates; if a bank quotes card mark-up per month, multiply it by 12. Suppose Bank A's card has Rs 180,000 owed at 40% a year with a Rs 9,000 minimum, Bank B's card has Rs 95,000 at 36% with a Rs 5,000 minimum, and you can pay Rs 25,000 a month in total.

The minimums take Rs 14,000, so the other Rs 11,000 goes to Bank A's card, the more expensive one: Rs 20,000 a month in months 1 to 10 and Rs 17,587.30 in month 11 clear it. Bank B's card then gets the full Rs 25,000 and is cleared in month 14. The plan costs Rs 68,809.98 of interest on Rs 275,000 owed. Paying the smaller balance first would cost Rs 2,666.43 more.

Payoff amount, extra payments and spreadsheets

Is there a payoff amount on credit cards? Yes. The balance on your statement is a snapshot from the end of the billing cycle, but interest keeps building day by day until your payment arrives if you are not inside a grace period. To close a card completely, ask the issuer or check the app for the current payoff amount, which can be slightly higher than the statement balance.

Extra payments go straight to the plan: raise the total you can pay each month and the calculator moves the extra to the target card. If you are paid weekly, add up a month of weekly payments (weekly amount × 52 ÷ 12) and type that as the monthly budget. Paying more often can trim interest a little on cards that charge it daily, so the result is slightly cautious.

In Excel or Google Sheets, one card's payoff time is =NPER(rate/12, -payment, balance). A plan for several cards needs one row per month with a column per card, which is what the Month by month table already shows here.

Ways to pay off credit card debt faster

  • Stop adding new purchases to the cards in the plan; the calculator assumes no new spending.
  • Keep paying the same monthly total even when a card is cleared, so its payment rolls on to the next one.
  • Move a high-rate balance to a 0% balance transfer card only if you can clear it before the offer ends, and count the transfer fee.
  • A consolidation loan at a lower rate replaces several card payments with one; compare its total interest with this plan before you sign.
  • Ask your issuers for a lower rate. It costs nothing to ask, and any cut shows up here as less interest.
  • In the US, the Credit CARD Act of 2009 sends any payment above the minimum to the balance with the highest APR on that card, so extra payments on a card with several rates already go to the costliest part first.

How it works

Enter the total you can put towards your cards each month and, for each card, what you owe, its minimum payment and its interest rate. Each month every card first gets its interest added and its minimum paid. Whatever is left of your budget goes to one card at a time: the one with the highest rate (the avalanche method) or the one with the smallest balance (the snowball method). When a card is paid off, the money that went to it moves on to the next card, so your monthly total stays the same until every card is clear.

The formula

  • Each card, each month: interest = balance × APR ÷ 100 ÷ 12, added to the balance
  • Each card then gets its minimum payment (or the whole balance if that is smaller)
  • Money left = your budget − the minimums paid; it goes to the target card, up to its balance, then to the next one
  • Target order: highest APR first (avalanche) or smallest starting balance first (snowball); ties keep your list order
  • Interest of a card = what you paid on it − what you owed at the start

A worked example

Three cards: $4,500 at 26.99% (minimum $110), $2,800 at 21.99% ($75) and $6,200 at 18.49% ($150), with $600 a month. The minimums take $335, so the other $265 goes to the 26.99% card: $375 a month until it is clear in month 15. Its money then moves to the 21.99% card ($450 a month), which is clear in month 20, and finally all $600 goes to the last card. All three are paid off in 29 months (2 years 5 months) with $3,746.81 of interest.

Questions

How do I calculate credit card payoff time for several cards?

Add each card's interest every month, pay every minimum, and send what is left of your budget to one target card until it is clear, then roll its payment on. Doing that by hand takes a spreadsheet row per month, so the calculator does it and shows the month each card is cleared.

Which is better, highest rate first or smallest balance first?

Highest rate first always costs the least interest, because the money goes where interest grows fastest. Smallest balance first clears whole cards sooner, which some people find easier to stick with. The results show both, so you can see what the quicker wins cost.

Why must the budget cover all the minimum payments?

Missing a minimum payment usually brings a late fee, a mark on your credit record and often a higher penalty rate. The plan only works if every card gets at least its minimum each month.

What does "What to pay" in the card table mean?

It is the payment plan for that card: how much to pay on it each month and for which months. The amount goes up when a card before it is paid off and its money moves on.

How do I add extra payments?

Raise the total you can pay each month. Every extra amount goes to the current target card, and the headline shows the new debt-free date and interest straight away.

Can I use weekly payments?

The plan works in months, so convert first: weekly amount × 52 ÷ 12. Paying 150 a week is 650 a month. On cards that charge interest daily, paying weekly saves a little more than the result shows.

Is there a payoff amount on credit cards?

Yes. Because interest can build daily until your payment arrives, the amount to close a card can be a little more than the statement balance. Ask the card issuer or check the app for the payoff amount on the day you pay.

Is a consolidation loan better than paying off the cards?

It can be, if the loan's rate is clearly lower than your cards and you stop using the cards. Compare the loan's total interest with the total interest in this plan, and include any loan fee.

Does it include new spending on the cards?

No. It assumes you stop using the cards while paying them off. New purchases or fees make the plan longer.

Can I add more than three cards?

Yes. Change the number of cards (up to 20) and fill in the new rows. A row with no balance is left out.
