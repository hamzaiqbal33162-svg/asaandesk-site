What a mortgage calculator shows you

A mortgage is a loan for buying a home in which the home itself is the security: if the payments stop, the lender can take the property and sell it to get its money back. A mortgage calculator turns the price, your down payment, the loan term and the interest rate into the number most buyers care about, the monthly payment, and then shows where every payment goes for the life of the loan.

This calculator works out a fixed-rate loan, where the rate and the principal-and-interest payment stay the same from the first month to the last. On top of that it can add the yearly costs of owning the home (property tax, home insurance, private mortgage insurance, HOA or building fees and anything else you want to count), test extra payments, and compare paying monthly with paying every two weeks.

Lenders often talk about PITI: principal, interest, taxes and insurance. The first number in the results is the principal and interest, the part the loan contract fixes. The second number adds taxes, insurance and the other costs, which is closer to what actually leaves your bank account each month when the lender collects them through an escrow account.

Everything is worked out in your browser as you type, in any currency. The maths is the same for dollars, pounds, dirhams or Pakistani rupees; what changes from country to country are the rules around the loan, such as minimum down payments, insurance and tax, and the sections below cover the US and Pakistan in detail.

A few words come up again and again on a mortgage, so here they are in plain terms:

  • Principal: the amount you borrow, and later the part of it you still owe.
  • Interest: what the lender charges for the money, worked out each month on the balance still owed.
  • Down payment: the part of the price you pay yourself; the rest is the loan.
  • Equity: the share of the home you own outright, the home's value minus what you still owe.
  • Loan-to-value ratio (LTV): the loan divided by the home's value, so 20% down means an LTV of 80%.
  • Term or tenure: how many years you take to repay, such as 15 or 30.
  • Amortization: paying the loan off in equal payments, each split between interest and principal.
  • Escrow: an account the lender uses to collect and pay your property tax and insurance.
  • Mortgage deed: the document that pledges the home to the lender as security until the loan is repaid.

How to use this mortgage calculator, step by step

The four main fields are always shown; the groups below them open when you need them. The results change after every edit, so you can try several versions of the same purchase in a minute.

  • Home price: the purchase price of the home, before the down payment is taken off.
  • Down payment: type it as a percentage of the price or as an amount, using the % or currency switch next to the field. The loan amount is the price minus the down payment; at 100% there is nothing left to borrow and the calculator tells you so.
  • Loan term: the number of years you repay over, from 1 to 50. Thirty and fifteen years are the usual choices in the US; in Pakistan the State Bank allows housing finance for up to 30 years.
  • Interest rate: the yearly rate on the loan, in percent (6.5 by default). Use the note rate, not the APR, because the APR folds in fees that are not part of the monthly payment.
  • Taxes and costs: tick the box to include property tax, home insurance, mortgage insurance (PMI), an HOA or building fee and other costs. Each can be a yearly amount or a percentage; for tax, insurance, HOA and other costs the percentage is of the home price, and for PMI it is of the loan.
  • Yearly cost increases: how fast property tax, insurance, the HOA fee and other costs rise each year. With a 3% rise, every loan year costs 3% more than the one before, and the totals over the loan include those rises.
  • Extra payments: an extra amount every month (from a start month you choose), every year (from the month of the first one), one one-time payment in a month you pick, and a list of more one-time payments written as amount and month, for example 5000 2028-06; 2500 2030-01.
  • Dates and comparison: the month of your first payment, which sets the dates in the schedule and the payoff month, and a box that adds a comparison with paying half the payment every two weeks.
  • Currency: choose it at the top of the calculator. It changes the symbol and number format only, not the maths.

A good habit is to run the plain loan first, with taxes and costs switched off, and then add one thing at a time. You then see exactly how much each choice, such as a smaller down payment or an extra $200 a month, changes the payment and the total cost.

First-time buyers get the most out of it with three numbers ready: the price range you are looking at, the cash you can put down after keeping some savings aside, and a rate quote from a lender for your credit profile. Run the price range at that rate and at one point higher, and you will know which homes stay affordable even if rates rise before you buy.

Understanding your mortgage results

Monthly payment is principal and interest only. With taxes and costs switched on, Monthly with costs adds one twelfth of each yearly cost, and PMI only while it applies. Loan amount and Down payment confirm what you borrow and what share of the price you pay up front.

Total of N payments adds up every scheduled payment, and Total interest is the part of that total that is not the loan itself. On a long loan this figure surprises many buyers: at 6.5% over 30 years, a $320,000 loan costs $408,142.36 in interest, more than the amount borrowed. Paid off in gives the payoff month and how long the loan runs.

When you add extra payments, three more lines appear: Extra payments in total, Interest saved by the extras and how much sooner the loan ends. With PMI included, Mortgage insurance ends shows the month it stops and after how many payments. At a 0% rate the payment is simply the loan divided by the number of months, $888.89 for $320,000 over 30 years, and a note says that every payment goes to the loan.

The tables go deeper. Monthly and lifetime costs lists every cost per month and over the whole loan. Monthly or every two weeks compares the scheduled plan with your extras and with half payments every two weeks. The Yearly schedule shows the interest, the principal and the balance for each loan year, and the Monthly schedule (open it to see it) lists every payment. A donut chart shows where the money goes over the loan, and a line chart tracks the balance, the interest paid so far and the total paid so far.

Mortgage amortization explained, with a 30-year example

Amortization is the way a level payment pays off a loan. Each month the lender first charges interest on the balance still owed, at one twelfth of the yearly rate, and whatever is left of the payment reduces the balance. Because the balance is largest at the start, early payments are mostly interest; as the balance falls, the interest part shrinks and the principal part grows, while the payment stays the same.

Take the default example: a $400,000 home with 20% down, so a $320,000 loan at 6.5% for 30 years, with the first payment in January 2027. The payment is $2,022.62. In the first month the interest is $320,000 × 0.065 ÷ 12 = $1,733.33, so only $289.28 goes to the loan. Not until payment 233, in May 2046, is the principal part of a payment larger than the interest part.

After 10 years of payments you still owe about 85% of the original loan, and the balance only drops below half during year 22. This is why selling or refinancing in the early years leaves less equity than many owners expect, and why extra payments made early save the most interest.

The share of interest in what you pay depends on the rate and the term together. Over 30 years at 6.5%, 56% of the $728,142.36 you pay is interest. Over 15 years at the same rate, the payment is $2,787.54 and only 36% of the $501,757.84 total is interest, because the balance falls much faster.

Where the payment formula comes from: the payments, each discounted back to today at the monthly rate r, must add up to the loan L. That gives L = M ÷ (1 + r) + M ÷ (1 + r)^2 + … + M ÷ (1 + r)^n. This is a geometric series; summing it gives L = M × (1 − (1 + r)^−n) ÷ r, and solving for M gives the mortgage payment formula M = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n). With r = 0.065 ÷ 12 and n = 360, the 360 discounted payments of $2,022.62 add up to exactly $320,000.

In Excel or Google Sheets the same payment is =PMT(6.5%/12, 30*12, -320000), which returns 2,022.62. Keep the units consistent, a monthly rate with a number of months; the minus sign only tells the spreadsheet that the loan is money you receive.

PMI, property tax, home insurance and HOA fees

Private mortgage insurance protects the lender, not you. On a conventional US loan it is usually required when the down payment is under 20% of the price, and Freddie Mac estimates it at roughly $30 to $70 a month for every $100,000 borrowed, with the cost depending on your credit score and how much you put down. Low down payments are possible: HUD says FHA down payments can be as low as 3.5%, and Fannie Mae offers 97% loan-to-value loans, so 3% down, for HomeReady buyers and some first-time buyers. FHA and VA loans have their own insurance or funding fee rules; see the FHA Loan Calculator for those.

Under the US Homeowners Protection Act, as the CFPB explains, you can ask your servicer to cancel PMI once the balance is scheduled to reach 80% of the home's original value, if your payments are current and the home has not lost value. It must end automatically when the balance is scheduled to reach 78%, and in any case the month after the midpoint of the loan term. This calculator counts PMI until the balance after a payment falls to 80% of the price, the point at which you can ask for it to stop.

Example: the same $400,000 home with only 10% down is a $360,000 loan, so the payment rises to $2,275.44. With PMI at 0.5% of the loan a year ($150 a month, inside Freddie Mac's range), the results show that mortgage insurance ends after 94 payments, in November 2034 for a January 2027 start, and costs $14,100 in all. Without a request it would run until the balance is scheduled to reach 78%, which in this schedule is payment 109. Counting the default property tax and insurance, the monthly cost starts at $2,950.44 and the loan costs $1,022,260.16 over 30 years.

Property tax is set by your local government, usually as a share of the assessed value, and it can change every year. The 1.2% default is only a starting point: look up the rate or last year's bill for the home you are considering and enter it as a percentage or an amount. Home insurance protects the building and your belongings; enter the quote you get from an insurer.

Homeowners association (HOA) or building fees pay for shared areas and services in a development or apartment block. They are a fixed part of the cost of owning such a home, so include them here. Use Other costs for upkeep and repairs, which are easy to forget in a first budget.

Many lenders collect property tax and insurance with each payment, keep the money in an escrow account and pay the bills for you. The CFPB notes that because taxes and premiums change, the escrow part of your payment can go up or down from year to year; the Yearly cost increases fields let you test a steady rise.

Rises add up over a long loan. With property tax and insurance both rising 3% a year on the default example, tax grows from $400 a month in the first year to about $943 in year 30, and insurance from $125 to about $295. Over the loan, tax then comes to $228,362.00 instead of $144,000.00 and insurance to $71,363.12 instead of $45,000.00, while the principal-and-interest payment never changes.

Extra payments and bi-weekly payments

Any money you pay on top of the scheduled payment goes straight to the balance. From the next month on, interest is charged on a smaller balance, so the loan ends sooner while the scheduled payment stays the same. Paying half the monthly payment every two weeks works because a year has 26 two-week periods: 26 half payments equal 13 monthly payments, one more than the usual 12. Adding one twelfth of the payment every month gets almost the same result, as the last two rows show, and it does not depend on your lender accepting payments every two weeks. If a company offers to set up a bi-weekly plan for a fee, compare that fee with simply adding the extra yourself.

To see what it would take to pay the loan off completely at some point, read the balance in the Monthly schedule for the month you have in mind. On the default loan the balance is $299,555.13 after 5 years and $271,283.60 after 10 years. A lender's payoff quote can differ a little from the schedule, because it counts interest to the exact payoff day and may add fees.

Before you prepay, check your loan papers for a prepayment penalty. The CFPB says many mortgages do not have one; where it exists it usually covers the first three or five years and applies to paying off the whole loan or a large part at once, while small extra principal payments are normally allowed. Ask the servicer to apply extra money to principal, not to next month's payment.

An extra payment earns a guaranteed return equal to your loan rate, but money paid into a house is hard to get back out. It usually makes sense to keep an emergency fund and clear dearer debts, such as credit cards, before you prepay a mortgage. To plan a payoff by a target date, try the Mortgage Payoff Calculator.

Interest rate vs APR, points and mortgage rates over time

The interest rate is the price of the money you borrow, and it sets your monthly payment. The annual percentage rate (APR) is broader: the CFPB describes it as the interest rate plus points, broker fees and other charges you pay to get the loan, expressed as a yearly rate. That is why the APR is usually higher, and why it is useful for comparing offers but not for working out the payment.

A worked example: a $320,000 loan at 6.5% for 30 years has a payment of $2,022.62. If you pay $6,000 in fees to get it, you really receive $314,000 for that same payment, which works out to an APR of about 6.683%. The APR Calculator does this for your own fees.

Discount points are a fee paid at closing for a lower rate. According to the CFPB, one point costs 1% of the loan amount, so one point on $320,000 is $3,200. Whether points pay off depends on how long you keep the loan: divide the cost of the points by the monthly saving to see how many months it takes to earn the money back.

Rates themselves move with the economy. Freddie Mac has published a weekly average 30-year fixed rate since April 1971. On 19 November 2026 it stood at 7.28%, with the 15-year average at 6.66%. The highest week on record was 18.63% on 9 October 1981 and the lowest 2.65% on 7 January 2021.

This calculator is for fixed rates. An adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM) starts with a rate that is usually fixed for the first few years and then changes at set intervals. As the CFPB explains, the new rate is an index that moves with market conditions plus a margin that is set in your loan agreement and does not change after closing; caps in the contract limit how far the rate can move. To estimate an ARM here, run the loan at the starting rate, note the balance in the yearly schedule at the first reset, then run that balance for the years left at the index plus the margin, and again at the highest rate the caps allow.

15-year vs 30-year mortgage, refinancing and tax

A shorter term means a higher payment but far less interest, and lenders usually charge a lower rate for it. Here is a $320,000 loan at Freddie Mac's average rates for 19 November 2026.

The 15-year loan costs $626.29 more a month but saves $281,374.47 of interest and ends 15 years sooner. The 30-year loan keeps the required payment low, which leaves room in the budget, and you can still prepay it as if it were a shorter loan. A middle path is to take the 30-year loan and add a fixed extra amount each month, with the option to stop if money gets tight.

Refinancing means taking a new loan to pay off the old one, usually to get a lower rate, change the term or take cash out. Suppose that after five years of the default loan you owe $299,555.13 and can refinance at 5.5% for 25 years with $6,000 of closing costs. The payment falls from $2,022.62 to $1,839.53, a saving of $183.09 a month, so the costs are earned back after about 33 months, and the interest over the remaining 25 years drops from $307,230.17 to $252,304.04.

Stretching the new loan to 30 years cuts the payment further, to $1,700.84, but the total interest rises to $312,747.66, more than you would pay by keeping the old loan. To test any refinance, enter your current balance as the home price with 0% down, plus the new rate and term, or use the Refinance Calculator.

Whatever the term, lenders check that the payment fits your income. The CFPB defines the debt-to-income ratio (DTI) as all your monthly debt payments divided by your gross monthly income, and notes that limits differ between loan products and lenders. For example, the default payment with costs of $2,547.62 plus a $450 car payment, on a gross income of $9,000 a month, is a DTI of about 33%.

In the US, mortgage interest can be an itemized deduction. IRS Publication 936 allows interest on up to $750,000 of home acquisition debt ($375,000 if married filing separately), or $1 million for debt taken out before 16 December 2017. The draft 2026 edition adds that qualified mortgage insurance premiums, including PMI, can be deducted again for 2026, with a phase-out once adjusted gross income passes $100,000 ($50,000 if married filing separately). Tax rules change, so check the current publication or a tax adviser before you count on a deduction.

Home loans in Pakistan: using the calculator in PKR

Choose PKR at the top and the same calculator works as a home loan calculator for Pakistan. The State Bank of Pakistan's Prudential Regulations for Housing Finance (updated 18 August 2026) set the outer limits: a tenor of at most 30 years, a loan-to-value ratio of at most 90:10, so at least 10% down, and total monthly installments of no more than 65% of the borrower's net disposable income. Banks must also insure or take takaful cover on the house for the amount still owed, and must disclose all fees, any prepayment penalty and the annualized percentage rate before the loan starts.

A bank may lend less than these limits. As one example, HBL's Islamic home finance FAQ (checked in November 2026) offers financing up to 70% of the property value for up to 25 years, priced at the 1-year KIBOR (Karachi Interbank Offered Rate) plus 3% to 4.2% depending on the customer, with the monthly amount fixed for each 12 months. A rate set this way floats: when KIBOR moves, the installment moves at the next reset. For context, the State Bank raised its policy rate to 12% from 27 October 2026.

Islamic banks offer home finance through Diminishing Musharakah, a form of joint ownership for which the State Bank has adopted a Shariah standard: you and the bank own the house together, you pay rent on the bank's share and buy its units over time. The amounts usually follow a declining-balance pattern much like a loan, so entering the profit rate as the interest rate gives a useful estimate; your bank's repayment schedule is the final word.

Worked example: a PKR 20,000,000 house with 30% down is a PKR 14,000,000 loan. At 15% a year for 20 years the monthly installment is Rs 184,350.54, and the 240 installments add up to Rs 44,244,129.97, so Rs 30,244,129.97 is interest or profit. In the first year, only Rs 120,251.34 of the Rs 2,212,206.49 you pay reduces the loan.

PMI as described above is a US arrangement, so leave it at 0 unless your bank charges something similar. Enter the yearly property insurance or takaful premium your bank quotes under Home insurance and your yearly property tax under Property tax, as amounts.

Other State Bank rules worth knowing: home finance above PKR 10 million needs a valuation by at least one valuer on the Pakistan Banks' Association panel, loans up to PKR 5 million may be secured by marking a lien in the land records, and your bank must send you a yearly statement of the principal repaid, the principal outstanding and the markup or profit paid. Compare that statement with the yearly schedule here to check that your loan is on track.

Mortgage tips, common mistakes and related calculators

  • Use the note rate for the payment and the APR to compare lenders; mixing them up makes the payment look higher than it is.
  • Count the whole monthly cost. In the default example, tax and insurance turn a $2,022.62 payment into $2,547.62, a difference of $525 a month.
  • Test a higher rate before you commit. If your rate is not locked, see what a rate half a point or a full point higher does to the payment.
  • Remember closing costs. Fees, taxes and other charges at closing come on top of the down payment; your Loan Estimate lists them, and US law gives you the final Closing Disclosure three business days before closing so you can check it against this calculator.
  • Pick the plan you can keep. A 15-year loan saves the most interest, but a 30-year loan with steady extra payments can come close while keeping the required payment lower.
  • For a floating-rate loan, rerun the calculator at each reset with the balance from your latest statement as the price, 0% down and the years left as the term.
  • Second homes, land, mobile homes, investment and commercial property use the same maths, but the terms often differ from a main-home loan, so enter the down payment and rate you are actually offered.

Related tools: the House Affordability Calculator shows what price fits your income, the Amortization Calculator handles any fixed-rate loan, and the UK Mortgage Calculator and Canadian Mortgage Calculator follow local loan conventions.

How it works

Type the home price, your down payment, the loan term and the yearly interest rate. The calculator finds the one monthly payment that clears the loan by the end of the term, then follows the loan month by month: each month's interest is the balance times the monthly rate, and the rest of the payment lowers the balance. Property tax, insurance, PMI, HOA and other costs are added on top of that payment, with any yearly rises you enter. Extra payments go straight to the balance, so the loan ends sooner and you pay less interest, and a second table compares paying monthly with paying half the payment every two weeks.

The formula

  • Loan L = home price − down payment
  • Monthly rate r = yearly rate ÷ 100 ÷ 12; number of payments n = years × 12
  • Monthly payment M = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n); at 0% interest, M = L ÷ n
  • Each month: interest = balance × r; principal = M − interest + any extra; new balance = balance − principal
  • Yearly costs in % are a share of the home price (PMI: of the loan), paid as one twelfth a month; with a yearly rise g, loan year y costs amount × (1 + g)^(y − 1)
  • PMI is paid while the balance after a payment is above 80% of the home price
  • Every two weeks: M ÷ 2 paid 26 times a year at a rate of yearly rate ÷ 26 per payment, until the loan is cleared

A worked example

A $400,000 home with 20% down is a $320,000 loan. At 6.5% for 30 years, r = 0.065 ÷ 12 ≈ 0.0054167 and n = 360, so M = 320,000 × 0.0054167 ÷ (1 − 1.0054167^−360) = $2,022.62 a month. The 360 payments add up to $728,142.36, so $408,142.36 of it is interest. Adding the default property tax (1.2% of the price, $400 a month) and home insurance ($1,500 a year, $125 a month) makes $2,547.62 a month.

Questions

How do you calculate a mortgage payment?

Turn the yearly rate into a monthly rate r (rate ÷ 100 ÷ 12) and the term into a number of payments n (years × 12). The payment is M = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), where L is the loan after the down payment. For $320,000 at 6.5% over 30 years that is $2,022.62 a month, with property tax, insurance and any PMI on top.

How much is a $300k mortgage a month?

On a 30-year loan of $300,000, principal and interest come to $1,798.65 a month at 6%, $1,995.91 at 7% and $2,201.29 at 8%. Over 15 years the same loan costs $2,531.57 to $2,866.96 a month at those rates. Add property tax, home insurance and any PMI or HOA fee for the full monthly cost.

Does the monthly payment include property tax and insurance?

The first number is principal and interest only, the part the loan fixes. With Taxes and costs switched on, Monthly with costs adds one twelfth of each yearly cost. That second figure is closer to your real bill when the lender collects tax and insurance through escrow.

When does mortgage insurance (PMI) stop?

This calculator stops PMI once the balance after a payment drops to 80% of the home price, when US law lets you ask for it to be cancelled if your payments are current. If you do not ask, it must end when the balance is scheduled to reach 78% of the original value, or after the midpoint of the term at the latest. FHA and VA loans follow different rules.

How do I calculate mortgage insurance?

PMI is usually quoted as a yearly percentage of the loan. Multiply the loan by that rate and divide by 12: 0.5% of $360,000 is $1,800 a year, or $150 a month. Freddie Mac puts typical costs at about $30 to $70 a month per $100,000 borrowed; enter your quote under Taxes and costs as a % of the loan or as an amount.

How do I use the mortgage calculator with extra payments?

Open Extra payments and add an amount every month, every year, once, or a list of one-time payments with their months. The results then show the total extra paid, the interest saved and how much sooner the loan ends. On the default loan, $200 more a month saves $105,428.67 and ends the loan 6 years 7 months early.

Why does paying every two weeks finish the loan sooner?

Half the monthly payment every two weeks makes 26 half payments a year, the same as 13 monthly payments instead of 12. The extra payment each year goes straight to the balance. On a $320,000 loan at 6.5% it ends the loan in about 24.1 years and saves $93,997.60 of interest.

How do I calculate a mortgage payment in Excel?

Use =PMT(rate/12, years*12, -loan), for example =PMT(6.5%/12, 360, -320000), which gives 2,022.62. Google Sheets uses the same function. Enter the loan as a negative number, or put a minus sign in front of PMT, to get a positive payment.

Can I use this as a home loan calculator for Pakistan?

Yes. Choose PKR, then enter the price, your down payment (at least 10% under State Bank rules, and your bank may ask for more), the tenure and the rate the bank quotes, often KIBOR plus a margin. Because such rates float, rerun the calculator at each reset with your remaining balance.

How do I calculate the mortgage interest deduction?

Add up the interest you paid in the year (your lender reports it on Form 1098, and the yearly schedule here gives an estimate) and deduct it on Schedule A if you itemize. The IRS limits it to interest on $750,000 of home acquisition debt, or $375,000 if married filing separately, for debt taken out after 15 December 2017. Your tax saving is roughly the deduction times your top tax rate, and only if itemizing beats the standard deduction.

Is a 15-year or 30-year mortgage better?

A 15-year loan has a higher payment but costs far less: at the 19 November 2026 averages, $320,000 costs $2,815.77 a month and $186,838.08 of interest over 15 years, against $2,189.48 and $468,212.55 over 30 years. The 30-year loan is more flexible because you can prepay when you can afford it. Choose the payment you can still make in a bad month.

Can I use it for a refinance, land or an investment property?

Yes. For a refinance, enter the current balance as the home price with 0% down, plus the new rate and term, and compare the payment with what you pay now. For land, a second home or a rental, enter the terms the lender offers; the maths is the same even when the rate and down payment differ.
