What this 401(k) calculator shows you

A 401(k) calculator estimates how much your workplace retirement account will hold on the day you retire, based on your pay, the share of it you save, your employer's match and the return your investments earn. This one also turns that balance into a monthly or yearly income, shows what cashing out early would really leave you, and finds the contribution rate that collects every dollar of the match.

A 401(k) is a retirement savings plan that US employers offer to their staff. It takes its name from subsection 401(k) of the Internal Revenue Code. You choose a percentage of each paycheck, the plan invests it in funds you pick from its menu, and the money grows without yearly tax on interest, dividends or gains. Many employers add matching money on top, which is why the match gets so much attention.

It is a defined contribution plan: what you get at retirement depends on what went in and how the investments did, not on a promise based on your years of service the way an old-style pension works. That is exactly why a projection helps. Small changes to the rate you save or the return you assume make a large difference over 30 or 40 years, and the calculator lets you see those differences in dollars.

How to use the 401(k) calculator, step by step

The page has three tabs. Fill in the boxes for the question you have; every result updates as you type.

  • My 401(k) at retirement: enter Your age and Retirement age, your Yearly pay before tax, the share of it You put in (a % of pay) and your 401(k) balance today. The defaults are age 30, retiring at 65, $75,000 of pay, 10% and $25,000 saved.
  • Describe the match: Employer matches (a % of what you put in) and On up to (a % of pay). A common formula, 50% of the first 6% of pay, is the default. If your plan has a second step, such as 100% of the first 3% then 50% of the next 2%, open Second match tier and fill in Then matches and Up to.
  • Open Assumptions to change Life expectancy (85), Yearly pay rise (3%), Yearly investment return (6%) and Yearly inflation (3%). Inflation also raises the IRS limits in later years and converts the results into today's money.
  • Under IRS limits, choose the tax year the projection starts in (2026, or 2027 once those limits apply). Tick Ignore the IRS limits only to see what your percentage would give if the law did not cap it.
  • Cost of taking it out early: enter the Amount you take out early, your Federal tax rate, State tax rate and City or local tax rate, then answer the questions about leaving your job at 55 or later, disability and other IRS exceptions.
  • Get the full match: enter your age, Yearly pay and the match formula. The tab returns the lowest and highest percentage of pay that collects the whole match without running into the IRS limit before December.

401(k) contribution limits for 2026 and 2027

The IRS caps how much of your pay you can defer into a 401(k) each year, and it raises the caps with inflation. The same deferral limit applies to 403(b) plans, most 457 plans and the federal Thrift Savings Plan. The calculator uses these figures automatically for the tax year you pick, and it grows them with your inflation rate for later years.

So the most a 45-year-old can defer in 2026 is $24,500; a 55-year-old can defer $32,500, and someone aged 60 to 63 can defer $35,750. The employer match does not use up your deferral limit, but your deferrals plus all employer money must stay under the total limit (catch-ups sit on top of it).

One 2026 change matters for higher earners. If your FICA wages from the same employer were above $150,000 in 2025, any catch-up contributions you make in 2026 must go in as Roth money, taxed now, rather than pre-tax. Your regular deferrals can still be pre-tax. If your plan has no Roth option, you may not be able to make catch-ups at all, so check with your plan.

Self-employed with no staff? A one-participant (solo) 401(k) lets a business owner, and a spouse who works in the business, contribute twice: once as the employee, up to the deferral limit, and once as the employer, up to 25% of compensation (a special calculation applies to sole proprietors). Both parts together stay within the same $72,000 total for 2026, plus catch-ups.

How 401(k) matching works

A match is money your employer adds in proportion to what you put in. It is written as two numbers: the rate it matches and the slice of pay it applies to. To calculate a 401(k) matching contribution, take the smaller of your contribution rate and the match limit, multiply it by your pay, then by the match rate. With $75,000 of pay and a match of 50% on up to 6%, saving 6% or more brings in 75,000 × 6% × 50% = $2,250 a year. Saving only 4% brings in 75,000 × 4% × 50% = $1,500, so $750 of free money is left behind.

The third row is the basic safe harbor formula in the IRS rules. Plans that use it skip some yearly fairness tests, and the IRS requires those safe harbor matches to be fully yours (100% vested) from day one.

Other plans use a vesting schedule, which decides how much of the employer's money you keep if you leave. Your own deferrals are always 100% yours. Under a cliff schedule, the match becomes fully yours after 3 years of service and none of it is yours before that. Under a graded schedule you might own 20% after 2 years, 40% after 3, and so on until 100% after 6 years. If you are close to a vesting date, leaving a few weeks later can be worth thousands of dollars.

The Get the full match tab adds one more check. If you save a very high percentage, you can hit the yearly IRS limit in, say, September. When the employer matches each paycheck separately, the last paychecks of the year then get no match. The tab gives the highest rate that avoids this; plans with a true-up at year end fix it for you, so ask your plan which kind it has.

Worked 401(k) examples

Starting early, with the defaults. A 30-year-old earning $75,000 saves 10% with a 50% match on the first 6% and $25,000 already in the plan. In the first year she adds $7,500, the employer adds $2,250 and the account earns 25,000 × 6% on the old balance plus 9,750 × 3% on the new deposits (half a year, since they arrive through the year), which is $1,792.50. By 65 the balance is $1,823,129, worth $647,910 in today's prices. Of that, $614,505 went in and $1,208,624 is growth.

Catching up in your fifties. A 55-year-old earning $140,000 wants to save 25% of pay ($35,000) for 10 years, with $400,000 saved and a 100% match on the first 5%. The 2026 limit for someone 50 or over is $32,500, so the calculator cuts her deferral in 6 of the 10 years. In the years she is 60 to 63 the higher catch-up lifts her limit to $35,750 and the cut disappears. She retires at 65 with $1,336,899, or $994,778 in today's money.

Collecting a two-step match. A 40-year-old earning $60,000 has a plan that matches 100% of the first 3% of pay and 50% of the next 2%. On the Get the full match tab, the answer is to save between 5% and 40.83% of pay. At 5% she puts in $3,000 and the employer adds $2,400, so $5,400 a year goes in for an outlay of $3,000.

Cashing out early. Taking $10,000 out at 40 while still working there, with a 22% federal rate and a 5% state rate, costs $2,200 in federal tax, $500 in state tax and a $1,000 penalty. Only $6,300 is left, 63% of the amount. If the same person had left that job in the year they turned 55 or later, the penalty would not apply and $7,300 would remain.

Traditional 401(k) or Roth 401(k)?

Many plans now offer two kinds of account. In a traditional 401(k), the money goes in before income tax, which lowers your tax bill this year, and every withdrawal in retirement is taxed as ordinary income. In a Roth 401(k), the money goes in after tax, so there is no saving today, but qualified withdrawals, growth included, are tax-free.

A Roth withdrawal is qualified when your first Roth contribution to the plan was at least five tax years earlier and you are 59½ or older, disabled, or the payment goes to your beneficiary after your death. You can split your savings between the two in any proportion, but both count toward the same yearly deferral limit of $24,500 in 2026. Employer matching money usually goes into the pre-tax side, so it is taxed when you take it out.

Which is better depends mainly on whether your tax rate will be higher now or in retirement. If you are early in your career and in a low bracket, paying tax now through the Roth often wins. If you are in your peak earning years and expect a smaller income later, the up-front deduction of the traditional account usually wins. Many people split the difference to keep both options open.

The calculator treats both kinds the same way: it projects the balance before any tax on withdrawals. For a traditional account, remember that part of every payout will go to income tax; for a qualified Roth account, the figures are what you keep.

Taking money out: early withdrawals, loans, rollovers and RMDs

The plan is built for retirement, so the tax code makes early access expensive. Before age 59½, a withdrawal is normally taxed as income and charged a 10% additional tax. The IRS lists exceptions that remove the 10% (but not the income tax), including leaving your job in or after the year you turn 55 (50 for public safety workers in a government plan), total and permanent disability, a series of substantially equal payments, medical bills above 7.5% of your income, a payment to an ex-spouse under a court order, up to $5,000 per child for a birth or adoption, one personal emergency payment of up to $1,000 a year and up to $22,000 after a federally declared disaster.

Some plans also allow hardship withdrawals for an immediate and heavy need. The IRS safe harbor list covers medical care, buying your main home, the next 12 months of college costs, preventing eviction or foreclosure, funeral costs and certain repairs to your home. A hardship withdrawal is still taxed, and the 10% penalty may apply unless one of the exceptions above also fits.

A 401(k) loan is often the cheaper route, if your plan offers one. You can usually borrow the lesser of $50,000 or 50% of your vested balance (some plans allow up to $10,000 even when half the balance is less). You repay it with interest, at least every quarter, within 5 years unless the loan is for your main home. If you leave your job or miss payments, the unpaid balance can be treated as a taxable distribution.

When you change jobs you can leave the money in the old plan if it allows, move it into the new employer's plan, roll it into an IRA or cash it out. A direct rollover from plan to plan or plan to IRA has no tax withheld. If the plan pays you instead, it must withhold 20% for federal tax, and you have 60 days to put the full amount, including the withheld part, into the new account to avoid tax on it.

In retirement you can take a lump sum, regular payments or an annuity, and you can also leave the money growing. You must start required minimum distributions (RMDs) at 73. If you are still working and your plan allows it, you can wait until April 1 of the year after you retire. Missing an RMD triggers a 25% excise tax on the shortfall, reduced to 10% if you correct it within 2 years. Designated Roth 401(k) accounts no longer need RMDs while the owner is alive.

How to find a 401(k) from an old job

Lost accounts are common because people change jobs many times. Start with the plan's own paperwork: old statements, the summary plan description or a W-2 from that employer, which shows retirement deferrals in box 12. The plan administrator or record keeper named there can confirm whether money is still in your name.

If you cannot find the papers, use the US Department of Labor's Retirement Savings Lost and Found database at lostandfound.dol.gov. It was set up under the SECURE 2.0 Act, is free, and after you verify your identity through Login.gov it lists private-sector plans that reported you as a participant, with contact details for each administrator. It does not cover IRAs, government plans or Social Security, and a listing only means you took part at some point; the administrator tells you whether a balance is still owed.

Once you find an old account, compare its fees and funds with your current plan and with an IRA before you decide where it should live. Use a direct rollover so nothing is withheld.

Common 401(k) mistakes and related tools

  • Saving below the match limit. The match is an instant return of 50% or 100% on that slice of your pay, which no ordinary investment offers.
  • Treating one projection as a promise. Returns vary from year to year; check a lower and a higher return, and look at the figure in today's money, not only the future dollar amount.
  • Forgetting fees. A plan or fund fee of 1% a year comes straight off your return, so enter a return net of fees.
  • Cashing out when you change jobs. On a $10,000 balance, tax and the penalty can take over a third; a rollover keeps it all working.
  • Ignoring the catch-ups. From 50, and especially from 60 to 63, you can put in thousands more each year; the calculator applies them by age automatically.
  • Skipping the beneficiary form. The plan pays whoever is named on it, so update it after a marriage, divorce or birth.

As a rough check on progress, Fidelity's published guideline is to have about 1 times your salary saved by 30, 3 times by 40, 6 times by 50, 8 times by 60 and 10 times by 67, counting all retirement savings. It is a rule of thumb, not a requirement, and it assumes saving about 15% of pay a year from age 25.

How it works

The first tab follows your 401(k) year by year until you retire. Each year your pay rises, you put in your percentage of it and your employer adds its match. Your own part is capped at the IRS limit for your age (with the catch-up from 50, and the higher catch-up at 60 to 63), pay above the IRS pay limit is not counted, and you and your employer together stay under the total additions limit; the limits grow with inflation in later years. Deposits come in through the year, so they earn half a year of return at first. At retirement the page shows three ways to spend the balance by your life expectancy. The second tab shows what an early withdrawal leaves after income tax and the 10% penalty, unless an IRS exception applies. The third tab finds the range of contribution rates that collects the whole match without reaching the IRS limit before the year ends.

The formula

  • Your deposit = your % × pay (pay capped at the 401(a)(17) limit), at most the 402(g) limit + the catch-up for your age
  • Match = pay × (tier-1 % × the part of your % up to its limit + tier-2 % × the part between the two limits)
  • Yearly growth = balance × R + (your deposit + match) × R ÷ 2; balance ← balance + deposits + growth
  • Same yearly amount: B × R ÷ (1 − (1 + R)^−Y); same monthly amount: B × (R/12) ÷ (1 − (1 + R/12)^−12Y); rising monthly amount: the same with (R − inflation) ÷ 12
  • Early withdrawal: you receive = amount − amount × (federal + state + local rate) − 10% × the part no exception covers

A worked example

At 30 on $75,000 with $25,000 already saved, putting in 10% with a 50% match on the first 6% of pay: the first year you add $7,500 and your employer $2,250. At 6% a year, that year grows by 25,000 × 6% + 9,750 × 3% = $1,792.50, so the balance ends the year at $36,542.50. With 3% pay rises the balance reaches $1,823,129 at 65 ($647,910 in today's money), which pays $158,949 a year until 85.

Questions

How do I calculate my 401(k) at retirement?

Each year, add your contribution and the employer match to the balance and grow it by your expected return, then repeat until you retire. This calculator does that for every year, raises your pay, applies the IRS limits for your age and shows the result in future and today's dollars.

How much should you have in your 401(k) at 30?

A common guideline from Fidelity is about one year's salary saved by 30, counting all retirement accounts. It is only a rule of thumb; what matters more is saving at least enough to get the full match and raising your rate as your pay grows.

What is the 401(k) max for 2026?

For 2026 you can defer $24,500 of your pay, plus an $8,000 catch-up from age 50, or $11,250 instead at ages 60 to 63. For 2027 the figures are $25,500, $8,500 and $11,750. You and your employer together are capped at $72,000 in 2026 and $75,000 in 2027, not counting catch-ups.

How do I calculate 401(k) matching contributions?

Multiply your pay by the smaller of your contribution rate and the match limit, then by the match rate. On $75,000 with a 50% match up to 6%, saving 6% or more brings $2,250 a year. The third tab finds the lowest rate that gets all of it.

What happens to my employer match if I leave before I am vested?

You keep all of your own deferrals, but you lose the part of the employer's money that has not vested yet. With a 3-year cliff schedule you keep none of the match before 3 years of service; with a graded schedule you keep a growing share each year.

How much tax do I pay on a 401(k) withdrawal?

A traditional 401(k) withdrawal is taxed as ordinary income at your federal and state rates, plus a 10% penalty before 59½ unless an exception applies. With 22% federal and 5% state tax, a $10,000 early withdrawal leaves about $6,300. The second tab works it out for your rates.

What is the rule of 55 for a 401(k)?

If you leave your job in or after the year you turn 55, withdrawals from that employer's plan avoid the 10% penalty; for public safety workers in a government plan the age is 50. Income tax is still due, and the rule does not cover IRAs or plans from earlier jobs.

How do I find a 401(k) from an old job?

Look for old statements or W-2s from that employer and contact the plan administrator named there. If you have nothing, search the free Department of Labor Retirement Savings Lost and Found database, which lists plans that reported you as a participant.

Is a Roth 401(k) better than a traditional 401(k)?

A Roth 401(k) suits you if you expect a higher tax rate in retirement than today, because qualified withdrawals are tax-free. A traditional 401(k) suits you if your tax rate is high now and likely lower later. Both share the same yearly limit, and many people use both.

Can I borrow from my 401(k)?

If your plan allows loans, you can usually borrow up to the lesser of $50,000 or half your vested balance and repay it within 5 years, longer for a home purchase. Leaving your job with a loan outstanding can turn the unpaid part into a taxable distribution.

Why is today's balance not in my contributions?

It is money you already saved, so it is shown on its own line. The total money in adds it to your future contributions and the employer match; everything above that is investment growth.

Will the 401(k) limits really rise every year?

The IRS raises them with inflation, usually in $500 steps, as it did from $24,500 for 2026 to $25,500 for 2027. The projection grows them with the inflation you enter, which is close to what happens over time.
