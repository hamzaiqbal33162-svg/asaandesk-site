What wind chill means

Wind chill is how cold the air feels on bare skin once the wind is taken into account. Your body warms a thin layer of air next to your skin; wind keeps blowing it away, so skin loses heat faster and feels colder. This wind chill calculator turns a wind speed and an air temperature into the "feels like" temperature that would cool skin just as fast in calm air.

The wind chill is not the real temperature of anything. A parked car or a water pipe never gets colder than the air, however hard the wind blows; it just cools down to the air temperature faster. Wind chill matters for people and animals, because it sets how quickly frostbite and hypothermia can happen. Treat it as a safety estimate, not medical advice.

How to use the wind chill calculator

  • Wind speed: the forecast wind, measured at about 10 m height as weather reports give it, in km/h, mph, m/s or knots.
  • Air temperature: in °C, °F or K. The index is defined from −50°F to 50°F (about −45.6°C to 10°C).
  • Read the results: the wind chill in your unit and the other two, the heat loss of bare skin in watts per square metre, the time to frostbite when there is a risk, and the risk level with advice.
  • The banded scale shows where the result sits on the Environment and Climate Change Canada levels, from no risk to extreme risk.
  • At 3 mph (about 5 km/h) or less the wind chill equals the air temperature, because the index is not defined for such light wind.

How to calculate wind chill: the formula

The wind chill formula in Fahrenheit is WC = 35.74 + 0.6215T − 35.75V^0.16 + 0.4275T × V^0.16, with T the air temperature in °F and V the wind speed in mph. The metric version used in Canada is WC = 13.12 + 0.6215T − 11.37V^0.16 + 0.3965T × V^0.16, with T in °C and V in km/h; both give almost the same answer.

Worked example in Celsius: −12°C with a 15 km/h wind. The calculator converts to 10.4°F and 9.32 mph, finds V^0.16 = 1.4293, and gets a wind chill of −2.5°F, which is −19.2°C. The risk is moderate, with frostbite taking more than 30 minutes, and bare skin loses about 1,396 W/m².

In Fahrenheit: 25°F with a 30 mph wind feels like 8°F (−13°C), also a moderate risk. In a spreadsheet, put T in A1 and V in B1 and enter =35.74+0.6215*A1-35.75*B1^0.16+0.4275*A1*B1^0.16.

Where the formula comes from

The idea goes back to Paul Siple and Charles Passel, who measured in Antarctica how fast a plastic bottle of water froze in different winds. Their heat-loss formula is still shown here as the heat loss of exposed skin, in watts per square metre.

In November 2001 Canada, the United States and the United Kingdom adopted a new index from the Joint Action Group for Temperature Indices (JAG/TI). It models the skin of a bare face of a person walking into the wind at about 1.4 m/s (5 km/h), corrects the 10 m wind to face height in an open field, and gives less extreme, more realistic values than the old tables. It was designed for cold, fairly dry air and does not include humidity or sunshine.

Motorcycles, cold mountains and other cases

  • Motorcycle riding: the air you feel is your riding speed plus any head wind, so enter that speed as the wind. At 5°C and 100 km/h the calculator gives a wind chill of −3°C. That is beyond the 60 mph range of the NWS chart, so treat it as a rough guide and wear windproof gear, gloves and a full-face helmet.
  • Cold mountains: suppose a winter morning in Skardu or another high valley in northern Pakistan reads −25°C with a 40 km/h wind. The wind chill is −41°C, a very high risk, with frostbite on bare skin in 5 to 10 minutes.
  • Above 10°C (50°F): there is no wind chill; a breeze then feels pleasant rather than dangerous, and in hot, humid weather the Heat Index Calculator applies instead.
  • Children, older people and pets lose heat faster or cannot say they are cold, so bring them in earlier than adults would need.
  • Wet skin and clothes lose heat much faster than dry ones, so the real risk is higher in rain, sleet or after sweating.

How it works

Enter the wind speed and the air temperature, each in the unit you have. Wind blows away the thin layer of warm air next to your skin, so you lose heat faster and feel colder than the thermometer says. The wind chill is the calm-air temperature that would cool bare skin just as fast. It uses the index the US National Weather Service and the Meteorological Service of Canada adopted in 2001, which holds for air from −50°F to 50°F (about −45°C to 10°C) and wind above 3 mph. The risk level and frostbite time follow the wind chill levels of Environment and Climate Change Canada. The heat loss of bare skin uses the older Siple and Passel formula.

The formula

  • Wind chill (°F) = 35.74 + 0.6215T − 35.75V^0.16 + 0.4275T·V^0.16, with T in °F and V in mph (wind at 10 m height)
  • Metric form used in Canada (almost the same result): 13.12 + 0.6215T − 11.37V^0.16 + 0.3965T·V^0.16, with T in °C and V in km/h
  • At 3 mph or less there is no wind chill: it equals the air temperature
  • Heat loss of bare skin (Siple and Passel) = (10.45 + 10√v − v) × (33 − T) kcal/m²/h, v in m/s, T in °C; × 4.184 ÷ 3.6 for W/m²
  • Frostbite on exposed skin (Environment Canada): −28 to −39°C wind chill: 10 to 30 minutes; −40 to −47: 5 to 10; −48 to −54: 2 to 5; −55 and colder: under 2 minutes

A worked example

At 10°F with a 15 mph wind: V^0.16 = 15^0.16 = 1.5423, so wind chill = 35.74 + 0.6215 × 10 − 35.75 × 1.5423 + 0.4275 × 10 × 1.5423 = −6.6°F, about −7°F (−21°C). Bare skin loses about 1,558 W/m², and frostbite takes more than 30 minutes.

Questions

What is wind chill?

Wind chill is how cold it feels on bare skin when wind is added to the air temperature. Wind carries away the warm layer of air next to your skin, so you lose heat faster; the wind chill is the calm-air temperature that would feel the same.

How do I calculate wind chill?

Use WC = 35.74 + 0.6215T − 35.75V^0.16 + 0.4275T·V^0.16 with T in °F and V in mph, or enter the wind and temperature here in any units. At −12°C with a 15 km/h wind it feels like about −19°C.

How do I calculate wind chill in Celsius?

Use the metric formula 13.12 + 0.6215T − 11.37V^0.16 + 0.3965T·V^0.16 with T in °C and V in km/h, or pick °C and km/h in the calculator. At 0°C and 30 km/h the wind chill is about −6°C.

Is wind chill the real temperature?

No. Wind chill only describes how fast bare skin loses heat. A car, a water pipe or a bottle of water will not get colder than the air temperature, however hard the wind blows; they only cool down to it faster.

Why is there no wind chill above 10°C (50°F)?

The index was built for cold weather, where frostbite and hypothermia are the danger. In mild or warm weather a breeze feels pleasant rather than dangerous, and in hot humid weather the heat index matters instead.

Does humidity change the wind chill?

The North American index does not include humidity; it was designed for cold, fairly dry air. Wet skin or damp clothes do make you lose heat faster, so the real risk is higher in wet weather than the number shows.

How do I work out wind chill on a motorcycle?

Enter your riding speed, plus any head wind, as the wind speed. At 5°C and 100 km/h the wind chill is about −3°C. Speeds above 60 mph (97 km/h) are beyond the official chart, so take the result as a rough guide.

Who created the wind chill formula?

Paul Siple and Charles Passel made the first wind chill measurements in Antarctica. The index used today was developed by the Joint Action Group for Temperature Indices and adopted by Canada, the US and the UK in 2001.

How fast can frostbite happen?

Below a wind chill of about −28°C (−18°F), bare skin can freeze in 10 to 30 minutes; below −40°C (−40°F) in 5 to 10 minutes; below −55°C (−67°F) in under 2 minutes. Strong winds over 50 km/h can make it faster. Numb, white or waxy skin is a warning sign.

How should I dress for a low wind chill?

Wear several layers: a thin layer that pulls sweat off the skin, a warm middle layer such as fleece or wool, and a windproof outer layer. Cover your head, ears, face and hands, keep dry, and come indoors to warm up regularly.
