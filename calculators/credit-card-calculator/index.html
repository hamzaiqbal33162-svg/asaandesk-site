What this credit card calculator shows

This credit card calculator answers the two questions people ask about card debt: how long will my monthly payment take to clear the balance, and how much do I need to pay each month to be debt-free by a certain date? Either way it shows the total interest, the total you will pay, and how much interest the card adds in the first month.

A credit card lets you borrow up to a limit and repay as much or as little as you like each month, as long as you cover the minimum. Whatever you leave unpaid after the due date is charged interest at the card's APR, which is usually far higher than the rate on a personal loan or car loan. That makes a card balance one of the most expensive debts most households carry, and also one where a slightly higher payment saves the most.

Below the result, a comparison table runs the same balance through six other ways of paying, including the minimum payment that shrinks as the balance falls. Seeing those plans side by side is often the quickest way to choose a payment you can live with.

How to use the credit card calculator

  • Currency: pick yours at the top. Only the symbol changes, so rupees, dollars, pounds or any other currency work the same way.
  • Card balance: what you owe today, from your latest statement or app. The default is 6,000.
  • Interest rate (APR): the yearly purchase rate on the card, 22% by default. If your card shows a monthly rate instead, multiply it by 12 (3% a month is 36% a year).
  • How do you want to pay it off?: choose A set amount each month to see the payoff time, or Within a set time to see the payment you need.
  • Monthly payment (set amount only): choose An amount I choose and type it in Amount each month (250 by default), or let a quick pick fill it: Interest + 1% of the balance, or 2%, 3%, 4% or 5% of the balance. Each quick pick is worked out once from today's balance and then kept the same.
  • Pay it off within (set time only): the years and months you want the card cleared in, 2 years by default.

If the payment you choose is no more than one month's interest, the calculator stops and tells you the smallest payment that would start to reduce the balance. It assumes you make no new purchases while paying off, and that the rate stays the same.

Where each payment goes: the first six months

A card payoff works like a loan amortization schedule. Each month the card first takes its interest, and only the rest of your payment reduces the balance. Because the balance is a little smaller every month, the interest shrinks and more of the same payment goes to the debt.

How credit card interest is calculated

Card interest is quoted as a yearly APR but charged in small slices. The CFPB explains that many issuers work out interest every day using a daily periodic rate, which is the APR divided by 365 (some use 360), applied to the average daily balance of the billing cycle. This calculator adds interest once a month at APR ÷ 12, which gives almost the same answer and keeps the maths clear.

Here is the daily method with simple numbers. Say your card has a 21.9% APR, so the daily rate is 21.9% ÷ 365 = 0.06%. In a 30-day cycle you owe 1,200 for the first 10 days, then a purchase takes the balance to 2,000 for the last 20 days. The average daily balance is (1,200 × 10 + 2,000 × 20) ÷ 30 = 1,733.33, and the interest is 1,733.33 × 0.06% × 30 = 31.20. The monthly method used here gives 31.63 on the same balance, so it errs slightly on the side of caution.

Interest is added to what you owe, so next month you pay interest on this month's interest too. That compounding is why a balance left to sit grows faster than the APR alone suggests.

Not sure of your rate? Your statement lists the APR for each type of balance, usually in a box headed interest charge calculation. You can also estimate it yourself: divide the interest charged in a month by the balance it was charged on, then multiply by 12. If a 2,500 balance was charged 45.83 of interest, that is 45.83 ÷ 2,500 × 12 = 22%.

  • Grace period: the CFPB describes it as the time between the end of a billing cycle and the due date. If you pay the full statement balance by the due date, purchases are generally not charged interest at all.
  • Losing the grace period: pay in full some months but not others and you can lose it for the unpaid month and the month after, so new purchases start costing interest straight away.
  • Cash advances: the grace period usually applies only to purchases. Interest on a cash withdrawal generally starts on the day you take it, often at a higher APR and with a fee.
  • Statement timing: US issuers must send your bill at least 21 days before the payment is due.

The minimum payment trap

Many cards set the minimum as the month's interest plus a small share of the balance. This calculator models it as interest plus 1% of what you owe, with a floor of 15, and works it out again every month. As the balance falls the minimum falls too, so almost all of each payment keeps going to interest and the debt drags on for decades. Check your card agreement for its own formula, since issuers differ.

On the default 6,000 at 22%, the first minimum is 170.00. If you keep paying exactly the minimum, the card takes 24 years 11 months to clear and costs 10,359.87 in interest, more than the original balance. If you simply keep paying 170.00 every month even as the bill asks for less, it is gone in 4 years 10 months for 3,745.37. Same starting payment, about 20 years and 6,600 less.

A payment that only matches the interest never clears anything. At 22%, 6,000 adds 110.00 of interest in the first month, so a payment of 110.00 is refused, and 115.00 a month would take 14 years 5 months and cost 13,847.85. A plan based on 2% of the balance can fall into the same hole: on a 4,500 balance at 24.99%, 2% is 90.00 while the interest is 93.71, so the table shows it is never paid off.

Under the US Credit CARD Act of 2009, statements must show how long the balance would take to clear with minimum payments only, and the payment needed to clear it in 36 months with its total cost. For the default card, the 36-month figure from the Within a set time option is 229.14 a month and 2,249.14 of interest.

A useful rule of thumb from these numbers: once your payment is more than double the first month's interest, the payoff time falls below about five years. Each step up from there buys a shorter payoff for a smaller extra amount.

The same sums work in a spreadsheet. In Excel or Google Sheets, =NPER(22%/12, -250, 6000) returns 31.92 months for the default card, and =PMT(22%/12, 24, -6000) returns the 311.27 payment that clears it in two years. The payment is typed as a negative number because it is money leaving your account.

Is it better to pay the card down or keep the cash in savings? Every unit of debt you clear stops costing the card's APR, so paying down a 22% balance works like a guaranteed 22% return, far above what a savings account or fixed deposit pays. Keep a small emergency fund so you do not have to borrow on the card again, and send the rest to the balance.

Worked examples in dollars, pounds and rupees

  • United States: 4,500 owed at 24.99% APR, paying 180 a month. The first month's interest is 93.71, the card is clear in exactly 3 years (36 payments), and the interest totals 1,921.24, so you pay 6,421.24 in all.
  • United Kingdom: £3,000 at 24.9% APR, paying £150 a month. The first month adds £62.25 of interest; the balance is gone in 2 years 3 months (27 payments) with £915.81 of interest. Paying only the shrinking minimum instead would take 19 years 8 months and cost £5,545.43.
  • Canada: C$5,000 at 20.99% APR, paying C$200 a month. Interest in the first month is C$87.46; the card is clear in 2 years 10 months (34 payments) with C$1,631.82 of interest. A fixed 2% of the balance (C$100) would stretch it to 10 years and C$6,972.66 of interest.
  • Pakistan: Rs 250,000 on a card charging 3% a month, which you type as a 36% APR, paying Rs 15,000 a month. The first month's interest is Rs 7,500.00. The card is cleared in 2 years (24 payments) with Rs 101,746.58 of interest. To clear it in 1 year you would need Rs 25,115.52 a month, which cuts the interest to Rs 51,386.26.
  • The Pakistan example also shows why percentage plans can fail at high rates: at 36%, 2% or 3% of the balance (Rs 5,000 or Rs 7,500) never covers the interest, so the table marks both as never paid off.

Many banks also offer to turn a card balance into fixed monthly instalments at a set rate. Enter the balance, the instalment plan's yearly rate and its length in the Within a set time option to see the monthly instalment and compare the total interest with what your card would charge.

If no payment you can afford clears the card in a reasonable time, talk to the card issuer before you fall behind. Ask whether it offers a lower rate, a fixed repayment plan or a pause on fees, and get any agreement in writing. Missing payments usually adds late fees and can trigger a higher penalty rate, which makes the numbers above worse.

APR types, fees, credit limits and your rights

One card can carry several rates. The purchase APR applies to everyday spending; the cash advance APR, usually higher, applies to cash withdrawals; a balance transfer APR applies to debt moved from another card; and a penalty APR can replace the others after a late or returned payment. Introductory rates, often 0%, last for a set period and then jump to the standard rate. Use the rate that applies to most of your balance, or run the calculator once per balance.

How high are card rates? In the Federal Reserve's G.19 release of 6 November 2026, the average rate on US bank credit card plans in the third quarter of 2026 was 21.19% across all accounts and 22.36% on accounts that were charged interest. Rates change with the market, so check your own statement.

A 0% balance transfer can save a lot if you clear the debt before the offer ends. Moving the default 6,000 to a 0% card with a 3% fee makes the balance 6,180, which clears in 18 months at 343.33 a month with no interest. Staying at 22% and clearing it in the same 18 months costs 394.37 a month and 1,098.69 of interest, so the transfer saves 918.69 after the 180 fee.

  • US issuers must give 45 days' notice before raising your rate, and an introductory rate must last at least six months (Credit CARD Act of 2009).
  • Payments above the minimum must go to the balance with the highest APR first, so extra payments attack the most expensive debt.
  • Fees in the first year of a new account are limited to 25% of the credit limit.
  • Applicants under 21 need independent income or a co-signer.

Your credit utilization is the share of your available credit that you are using: balance ÷ credit limit × 100. A 6,000 balance on a 20,000 limit is 30% utilization; the same balance on a 10,000 limit is 60%. With several cards, add up all balances and all limits.

Utilization matters because credit scores look at it. The CFPB notes that many experts recommend using less than 30% of your total credit limit, and that people with the highest scores often use less than 10%. Paying the card down therefore helps twice: it cuts interest and lowers your utilization.

Card limits are set by each bank from your income, your other debts and your repayment history, and there is no single formula from salary to limit. A higher limit is only useful if the balance stays low; it does not change the interest you pay on what you owe.

Tips to pay off a credit card faster

  • Fix your payment at today's amount and keep paying it even when the statement asks for less.
  • Stop adding new purchases to the card you are paying off; use a debit card or cash meanwhile.
  • Pay the full statement balance on any card you still use, so purchases stay inside the grace period.
  • If you have several cards, put every spare amount on the highest APR first while paying the minimum on the rest. This order, often called the avalanche method, costs the least interest; the snowball method clears the smallest balance first instead, which costs a little more but gives quick wins that keep some people motivated.
  • Recheck your plan whenever your rate, balance or income changes: run the calculator again with the new numbers rather than relying on an old payoff date.
  • Consider a 0% balance transfer or a lower-rate personal loan, but only with a plan that clears the balance before the offer ends.
  • Set up an automatic payment a few days before the due date to avoid late fees and a penalty APR.

How it works

Type what you owe on the card and its yearly interest rate (APR), then choose how you want to pay. With a set amount each month, the calculator counts the months until the balance reaches zero and adds up the interest. With a set time, it finds the one monthly payment that clears the card by then. A quick pick can fill the payment for you (interest plus 1% of the balance, or 2% to 5% of it), and a table shows how each of those plans, and paying only the shrinking minimum, would turn out.

The formula

  • Monthly rate r = APR ÷ 100 ÷ 12
  • Months to pay off n = −ln(1 − B × r ÷ P) ÷ ln(1 + r), shown rounded up to whole months (B = balance, P = payment)
  • Payment for a set time of n months: P = B × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n)
  • Total interest = P × n − B
  • The payment must be more than the first month's interest, B × r, or the balance never goes down

A worked example

A $6,000 balance at 22% has a monthly rate of 22 ÷ 12 = 1.8333%, so the first month adds $110 of interest. Paying $250 a month gives n = −ln(1 − 6,000 × 0.018333 ÷ 250) ÷ ln(1.018333) ≈ 31.92, so the card is clear in 32 months (2 years 8 months), and the interest is about 250 × 31.92 − 6,000 = $1,978.87.

Questions

How do I calculate credit card interest per month?

Divide the APR by 12 and multiply by the balance. A 6,000 balance at 22% adds 6,000 × 22% ÷ 12 = 110 of interest in a month. Cards that charge daily use APR ÷ 365 on the average daily balance, which comes to almost the same amount.

How do I calculate my credit card payment with interest?

Use P = B × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), where B is the balance, r is the APR ÷ 12 and n is the number of months. For 6,000 at 22% over 24 months that is 311.27 a month. Choose Within a set time and the calculator does it for you.

How is the credit card minimum payment calculated?

Each issuer has its own formula, but a common one is the month's interest and fees plus about 1% of the balance, with a fixed floor. This calculator uses interest plus 1%, at least 15, and shows how long paying only that would take. Your card agreement gives the exact rule.

Why does paying only the minimum take so long?

Because the minimum falls as the balance falls, most of each payment keeps going to interest. On 6,000 at 22%, minimum-only payments take almost 25 years. Keeping your payment at today's amount, even when the bill asks for less, cuts that to under 5 years.

What if my payment is smaller than the interest?

Then the card adds more interest each month than you pay, and the balance never goes down. The calculator refuses such a payment and tells you the smallest amount that starts to reduce the balance.

How long will it take to pay off my credit card?

Enter the balance, APR and the amount you pay each month, and the result gives the time in years and months. At 22%, a 6,000 balance takes 2 years 8 months at 250 a month and 1 year 2 months at 500 a month.

Does the calculator count new purchases on the card?

No. It assumes you stop using the card while you pay it off. New purchases add to the balance and make the payoff longer, so run it again with the new balance if you keep spending.

How do I use it as a credit card interest calculator in Pakistan?

Pick PKR as the currency and type the card's yearly mark-up rate. If the bank quotes a monthly rate, multiply it by 12, so 3% a month becomes 36%. Then enter your balance and payment as usual.

How do I calculate credit card utilization?

Divide your balance by your credit limit and multiply by 100. A 3,000 balance on a 10,000 limit is 30%. The CFPB notes that many experts recommend staying under 30%.

How is a credit card limit decided from salary?

There is no fixed formula. Each bank looks at your income, existing debts and repayment record and sets its own limit, which it may raise or lower later. What matters for interest is the balance you carry, not the size of the limit.

What is the difference between a credit card and a debit card?

A debit card spends money already in your bank account, so there is no interest. A credit card spends the bank's money up to a limit, and you are charged interest on anything not repaid in full by the due date.

Is the result exact for my card?

It is a close estimate. It adds interest once a month at the yearly rate divided by 12; many cards charge interest daily and some add fees, which makes the real cost a little higher.
