What this Social Security calculator does

This Social Security calculator helps with the biggest decision most Americans make about their retirement benefit: at what age to claim it. You can start your retirement benefit at any age from 62 to 70. Claim early and you get a smaller check for more years; wait and you get a larger check for fewer years. The calculator weighs the two for your birth year and the age you expect to live to, and names the claiming age that gives the most value in today's money.

The second tab answers the break-even question. Type two claiming ages and the monthly benefit at each, taken from your my Social Security statement, and it finds how long you would need to live for the later, larger benefit to catch up with the earlier one.

The first tab needs no benefit amount at all, because the early reduction and the delayed credits are percentages of your full benefit. If you add your full benefit from your SSA statement, the table also shows the dollar amount at each age. The results are a money comparison only and an estimate, not advice; spouse and survivor benefits, work before full retirement age and taxes can change the best choice.

How to use the Social Security calculator

  • Best age to claim tab: type Your birth year (1966 by default; the rules here apply to people born in 1943 or later) and the Age you expect to live to (85 by default).
  • Compare two ages tab: type a first claiming age and its Monthly benefit at that age, then a later age and its benefit. Use the amounts from your SSA statement, in today's dollars.
  • Yearly return you could earn instead: the return on money you would save or invest, 4% by default. It values money received sooner more than money received later.
  • Under Assumptions: Yearly cost-of-living rise (COLA). Leave it blank to use SSA's figure for the year chosen (2.8% for 2026, 3.6% for 2027), or type your own long-run guess.
  • SSA figures for: 2026 or 2027. This sets the COLA used when the box is blank and the taxable maximum and credit amounts quoted in the notes.
  • Your monthly benefit at full retirement age (optional): from your statement, to turn the percentages into dollars.

If you do not know what age to expect, try a few. The answer often changes between about 78 and 88, and seeing that range is more useful than a single number.

Switching SSA figures for to 2027 changes the default COLA to 3.6% and the notes to the 2027 taxable maximum ($192,300) and credit amount ($1,970). The claiming rules themselves are set by law and are the same in both years.

The Every claiming age compared table lists each age from 62 to 70 with its share of the full benefit and the value today of all the payments, per $1,000 of full benefit (or in dollars if you entered yours). For the default case the values run from $167,522 at 62 to a peak of $185,148 at 69, and the bar chart shows the same shape. Ages close to the peak are nearly as good, so the choice between 68, 69 and 70 matters much less than the choice between 62 and 69.

Value today means present value: each future check is shrunk by the return you could have earned on money received sooner, so a dollar at 80 counts for less than a dollar at 63. It is not the cash you would collect in total, which is higher. Comparing present values puts an early claim and a late claim on the same footing, the way an investor compares two streams of income.

On the Compare two ages tab, the headline is the break-even age, with the value today of each option if you live exactly that long. The line chart draws both options for every age at death, so you can see how much is at stake if you live a few years more or less.

Full retirement age by birth year

Your full retirement age (FRA), also called normal retirement age, is the age at which you get 100% of your benefit, your primary insurance amount. It was 65 for people born in 1937 or earlier and rose in steps under the 1983 Social Security amendments. For anyone born in 1960 or later it is 67. If you were born on 1 January, SSA treats you as born in the previous year.

Claiming early or late: how much each age pays

Claim before your full retirement age and the benefit is cut for life: by 5/9 of 1% for each of the first 36 months early and 5/12 of 1% for each month beyond that. Claim after it and you earn delayed retirement credits of 2/3 of 1% a month, which is 8% a year, until age 70. Waiting past 70 adds nothing more.

Claiming at 70 instead of 67 means 24% more every month, in return for three fewer years. For someone born in 1958, whose full retirement age is 66 and 8 months, claiming at 70 pays 126.67%.

Money is only part of the choice. Claiming early can make sense if your health or family history points to a shorter life, if you have no other savings to live on, or if you have stopped working and need the income now. Waiting tends to pay if you are healthy, still working (so the earnings test would hold back benefits anyway), have savings or a pension to bridge the gap, or are the higher earner in a couple. The calculator compares whole ages, but you can start benefits in any month between 62 and 70, so the best month may fall between two of the ages shown.

Worked example: the best age for your life expectancy

With the defaults (born 1966, full retirement age 67, 4% return, 2.8% COLA), the calculator adds up every monthly payment from each claiming age until the 85th birthday, raises them by the COLA each year and discounts them back to age 62. Claiming at 69 comes out on top at $185,148 of value per $1,000 of full benefit, just ahead of 70 ($184,441) and 68 ($184,252), and well ahead of 62 ($167,522).

Life expectancy is the input that matters most. Keeping everything else at the defaults, the best age changes like this.

The return you could earn matters too. At 85, a return of 0% or 2% makes 70 the best age, 4% gives 69, 6% gives 68, and 8% gives 62, because a high return makes money received early worth more. A cautious figure such as the rate on safe bonds is usually the fairest comparison, since a Social Security check is guaranteed and rises with inflation.

Add your full benefit and the same comparison appears in dollars. With a $2,200 full benefit, the defaults give a value today of $368,548 for claiming at 62, $399,798 at 67, $407,325 at 69 and $405,770 at 70. Waiting from 62 to 69 is worth about $38,777 in today's money if you live to 85, while the step from 69 to 70 costs about $1,555, small enough that a long-lived couple or someone worried about outliving their savings might still choose 70.

How to calculate the Social Security break-even age

The break-even age is how long you must live for a later claim to pay back the checks you gave up by waiting. On the Compare two ages tab, suppose your statement shows $1,750 a month at 62 or $2,500 at 67. With a 4% return and the 2.8% COLA, the break-even age is 79: live to 79 or longer and claiming at 67 is worth more, otherwise 62 is. If you live exactly to 79, the two options are worth $343,113 and $343,583 today.

Comparing 67 with 70, say $2,500 against $3,100 a month, the break-even age is 83. The lower the return you use, the sooner waiting pays off: for the 62 versus 67 example, a 0% return gives a break-even of 76 and a 6% return gives 82.

Because benefits rise with the COLA every year, the later benefit is entered in today's dollars and grows while you wait, as SSA's statements assume. The calculator values 12 payments at the start of each year of age, a simple yearly model that is close to the monthly reality.

Many quick break-even sums simply add up the checks: how many months of the larger benefit it takes to make up the payments missed while waiting. That is the same as setting the return to 0% here. It is a fair first look, but it ignores what the early checks could earn, so it makes waiting look a little better than it is.

How Social Security benefits are calculated

Your benefit starts from your earnings record. SSA indexes each year's earnings to wage growth, takes your highest 35 years and divides the total by 420 (the months in 35 years) to get your average indexed monthly earnings, or AIME. Years with no earnings count as zero, which is why working at least 35 years matters.

The primary insurance amount, your full benefit at full retirement age, is then a progressive formula with two bend points: 90% of AIME up to the first bend point, 32% between the first and second, and 15% above the second. For workers who turned 62 in 2024 the bend points were $1,174 and $7,078. Worked by hand with those figures, an AIME of $5,000 gives 90% × 1,174 + 32% × (5,000 − 1,174) = 1,056.60 + 1,224.32 = $2,280.92 a month. The bend points rise with national wages each year, so your own figure uses the ones for the year you turn 62.

Once you are receiving benefits, they rise each year with the cost-of-living adjustment, which SSA ties to inflation as measured by the CPI-W, a price index built from the spending of households of wage earners and clerical workers. The COLA was 2.8% for 2026 and is 3.6% for 2027. Your my Social Security statement shows your estimated benefit at 62, at full retirement age and at 70; those amounts are what the Compare tab needs.

  • Work credits: in 2026 you earn one credit for each $1,890 of covered earnings ($1,970 in 2027), up to four credits a year. You need 40 credits, about 10 years of work, to qualify for retirement benefits.
  • Taxable maximum: earnings above $184,500 in 2026 ($192,300 in 2027) are not taxed for Social Security and do not raise your benefit.
  • Your record: a my Social Security account shows your earnings history and benefit estimates. Check it for missing or wrong years, because any gap counts as zero in your highest 35 years and lowers the benefit for life.

Social Security tax, working while claiming, and tax on benefits

How to calculate Social Security tax: employees pay 6.2% of wages and employers pay another 6.2%, up to the yearly taxable maximum; Medicare adds 1.45% each with no cap (IRS, 2026). On a $60,000 salary the employee's Social Security tax is $3,720. At the 2026 maximum of $184,500 it reaches $11,439, and it stops there. Self-employed people pay both halves, 12.4%.

If you claim before full retirement age and keep working, the retirement earnings test can hold back $1 of benefits for every $2 you earn above a yearly limit that SSA sets each year. The withheld months are not lost for good: SSA recalculates the benefit at full retirement age to credit them. From full retirement age on, the earnings test no longer applies.

Benefits can also be subject to federal income tax. IRS Publication 915 (2026) adds half of your benefits to your other income; if that combined amount is above $25,000 for a single filer or $32,000 for a married couple filing jointly, up to 50% of benefits is taxable, and above $34,000 ($44,000 joint) up to 85%. This calculator compares benefits before tax.

Couples, survivors and Social Security outside the US

For married couples, the claiming decision is shared. A spouse can receive up to 50% of the worker's full benefit if they claim at their own full retirement age, less if earlier, and spousal benefits do not grow past full retirement age. A widow or widower can receive survivor benefits from age 60, up to the deceased worker's full benefit at their own full retirement age. Because the survivor keeps the larger of the two checks, the higher earner waiting until 70 often protects the surviving spouse, even when the money comparison for one person says claim earlier. A divorced spouse can claim on an ex-spouse's record if the marriage lasted at least 10 years and they have not remarried.

Run this calculator for each spouse to see the money side, then think about who is likely to live longer. For couples with an age difference, the higher earner's check may need to last until the younger spouse's expected age at death, so try the higher earner's claim with that longer life expectancy too. Disability benefits (SSDI), Supplemental Security Income and benefits for children follow separate SSA rules and are not modelled here.

Social Security is the US program; your Social Security number is the nine-digit number SSA issues to track your earnings. Pakistan has no Social Security number of this kind: the 13-digit Computerised National Identity Card (CNIC) issued by NADRA is the national identity number. Pakistan's main old-age pension scheme for private-sector workers is run by the Employees' Old-Age Benefits Institution (EOBI), set up under a 1976 act: employers pay 5% and workers 1% of the government's minimum wage, and the old-age pension starts at 60 after 15 years of insured service. Provinces also run social security institutions for workers' health and injury cover. The claiming rules on this page apply only to the US system.

  • Get your real numbers from your my Social Security statement before deciding.
  • Test several life expectancies, not just one, and use a cautious return.
  • If you are married, look at the higher earner's claiming age for the survivor as well as for yourself.
  • Remember Medicare: enrolment is a separate decision around age 65.
  • Revisit the choice each year before you claim, because a new COLA, a change in health or another year of work can move the best age.

How it works

The first tab needs only your birth year and the age you expect to live to. Your birth year sets your full retirement age (67 if born in 1960 or later). Claiming earlier cuts the monthly benefit for good; waiting past it raises the benefit until age 70. For each whole claiming age from 62 to 70 the calculator adds up every monthly payment until your expected age at death, raises them each year by the cost-of-living adjustment, and values them in today's money at the return you could earn elsewhere. The age with the largest total is the best one in money terms. The second tab takes two claiming ages with the monthly amounts from your SSA statement and finds the age you must live to for waiting to pay off.

The formula

  • Full retirement age: 66 for 1943 to 1954, +2 months a year for 1955 to 1959, 67 from 1960
  • Early: benefit falls 5/9 of 1% a month for the first 36 months before full retirement age, 5/12 of 1% a month beyond that
  • Late: +2/3 of 1% a month (8% a year) after full retirement age, up to age 70
  • Value of claiming at age A = Σ share(A) × (1 + COLA)^(whole years since 62) ÷ (1 + return)^(months since 62 ÷ 12), over the months from A to your life expectancy
  • Break-even (second tab): the first age at which the value of claiming later is at least the value of claiming earlier

A worked example

Born in 1966, your full retirement age is 67. Claiming at 62 pays 70% of the full benefit (60 months early: 36 × 5/9 + 24 × 5/12 = 30% less), at 69 it pays 116% and at 70 it pays 124%. If you expect to live to 85, could earn 4% a year and use SSA's 2.8% COLA for 2026, claiming at 69 gives the largest value today.

Questions

How are Social Security benefits calculated?

SSA indexes your earnings, averages your highest 35 years into a monthly figure (AIME) and applies a formula of 90%, 32% and 15% across two bend points to get your full benefit. Claiming before full retirement age cuts it and waiting past it raises it, and each year's COLA adds to it.

What is my full retirement age?

It is 67 if you were born in 1960 or later. For 1955 to 1959 it is 66 plus 2 months for each year after 1954 (66 and 10 months for 1959), and 66 for 1943 to 1954.

How much do I lose by claiming at 62?

With a full retirement age of 67, claiming at 62 pays 70% of the full benefit for life. The cut is 5/9 of 1% for each of the first 36 months early and 5/12 of 1% for each month beyond that.

How much more do I get by waiting until 70?

Each month you wait past full retirement age adds 2/3 of 1%, or 8% a year, up to age 70. With a full retirement age of 67 that is 124% of the full benefit. Waiting past 70 adds nothing.

Should I claim Social Security at 62 or 67?

It depends mainly on how long you expect to live and whether you need the money now. On the Compare tab, $1,750 at 62 against $2,500 at 67 breaks even at 79 with a 4% return: live longer and 67 wins. Health, savings and a spouse's needs also count.

How do I calculate the Social Security break-even age?

Add up the value of the payments from each claiming age for each possible age at death, allowing for COLA and the return you could earn, and find the first age where the later claim is worth at least as much. The Compare two ages tab does this from your two monthly amounts.

How do I calculate Social Security tax?

Multiply wages by 6.2% up to the yearly taxable maximum, $184,500 in 2026. A $60,000 salary means $3,720 from the employee and the same from the employer; the self-employed pay 12.4%.

How many Social Security credits do I need?

You need 40 credits to qualify for retirement benefits. In 2026 each $1,890 of covered earnings earns one credit, up to four a year, so about 10 years of work is enough to qualify.

Are Social Security benefits taxed?

They can be. Add half of your benefits to your other income; above $25,000 for a single filer or $32,000 for a joint return, up to 50% of benefits becomes taxable, and up to 85% above $34,000 or $44,000 (IRS Publication 915, 2026).

How do spousal benefits work?

A spouse can get up to half of the worker's full benefit at the spouse's own full retirement age, less if claimed earlier. Survivors can claim from 60 and receive up to the deceased worker's benefit, which is why the higher earner often waits.

Why does the best age depend on the return I enter?

Money received sooner can be saved or invested. A higher return makes early payments worth more today, which favours claiming earlier; a low return and a long life favour waiting.

Is there Social Security in Pakistan?

Not the US program. Pakistan's private-sector old-age pension is run by EOBI, funded by employer and worker contributions, with a pension from 60 after 15 years of insured service. There is no Social Security number; the 13-digit CNIC from NADRA is the national identity number.
