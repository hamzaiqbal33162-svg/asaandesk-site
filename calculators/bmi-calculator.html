What is BMI, and what does your number mean?

Body mass index (BMI) is a quick screening number that compares your weight with your height. This BMI calculator divides your weight in kilograms by your height in metres squared, so the unit is kg/m². A result of 22 means every square metre of your height-squared carries 22 kg; on its own that sounds abstract, but decades of population studies link BMI ranges to health risk, which is why doctors, insurers and national surveys use it.

The idea goes back to the Belgian statistician Adolphe Quetelet in the 19th century. The name body mass index came from Ancel Keys and colleagues in 1972, who compared several weight-for-height indices and recommended this one for adults. Today the World Health Organization (WHO) uses BMI for its adult weight classes and the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) uses it, compared by age, for children.

Treat the number as a first look, not a diagnosis. A BMI of 27 says your weight is high for your height; it cannot say whether that weight is fat, muscle or water, or where the fat sits. The sections below explain how to read the result, which cut-offs fit South Asian adults, and what to check alongside BMI.

How to calculate BMI with this calculator

  • Pick Metric (kilograms and centimetres) or US units (pounds, feet and inches) with the unit switch. If your weight is in kg but you know your height in feet, convert the height first: 1 ft = 30.48 cm and 1 in = 2.54 cm, so 5 ft 4 in = 162.6 cm.
  • Choose Male or Female. For adults this does not change the BMI or the class; for children and teens it picks the right growth chart, because boys and girls grow differently.
  • Enter the age in whole years. From 20 upwards you get the adult WHO class; from 2 to 19 the calculator switches to CDC growth chart percentiles.
  • For a child or teen, also fill in Extra months (the months since the last birthday). A 10-year-old who turned 10 seven months ago is 10 years and 7 months, and that extra precision matters most for young children.
  • Enter height and weight, ideally measured without shoes and heavy clothes, and read the result. Weigh yourself at the same time of day if you plan to track changes.

BMI categories for adults (WHO table)

For adults aged 20 and over the calculator uses the WHO classification from its report on obesity (Technical Report Series 894). Each class is half-open: 25.0 is overweight, while 24.99 is still normal. The healthy band of 18.5 to under 25 is the one the calculator turns into a healthy weight range for your height.

The CDC uses the same cut-offs with simpler names (underweight, healthy weight, overweight, obesity classes 1 to 3) and adds that BMI is one possible health indicator that should be considered with other factors.

Worked examples: a US adult and a Pakistani adult

  • US units: a 30-year-old man, 5 ft 10 in and 190 lb. His BMI is 27.3, in the overweight class. The healthy weight for his height is 128.9 to 174.2 lb, so losing about 15.8 lb would bring him to BMI 25. His BMI Prime is 1.09 (9% above the upper limit) and his Ponderal Index is 15.3 kg/m³.
  • Metric, Lahore: a 35-year-old woman, 160 cm and 68 kg. Her BMI is 68 ÷ 1.6² = 26.6, overweight on the WHO scale. Her healthy weight is 47.4 to 64.0 kg, so 4.0 kg of loss reaches BMI 25. On the Asian scale she is above the 23 action point and below 27.5; reaching BMI 23 would mean about 58.9 kg.
  • A child: a boy aged 10 years and 6 months, 140 cm and 38 kg, has a BMI of 19.4. On the CDC chart that is the 82nd percentile, so his weight status is healthy weight, close to the 85th percentile line. The healthy BMI range for his age is 14.4 to 19.8, which is 28.2 to 38.8 kg at his height.
  • Underweight: a 24-year-old woman, 157 cm and 44 kg, has a BMI of 17.9 (mild thinness). Gaining about 1.6 kg would bring her to BMI 18.5.

BMI for children and teens: percentiles explained

A child's BMI naturally falls in the preschool years and then rises through the teens, and the pattern differs between boys and girls. A fixed adult cut-off would therefore mislabel many healthy children. For ages 2 through 19 the calculator compares the BMI with the CDC 2000 growth charts for the same sex and age in months and reports a percentile: the 70th percentile means the BMI is higher than that of about 70 out of 100 children of that age and sex.

The calculator also gives weight-for-age and height-for-age percentiles and a healthy BMI and weight range for the child's age (the 5th to 85th percentile band). Above the 95th percentile, percentiles crowd towards 100, so the CDC suggests describing BMI as a percentage of the 95th percentile instead, which the calculator shows.

Asian and South Asian BMI cut-offs: what applies in Pakistan?

Many people of South Asian heritage develop type 2 diabetes and heart disease at a lower BMI than people of European heritage. A WHO expert consultation published in The Lancet in 2004 reviewed this evidence. It found that the BMI at which risk starts to rise ranged from 22 to 25 kg/m² across Asian populations, and the BMI linked with high risk ranged from 26 to 31 kg/m².

The consultation kept the standard WHO classes as the international system, because no single Asian cut-off fitted every population. Instead it added public health action points at 23, 27.5, 32.5 and 37.5 kg/m² so that countries could decide their own thresholds. Body composition studies point the same way: a 2002 review found that Asian groups, including Singaporean Indians, had 3 to 5 percentage points more body fat than Europeans at the same BMI.

In the UK, the NICE guideline on overweight and obesity (NG246, published January 2025) tells clinicians to use lower thresholds for people of South Asian, Chinese, other Asian, Middle Eastern, Black African or African-Caribbean background: overweight from BMI 23 to 27.4 and obesity from 27.5. The Pakistan Dietary Guidelines for Better Nutrition (2018) present the WHO classes and add a waist check: more than 94 cm for men or 80 cm for women means greater risk.

For example, a 42-year-old man who is 5 ft 7 in (170.2 cm) and 78 kg has a BMI of 26.9: overweight on both scales, but on the Asian scale he would need to reach about 66.6 kg (BMI 23), not 72.4 kg (BMI 25), to leave the higher-risk band.

BMI Prime and the Ponderal Index

BMI Prime was proposed by John Gadzik in 2006 as a simpler way to say how far someone is from the upper healthy limit. It is your BMI divided by 25, so it has no unit: 1.00 is exactly at the limit, 0.90 is 10% under it and 1.20 is 20% over it. Because it is a ratio, it reads the same in any unit system, and the healthy band is 0.74 to just under 1.00.

The Ponderal Index (also called Rohrer's index) divides weight in kilograms by height in metres cubed. A body that is simply scaled up gains weight roughly with the cube of its height, not the square, so BMI drifts upwards for very tall people and downwards for very short ones. The Ponderal Index removes most of that drift. A handy shortcut is that the Ponderal Index equals your BMI divided by your height in metres.

The Ponderal Index is used most often for newborns and for very tall or short adults; there is no widely agreed adult cut-off, so the calculator shows it for comparison only.

Health risks of being overweight or underweight

The WHO estimates that in 2024 about 2.6 billion adults (45%) were overweight and 940 million (16%) were living with obesity, and that a higher-than-optimal BMI caused an estimated 3.7 million deaths in 2023. In Pakistan, the National Nutrition Survey 2018 found that 37.8% of women aged 15 to 49 were overweight or obese, up from 28% in 2011, while 14.4% were undernourished. The second National Diabetes Survey of Pakistan (2016 to 2017) found diabetes in 26.3% of adults and listed obesity among its main risk factors.

  • A high BMI raises the risk of high blood pressure, unhealthy blood fats (high LDL cholesterol, low HDL cholesterol, high triglycerides), type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease and stroke (CDC).
  • It is also linked with gallbladder disease, osteoarthritis, sleep apnoea and breathing problems, many types of cancer, body pain, depression and anxiety, a lower quality of life and earlier death (CDC).
  • Being underweight can mean you are missing vitamins and minerals, which the NHS links with a weak immune system and bone fractures.
  • Undernutrition shows up as tiredness, weakness, getting ill often and taking a long time to recover; in children it can mean not growing or gaining weight at the expected rate (NHS).
  • Unplanned weight loss deserves a doctor's visit: the NHS treats a loss of about 5% to 10% of body weight over 3 to 6 months as a main warning sign of malnutrition.

If you need to gain weight, the NHS suggests adding about 300 to 500 kcal a day through nourishing foods rather than sweets and sugary drinks. If you need to lose weight, even a modest loss usually improves blood pressure and blood sugar; the Calorie Calculator shows a safe daily target.

Limitations of BMI, and what to check alongside it

  • Muscle versus fat: BMI cannot tell them apart, so athletes and people who do heavy physical work can read as overweight while carrying little fat. NICE advises interpreting BMI with caution in adults with high muscle mass.
  • Age: older people often lose muscle and height, so a normal BMI can hide a high body fat share. NICE also advises caution for people aged 65 and over.
  • Sex and ethnicity: at the same BMI women usually carry more fat than men, and South Asian adults more fat than Europeans, which is why lower Asian thresholds exist.
  • Fat distribution: fat around the waist is more harmful than fat on the hips. NICE recommends keeping your waist to less than half your height (a waist-to-height ratio under 0.5); 0.5 to 0.59 means increased risk and 0.6 or more further increased risk.
  • Confirming obesity: a 2025 Lancet Diabetes and Endocrinology Commission recommends confirming excess fat with at least one more measure, such as waist circumference, waist-to-hip or waist-to-height ratio, or a direct body fat scan, except at very high BMI (above about 40) where excess fat can be assumed.
  • Pregnancy, oedema and amputation change weight in ways BMI was not designed for; ask a doctor how to read your number in these cases.

Tips: measure height properly rather than guessing, weigh yourself at the same time of day, and track the trend over weeks instead of single readings. How it works

BMI is your weight in kilograms divided by your height in metres squared. For adults (20 and over) the World Health Organization classes run from severe thinness below 16 to obese class III at 40 and above, with 18.5 to 25 counted as healthy; the healthy weight range is those two BMIs times your height squared. BMI Prime is your BMI divided by 25, so anything over 1.00 is above the healthy range. The Ponderal Index divides weight by height cubed, which keeps it fairer for very short or very tall people. Children and teens grow fast, so a single cut-off does not work for them: from 2 to 19 the BMI is compared with children of the same age and sex on the CDC 2000 growth charts and given as a percentile, and weight and height get their own percentiles too.

The formula

  • BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)²; in US units the pounds and inches are converted exactly (1 lb = 0.45359237 kg, 1 in = 2.54 cm)
  • Adult classes (WHO): under 16 severe thinness, 16 to 17 moderate thinness, 17 to 18.5 mild thinness, 18.5 to 25 normal, 25 to 30 overweight, 30 to 35 obese class I, 35 to 40 obese class II, 40 and over obese class III
  • Healthy weight = 18.5 × height² to 25 × height²; BMI Prime = BMI ÷ 25; Ponderal Index = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)³
  • Children 2 to 19: z = ((BMI ÷ M)^L − 1) ÷ (L × S) with the CDC L, M and S values for the child's sex and age in months, and the percentile is the share of the normal curve below z
  • Child classes (CDC): under the 5th percentile underweight, 5th to under the 85th healthy weight, 85th to under the 95th overweight, 95th and above obesity (120% of the 95th percentile or more: severe obesity)

A worked example

A 30-year-old who is 175 cm tall and weighs 70 kg: 1.75² = 3.0625, so BMI = 70 ÷ 3.0625 ≈ 22.9, in the normal class. The healthy range for that height is 18.5 × 3.0625 ≈ 56.7 kg to 25 × 3.0625 ≈ 76.6 kg, BMI Prime is 22.9 ÷ 25 ≈ 0.91 and the Ponderal Index is 70 ÷ 1.75³ ≈ 13.1 kg/m³.

Questions

What is a healthy BMI?

For adults, 18.5 up to 25. Under 18.5 is underweight, 25 to 30 is overweight and 30 or more is obese on the WHO scale. For people of South Asian background many doctors use lower lines, with overweight from 23 and obesity from 27.5, because health risks start at a lower BMI.

How do I calculate BMI in kg and feet?

Turn your height into metres first: multiply the feet by 30.48 and the inches by 2.54, add them and divide by 100. Then divide your weight in kg by that number squared. For 5 ft 6 in (167.6 cm) and 65 kg: 65 ÷ 1.676² ≈ 23.1. The calculator does this for you in either unit system.

What is the BMI formula in kg and cm?

BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)², and height in metres is centimetres divided by 100. An equivalent form is weight (kg) × 10,000 ÷ height (cm)². For 70 kg and 175 cm that is 70 × 10,000 ÷ 30,625 ≈ 22.9.

Is the BMI calculator the same for men and women?

For adults, yes: the formula and the WHO classes do not change with sex or age, although women usually carry more body fat than men at the same BMI. For children and teens, sex matters, so the calculator uses separate CDC growth charts for boys and girls.

Does age change my BMI result?

From age 20 the result is read on one adult scale whatever your age, though BMI is less reliable after 65 because of muscle loss. From 2 to 19, age in years and months decides the percentile, so the same BMI can be healthy at one age and overweight at another.

Why do children get a percentile instead of a class?

A child's BMI changes a lot as they grow, and boys and girls grow differently. So the BMI is compared with children of the same sex and age on the CDC growth charts. The 65th percentile means the BMI is higher than that of about 65 out of 100 children of the same age and sex.

What BMI is used in Pakistan?

Official guidance such as the Pakistan Dietary Guidelines for Better Nutrition presents the WHO classes, with 18.5 to 25 as normal, plus a waist check. Because South Asians tend to have more body fat at a given BMI, many clinicians also use the Asian action points from the 2004 WHO consultation, treating 23 and above as a reason for closer checks.

What does BMI mean in a medical report?

It is your weight in kg divided by your height in metres squared, written in kg/m². The report may also give the WHO class, such as normal or overweight. Doctors use it as a screening sign and usually look at your waist, blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol before drawing conclusions.

What are BMI Prime and the Ponderal Index?

BMI Prime is your BMI divided by 25, the top of the healthy range, so 0.92 means 8% under the upper limit and 1.20 means 20% over it. The Ponderal Index divides weight by height cubed instead of squared, which makes it fairer for very short or very tall people.

Is BMI accurate for everyone?

No. BMI cannot tell muscle from fat, so athletes can read as overweight, and older people who have lost muscle can read as normal while carrying a lot of fat. Waist size, body fat percentage and a doctor's check give a fuller picture.

Why does my child's exact age in months matter?

Growth chart values change month by month. A whole number of years is taken as the child's birthday; add the months since the last birthday for a closer result, especially for young children who grow fastest.
