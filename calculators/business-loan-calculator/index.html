What a business loan calculator shows

A business loan calculator works out the repayments on money you borrow for a company: the payment each week, month or quarter, the total interest, the fees and the real yearly cost once those fees are counted. It suits term loans for equipment, stock, vehicles, premises or working capital, and loans used to buy a business.

The quoted interest rate rarely tells the whole story. Lenders often take an origination or arrangement fee out of the loan before you get the money, so you receive less cash but repay the full amount with interest. This calculator shows that cost as a real APR, so you can compare offers with different rates, fees and schedules on equal terms.

How to use the business loan calculator

  • Loan amount: the full amount in the loan agreement, before any fees are taken off.
  • Interest rate: the yearly rate the lender quotes, in % a year.
  • Loan term: years plus extra months, for terms such as 2 years 6 months. The term must be at least one month.
  • How you repay: equal payments daily, weekly, every two weeks, twice a month, monthly, quarterly, every six months or yearly; Interest only, loan repaid at the end; or Everything at the end.
  • Origination fee: the lender's fee as a percentage of the loan. Lenders usually take it out of the loan.
  • More fees and compounding (optional): a documentation fee, other fees and how often interest compounds. Interest-only loans always pay the interest monthly.
  • Currency: choose US dollars, Pakistani rupees or another currency from the list.

If the fees add up to the whole loan or more, the calculator stops, because no money would be left for you. Weekly and two-weekly payments count a year as 52 weeks.

Worked business loan examples

A US company borrows $120,000 at 9.5% for 4 years to buy equipment, with a 2.5% origination fee and a $750 documentation fee. Monthly payments are $3,014.78 and the 48 payments total $144,709.27, of which $24,709.27 is interest. The fees of $3,750 come out of the loan, so $116,250 reaches the account and the real APR is 11.181%. Without fees the APR would be the quoted 9.5%.

In the first month, $950.00 of the payment is interest and $2,064.78 repays the loan. By the last month the interest is only $23.68, because each payment shrinks the balance the interest is charged on. In year 1 the company pays $10,292.17 of interest, in year 4 just $1,794.84.

The way you repay changes the cost a lot. The table keeps the same loan, rate, term and fees and changes only the schedule.

Business loan fees and the real APR

Common business loan costs are an origination or arrangement fee, documentation or underwriting fees, valuation or legal fees on secured loans, and sometimes a charge for paying the loan off early. Enter every fee the lender requires to give you the loan. Leave out costs you would have anyway, such as your own accountant.

A fee hurts more on a short loan, because it is spread over fewer months. The table keeps the $120,000 loan at 9.5% with $3,750 of fees and changes only the term.

Types of business loans

  • Term loans: a lump sum repaid in fixed installments over a set term, the case this calculator handles directly.
  • SBA loans (United States): loans made by banks and other lenders with a guarantee from the US Small Business Administration. The main 7(a) program lends up to $5 million, 504 loans give long-term fixed-rate finance through Certified Development Companies, and microloans go up to $50,000. The SBA says their rates and fees are generally comparable to loans without a guarantee.
  • Lines of credit and running finance: you draw and repay as you need, paying interest only on what you use. Model a drawn amount as an interest-only loan to see the monthly cost.
  • Equipment and vehicle finance, including leasing or Ijarah: the asset itself secures the loan, and the term usually follows its working life.
  • Islamic business finance: in Pakistan, banks offer Murabaha (the bank sells you the goods at a disclosed cost plus profit), Diminishing Musharakah (joint ownership that you buy out over time) and Ijarah (leasing). The profit rate works in the calculator in the same way as an interest rate.

Business loans in Pakistan: rates and schemes

Rates in Pakistan follow the State Bank of Pakistan (SBP) policy rate, which the Monetary Policy Committee raised to 12% with effect from 27 October 2026. Many bank loans are floating and reset with the market, while some schemes have fixed concessional rates. Rates change often, so take the current rate from the bank's offer letter.

Meezan Bank's SME page (2026) lists Islamic SME Asaan Finance for new or existing businesses without a current Meezan relationship: up to Rs 10 million at a fixed 9% a year. It also lists the Prime Minister's Youth Business and Agriculture Loan Scheme for Pakistani citizens with a CNIC aged 21 to 45: Tier 2 above Rs 0.5 million up to Rs 1.5 million at 5%, and Tier 3 above Rs 1.5 million up to Rs 7.5 million at 7%, with long-term finance of up to 8 years. Applications for several schemes go through the SBP's SME Connect portal.

Example: Rs 5,000,000 of SME finance at 9% for 5 years, with an assumed 1% processing fee and Rs 10,000 of documentation charges. Choose Pakistani rupee and the calculator shows a monthly installment of Rs 103,791.78. The 60 installments total Rs 6,227,506.57, so profit is Rs 1,227,506.57; with Rs 60,000 of fees you receive Rs 4,940,000.00. A Tier 2 youth loan of Rs 1,500,000 at 5% over 5 years would be Rs 28,306.85 a month, Rs 198,411.03 of profit in all. These schemes can include a grace period, which this calculator does not model.

Tips before you sign, and related calculators

  • Compare the real APR and the total of interest and fees, not just the quoted rate.
  • Check that your cash flow covers the payment in a slow month, not just an average one; weekly payments suit businesses paid daily, monthly payments suit businesses paid by invoice.
  • Ask whether the rate is fixed or floating, how often it resets and whether there is a charge for early repayment.
  • To work out how much you can borrow, start from the payment your business can carry and change the loan amount until the payment matches it.

How it works

Enter the loan amount, the yearly rate, the term and how you will repay: equal payments on any schedule from daily to yearly, interest only each month with the loan repaid in one go at the end, or nothing until the end. Add the lender's fees: an origination fee as a share of the loan, plus documentation and other fees under More fees and compounding. Fees usually come out of the loan, so you receive less cash but repay the whole amount. The real APR is the yearly rate at which your repayments are worth exactly the cash you receive, which is why it is higher than the quoted rate.

The formula

  • Rate per payment r = (1 + i ÷ m)^(m ÷ p) − 1, where i is the yearly rate, m the times interest compounds a year and p the payments a year
  • Equal payments: P = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), n = term in years × p
  • Interest only: interest each month = L × monthly rate; L is repaid with the last payment
  • Everything at the end: amount due = L × (1 + i ÷ m)^(m × T)
  • Fees = L × origination % + documentation fee + other fees; cash received = L − fees
  • Real APR = 12 × x, where x is the monthly rate at which all repayments are worth the cash received

A worked example

Borrow $120,000 at 9.5% for 4 years, repaid monthly: the payment is $3,014.78 and the 48 payments total $144,709.27, so interest is $24,709.27. With a 2.5% origination fee ($3,000) and a $750 documentation fee you receive $116,250, interest and fees come to $28,459.27, and the real APR is 11.181% instead of 9.5%.

Questions

How do I calculate business loan payments?

Use P = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), where L is the loan, r the rate per payment period and n the number of payments. For $120,000 at 9.5% over 4 years that is $3,014.78 a month; the calculator works it out for any schedule.

How do I calculate the EMI on a business loan?

EMI (equated monthly installment) is the same equal monthly payment. Enter the loan, the yearly rate and the term with How you repay set to monthly. A Rs 5,000,000 loan at 9% over 5 years gives an EMI of Rs 103,791.78.

How do I calculate the interest on a business loan?

Add up all the payments and subtract the loan amount. The calculator shows this as Total interest, and Interest and fees adds the lender's charges, which is the full cost of borrowing.

Why is the real APR so much higher than the interest rate?

Fees taken out of the loan mean you receive less cash but still repay the full amount with interest. Spread over the loan, that is the same as paying a higher rate on the money you actually got. Short loans show a bigger jump, because the fee is spread over fewer years.

What is an interest-only loan?

You pay only the interest each month, so the payments are small, and repay the whole loan in one amount at the end (a balloon payment). You pay more interest in total than with equal payments, because the balance never goes down.

What is the business loan interest rate in Pakistan?

It depends on the bank, the scheme and whether the rate is fixed or floating. The SBP policy rate was 12% from 27 October 2026; Meezan Bank listed its SME Asaan Finance at a fixed 9% and the youth scheme at 5% or 7% by tier in 2026. Always use the rate in your own offer.

What are the business loan requirements in Pakistan?

Each bank sets its own, but schemes publish their basics: the Prime Minister's youth loan scheme is open to Pakistani citizens with a CNIC aged 21 to 45, and banks also look at your business plan or accounts, cash flow and security. Ask the bank for its document list before you apply.

Does paying weekly or every two weeks cost less?

Slightly less interest, because the balance falls a little sooner between payments: $24,270.69 instead of $24,709.27 on our example. A year is taken as 52 weeks, so check that your income arrives often enough to meet the payments.

Which fees should I include?

Anything the lender charges to give you the loan: origination or arrangement fees, documentation or underwriting fees, and any other required charges. Leave out costs you would pay anyway, such as your own accountant.
