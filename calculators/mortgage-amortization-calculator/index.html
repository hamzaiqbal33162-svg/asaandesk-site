What a mortgage amortization calculator shows

A mortgage amortization calculator shows how a home loan is paid off, payment by payment. Amortization means clearing a debt with equal, regular payments, each covering that month's interest plus a slice of the balance. The list of those payments, with the interest, principal and balance after each one, is the amortization schedule.

The payment stays the same for the whole term on a fixed-rate loan, but what it pays for changes. Early on, most of it is interest; near the end, almost all of it reduces the balance. Seeing that split helps you judge how much an extra payment saves, how much equity you have after a few years, and what the loan truly costs.

This page starts from the home price and your down payment, gives the monthly principal and interest, adds property tax, insurance, mortgage insurance (PMI), HOA and other costs if you want them, and lists a dated schedule by year and by month.

Not every loan amortizes. With an interest-only period, the balance does not fall at all until principal payments begin. Some loans even let you pay less than the interest due; the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) calls this negative amortization, meaning that even when you pay, the amount you owe still goes up because the unpaid interest is added to the balance. A standard fixed-rate mortgage, the kind this page models, always amortizes to zero by the last payment.

  • Principal: the amount you borrowed and still owe, before interest.
  • Interest: the lender's charge for the money, worked out each month on the balance.
  • Balance: what is left to repay after each payment.
  • Equity: the home's value minus the balance; it grows as you pay principal and as the value rises.
  • Term or amortization period: the number of years over which the payments clear the loan.

Mortgage amortization formula: how to calculate it by hand

The mortgage payment formula is M = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), with L the loan, r the monthly rate (the yearly rate ÷ 12) and n the number of monthly payments. For the defaults, r = 0.065 ÷ 12 = 0.0054167 and n = 360, so M = 320,000 × 0.0054167 ÷ (1 − 1.0054167^−360) = $2,022.62.

Worked step by step: (1 + r)^360 = 6.9918, so (1 + r)^−360 = 0.1430 and 1 − 0.1430 = 0.8570. The top of the formula, L × r = $1,733.33, is exactly the first month's interest. Dividing it by 0.8570 gives $2,022.62. The payment is set so that the 360 payments, discounted at the loan rate, are worth exactly the $320,000 you borrow, which is why the last payment brings the balance to zero. At 0% interest the formula is simply the loan divided by the months: 320,000 ÷ 360 = $888.89.

To build the schedule, repeat three steps every month: interest = balance × r, principal = payment − interest, new balance = balance − principal. The first three rows of the default loan look like this:

The same loan amortization formula works for any fixed-rate loan. A $25,000 car loan at 7% over 5 years has r = 0.07 ÷ 12 and n = 60, so the payment is $495.03; the first month carries $145.83 of interest and $349.20 of principal, and the whole loan costs $4,701.80 of interest.

Each month the interest falls a little because the balance is a little lower, so the principal part grows by the same amount. You can jump to any month with the balance formula: balance after k payments = L × (1 + r)^k − M × ((1 + r)^k − 1) ÷ r.

In Excel or Google Sheets, =PMT(6.5%/12, 360, -320000) gives the payment, =IPMT(6.5%/12, 1, 360, -320000) the first month's interest ($1,733.33) and =PPMT(6.5%/12, 1, 360, -320000) its principal ($289.28). To build a full mortgage amortization schedule in Excel, put the balance in one column and fill the interest, principal and new balance formulas down 360 rows; =CUMIPMT(6.5%/12, 360, 320000, 1, 12, 0) returns the first year's interest, −$20,694.69.

Why early mortgage payments are mostly interest

Interest is charged on what you still owe, and in the early years you owe the most. The yearly schedule of the default loan shows how slowly the balance moves at first.

After 10 years you have paid $242,714, but only $48,716 of it has reduced the loan; the other $193,998 was interest. The principal part of a payment first passes the interest part in payment 233, during year 20, and the balance only falls below half the original loan during year 22. There is a neat pattern behind it: the principal part of each payment is the previous one times (1 + r), so at 6.5% it doubles roughly every 128 months, from $289.28 in month 1 to about $578 in month 129 and $1,153 in month 257.

A shorter term changes this picture. The same $320,000 at 6.5% over 15 years leaves $245,494.78 owed after 5 years and $142,467.67 after 10, against $299,555.13 and $271,283.60 on the 30-year loan. Before any change in the home's value, that is $257,532 of equity on the $400,000 home after 10 years on the 15-year loan, double the $128,716 on the 30-year loan.

This is why equity builds slowly in the first years of a 30-year mortgage, why selling soon after buying can leave little profit once costs are paid, and why extra payments made early save far more interest than the same payments made late.

A quick rule for any loan size: per $100,000 borrowed, a 30-year payment is $536.82 at 5%, $599.55 at 6%, $665.30 at 7% and $733.76 at 8%; over 15 years it is $790.79, $843.86, $898.83 and $955.65. Multiply by your loan in hundreds of thousands for a first estimate.

When the rate changes, the schedule is simply worked again from the balance left. That happens with an adjustable-rate mortgage when the fixed period ends, with a KIBOR-linked loan in Pakistan, and at each renewal in Canada. On the default loan, $299,555.13 is still owed after 5 years. Renewed for the remaining 25 years at 5.5%, the payment falls to $1,839.53; at 7.5% it rises to $2,213.69, and at 8.5% to $2,412.10. To run it here, enter the balance as the price with 0% down, the years left and the new rate.

Taxes, insurance, PMI and escrow

Your lender's payment covers principal and interest, but owning the home also means property tax and insurance, and many lenders collect them with each payment through an escrow account. The calculator spreads each yearly cost evenly over 12 months. With the defaults, 1.2% property tax on $400,000 is $4,800 a year ($400 a month) and insurance is $1,500 a year ($125 a month), so $2,547.62 leaves your account each month and $917,142.36 over 30 years.

With less than 20% down, lenders usually require private mortgage insurance (PMI). Take 10% down: the loan is $360,000, the payment $2,275.44, and with PMI of $1,800 a year the monthly total is $2,950.44. Here PMI is counted until the balance falls to 80% of the price, after 94 payments, for $14,100 in all. The table compares the two down payments on the same $400,000 home.

Under US rules described by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, you can ask in writing to cancel PMI once the balance reaches 80% of the original value (the lower of the price and the appraisal), with a good payment record. The servicer generally must end it when the balance is scheduled to reach 78%, and in any case the month after the midpoint of the schedule, such as year 15 of a 30-year loan, if you are up to date. Property tax and insurance usually rise over time; the Mortgage Calculator can add yearly increases.

The interest column of the schedule also matters at tax time in the US. For 2025 returns, IRS Publication 936 lets you deduct home mortgage interest on up to $750,000 of mortgage debt ($375,000 if married filing separately) if you itemize deductions; older loans taken out before 16 December 2017 have a higher limit. On the default loan the first 12 payments carry $20,694.69 of interest, which you can read straight from the yearly schedule. Your lender also reports the interest it received each year.

The CFPB notes that property taxes and insurance premiums can change from year to year, and your escrow payment, and with it your total monthly payment, changes with them, even when principal and interest stay fixed. If your statement shows a different monthly total from this page, compare the escrow lines first.

Paying off faster: extra payments, biweekly payments and their drawbacks

Any amount paid on top of the scheduled payment goes straight to the balance, so it stops interest from building on that money for the rest of the loan. On the default $320,000 loan, adding $200 a month from the start ends the loan in 23 years 5 months instead of 30 and saves $105,428.67 of interest. Paying half the payment, $1,011.31, every two weeks adds up to 13 monthly payments a year; it ends the loan in 24 years 3 months and saves $91,481.52. The Mortgage Payoff Calculator works out these plans for your own loan and balance. The table compares a few plans on the default loan, all starting with the first payment.

The biweekly saving comes almost entirely from that 13th payment each year, not from paying more often, so adding one twelfth of a payment to every monthly payment gives a similar result without changing your payment dates: $168.55 extra each month ends the default loan in 24 years 2 months, one month sooner than the biweekly plan. Whichever you choose, mark the extra money as a principal payment and check that your next statement shows the balance falling by that amount.

A lump sum works the same way, but a smaller sum early often beats a bigger one later, because it has more months to stop interest. Some lenders will also recast the loan after a large lump sum, keeping the end date but lowering the monthly payment; ask whether yours does and what it charges.

There are trade-offs. The CFPB explains that some mortgages carry a prepayment penalty if you pay off the whole balance within a set number of years, usually three or five, or pay a large amount at once, while small extra principal payments do not normally trigger it; check your loan terms. Money put into the house is also hard to get back without selling or borrowing again, so keep an emergency fund and clear more expensive debts first. If your mortgage rate is low, the money might earn more elsewhere.

This page does not model extra payments or balloon payments. A balloon mortgage leaves a large lump sum due at the end; to see one, compare the balance at the balloon date in the yearly schedule.

Mortgage amortization in Pakistan, Canada and the UK

Pakistan: many home loans are floating-rate and revised with KIBOR. For example, BankIslami's key fact statement for its Muskun home financing (May 2026) shows a rental rate of 12.99% fixed for the first three years and one-year KIBOR + 3% after that, revised every year with the spread unchanged. Take a Rs 20,000,000 home with 25% down: the Rs 15,000,000 loan over 20 years at 12.99% costs Rs 175,629.51 a month, with Rs 27,151,081.92 of profit over the term if the rate never changed. After three years Rs 14,420,292.63 is still owed; if the rate then rose to 13.99%, enter that balance with 0% down, 17 years and the new rate to see the new installment of about Rs 185,557.54. At rates like these the balance falls very slowly at first: after the first year of the 20-year loan only Rs 168,873.95 of the Rs 15,000,000 has been repaid.

Islamic home finance on diminishing musharakah splits each installment into rent and a unit purchase. In the BankIslami statement the unit purchase is fixed and the rent varies, so installments can differ from a level loan schedule; treat this page as an estimate and ask your bank for its own schedule. Read the early-settlement terms too: in the same statement, paying off within the first 12 months means the remaining units are bought at a price 5% higher.

Canada: the amortization period is the time to pay the whole loan off, while the mortgage term is the shorter contract, often a few years, after which you renew. Since 15 December 2024, insured mortgages can have a 30-year amortization for first-time buyers and buyers of new builds (Department of Finance Canada). The Interest Act requires blended-payment mortgages to state the rate calculated yearly or half-yearly, so use the Canadian Mortgage Calculator for half-yearly compounding. UK: a repayment mortgage amortizes in the same way, while an interest-only mortgage leaves the whole loan to be repaid at the end of the term, so its balance line would stay flat. When a fixed-rate deal ends and the loan moves to a different rate, the schedule is worked again from the balance left, just as with a renewal. The UK Mortgage Calculator covers UK loans in pounds.

Tips, common mistakes and related calculators

  • Budget for the monthly total with costs, not only principal and interest.
  • Compare loans by total interest as well as the payment; a lower payment over a longer term can cost far more.
  • Remember that the rate here is fixed. For a variable or KIBOR-linked loan, run it again at a higher rate to test your budget.
  • Check the payoff figure with your lender before you pay a loan off; daily interest and fees mean it differs slightly from the schedule.
  • Ask in writing to remove PMI as soon as you reach 80% of the original value; it does not always stop by itself until 78%.
  • Keep the schedule: the interest column helps with tax records where mortgage interest is deductible.
  • Use your own property tax rate. The 1.2% default is only a starting point; rates differ widely between cities, counties and countries, so take the figure from a recent tax bill or listing.
  • Round your payment up if you can. Even a small fixed extra amount, set up from the first month, shortens the loan by years, as the extra-payment table shows.
  • Match the dates. Set the first payment date to your real one so the schedule's months line up with your statements.

How it works

Enter the home price, your down payment as a % of the price, the term and the yearly rate. The calculator finds the monthly principal and interest payment and follows the loan month by month, so you can see how much of each payment is interest and how the balance falls, by year or by month. With home costs switched on, property tax (a % of the price), insurance, mortgage insurance, HOA and other yearly costs are added one twelfth a month, and the cost table shows them for one month and over the whole loan. Mortgage insurance stops once you owe 80% of the price.

The formula

  • Loan L = price × (1 − down % ÷ 100)
  • Monthly rate r = yearly rate ÷ 100 ÷ 12; payments n = years × 12
  • Monthly payment M = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n); at 0% interest M = L ÷ n
  • Each month: interest = balance × r; principal = M − interest; new balance = balance − principal
  • Balance after k payments = L × (1 + r)^k − M × ((1 + r)^k − 1) ÷ r
  • Each yearly cost is paid as one twelfth a month; property tax per year = price × tax % ÷ 100
  • PMI is paid with each payment that leaves the balance above 80% of the price

A worked example

The defaults: a $400,000 home with 20% down is a $320,000 loan. At 6.5% for 30 years, r = 0.065 ÷ 12 and n = 360, so M = $2,022.62 a month and $408,142.36 of interest over the loan. Property tax of 1.2% ($400 a month) and insurance of $1,500 a year ($125 a month) bring the monthly total to $2,547.62.

Questions

How do I calculate mortgage amortization?

Find the payment with M = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n). Then, each month, multiply the balance by the monthly rate for the interest, subtract it from the payment for the principal, and subtract the principal from the balance. Repeating that for every month gives the schedule the calculator shows.

What is a mortgage amortization schedule?

A table of every payment on the loan with how much goes to interest, how much to principal and the balance left afterwards. It shows when the loan is paid off and how much equity you have at any point.

Why does the balance fall so slowly at first?

Interest is charged on the balance you still owe, and at the start that balance is largest. On a 30-year loan most of each early payment is interest; the principal part grows every month, so the balance falls faster in later years.

What is the mortgage amortization formula in Excel?

Use =PMT(rate/12, years*12, -loan) for the payment, =IPMT and =PPMT with the payment number for the interest and principal of one month, and =CUMIPMT for the interest over a range of months. For $320,000 at 6.5% for 30 years, =PMT(6.5%/12, 360, -320000) = $2,022.62.

Can I use this for my existing mortgage?

Yes. Enter the balance you owe now as the home price, 0% down, the years left as the term and your current rate. The schedule then starts from your next payment.

When does mortgage insurance stop?

Here PMI is counted until the balance after a payment drops to 80% of the home price, when most lenders let you ask to cancel it. US rules also end it automatically at 78% of the original value if your payments are up to date.

Does the calculator include taxes and insurance?

Yes, with costs ticked under More options. Property tax is a percent of the price and insurance, PMI, HOA and other costs are yearly amounts; each is added one twelfth a month to the monthly total and over the life of the loan.

How much do extra payments save?

On a $320,000 loan at 6.5% for 30 years, $200 extra a month saves $105,428.67 of interest and ends the loan 6 years 7 months early. This page has no extra-payment box; use the mortgage payoff or mortgage calculator for that.

What is the difference between the amortization period and the mortgage term?

The amortization period is the time it takes to pay the loan off completely. In Canada and some other places the term is a shorter contract, after which you renew at a new rate. In the US the two are usually the same, such as 30 years.

Is this the same as the mortgage calculator?

It is a simpler page that puts the schedule first. Use the mortgage calculator for a down payment in money, cost rises each year, extra payments or paying every two weeks.

Are property tax and insurance part of my mortgage payment?

The headline number is principal and interest only, the part your lender sets. Many lenders also collect tax and insurance with each payment; the second number adds them so you can see what leaves your account each month.

Can I use this as a mortgage loan calculator in Pakistan?

Yes. Choose Rs as the currency and enter the price, your share as the down payment, the tenure and the bank's current rate. Floating KIBOR-linked rates change over time, so run it again with the remaining balance whenever the rate is revised.
