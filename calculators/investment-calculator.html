What an investment calculator tells you

An investment calculator shows what your money grows to when you invest a starting amount, keep adding a regular contribution and let the whole balance earn a return for a number of years. It also works the sum backwards: give it a goal and it finds the monthly contribution, the return rate, the starting amount or the time you need to get there.

The answer is a projection built on one steady return. Real investments such as shares and funds go up in some years and down in others, so read the result as one scenario. Run it again with a lower and a higher rate and you have a sensible range instead of a single number.

Every result splits the end balance into three parts: the money you started with, the contributions you added and the growth earned on both. That split is the clearest way to see how much of your future money comes from your own saving and how much comes from compounding.

How to use the investment calculator, step by step

  • Pick a tab: End amount (what you will have), Contribution (what to add), Return rate (what you must earn), Starting amount (what to put in now) or Time needed (how many years). The box for the value being solved disappears.
  • On the four goal tabs, enter Your goal: the amount you want at the end, for example 300,000.
  • Enter the Starting amount (0 is fine if you start from nothing) and the Regular contribution, then choose How often: Every month or Every year.
  • Enter Years invested (decimals such as 7.5 work) and the Return rate as a yearly percentage. A negative rate is accepted if you want to test a loss.
  • Open More options to change Compounding (Yearly by default; also twice a year, quarterly, monthly, twice a month, every two weeks, weekly, daily or continuously) and whether contributions go in at the end or at the start of each month or year.
  • Choose the currency at the top, such as US dollar or Pakistani rupee. Only the symbol changes; the maths is the same in every currency.

Understanding the results

The big number is the answer to the tab you chose: the end balance, the contribution needed, the return rate needed (to three decimals), the starting amount needed or the time needed in years, with the same time in years and months underneath. Below it you see the end balance, the starting amount, total contributions, total interest and Growth each month, which is the monthly rate the balance really grows by.

The investment formula, and how to check it in Excel

For a starting amount P and a contribution C at the end of each month, the end balance after n months is P(1 + j)^n + C((1 + j)^n − 1) ÷ j, where j is the monthly rate. The first part is the starting amount compounding on its own; the second part is the future value of a series of equal deposits. With deposits at the start of each month, the second part is multiplied by (1 + j) because every deposit earns one extra month.

This calculator turns a yearly rate into a monthly one with j = (1 + r ÷ m)^(m ÷ 12) − 1, where m is the number of compounding periods a year. With the default yearly compounding, 6% becomes 1.06^(1/12) − 1, or about 0.4868% a month, so twelve months of growth add exactly 6%.

To check a result in Excel or Google Sheets, use the FV function: FV(rate, nper, pmt, pv, type). Money you pay in is entered as a negative number. For the default example, =FV(1.06^(1/12)−1, 120, −1000, −20000, 0) returns 198,290.40, the same end balance the calculator shows. Change the last argument to 1 for deposits at the start of each month.

To work out an investment return percentage from your own numbers, divide the growth by what you put in: 58,290.40 ÷ 140,000 = 41.6% in total over the 10 years. A total like that is not a yearly rate, because the money went in at different times. For the yearly rate that fits a series of deposits, enter the starting amount, the contribution, the years and the end balance as the goal on the Return rate tab.

Worked examples

Growth: $20,000 to start, $1,000 at the end of each month, 10 years at 6% compounded yearly. The end balance is $198,290.40, of which $58,290.40 is growth. Moving the deposits to the start of each month raises it to $199,081.24, and switching to monthly compounding of the same 6% gives $200,267.28, because 6% compounded monthly grows like about 6.17% a year.

A goal of $300,000 with the same inputs, solved four ways: you need $1,626.01 a month instead of $1,000; or a return of 12.901% a year; or a starting amount of $76,794.11; or about 13.856 years (13 years 11 months) with $1,000 a month at 6%. Seeing the four answers side by side makes the trade-off clear: time and the size of your deposits are usually more in your control than the return.

A monthly plan in Pakistani rupees (often called a SIP, a systematic investment plan): Rs 10,000 at the end of each month for 15 years puts in Rs 1,800,000. The table shows what it could grow to at three steady yearly returns with yearly compounding, which are examples, not forecasts.

How time, rate and compounding change your investment growth

Compounding means growth earns growth. Over short periods the return rate hardly seems to matter, but over decades it dominates the result. The table follows a single $10,000 lump sum with no further deposits, compounded yearly.

A quick check is the Rule of 72 described by the SEC's Investor.gov: divide 72 by the yearly return to estimate the years it takes money to double. At 6% that gives 12 years, close to the exact 11.9.

Compounding frequency matters far less than people expect. A quoted 6% grows by 6.000% a year compounded yearly, 6.090% twice a year, 6.136% quarterly, 6.168% monthly, 6.183% daily and 6.184% continuously. Pick the option that matches how your account or product actually credits returns; for most funds, yearly compounding of an expected yearly return is the honest choice.

Choosing a realistic return rate

The return you type in should match the kind of investment you have in mind. The SEC's beginners' guide to asset allocation describes cash equivalents such as savings deposits and certificates of deposit (CDs) as the safest group with the lowest returns, bonds as steadier than shares with more modest returns, and shares (stocks) as the most volatile group, which has rewarded investors who could hold them for long periods.

Bank savings and CDs in the US are protected by FDIC insurance up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each ownership category, so the rate is close to certain but usually low. Bonds pay interest but their prices move with interest rates. Stock funds can fall sharply in a bad year, so a steady 6% to 8% in the calculator is an average over many years, never a yearly guarantee.

Fees come straight out of the return. In the SEC's example, $100,000 earning 4% a year for 20 years ends near $208,000 with a 0.25% yearly fee but only about $179,000 with a 1% fee. Enter the return after fees, and remember that taxes and inflation lower what you can spend later.

  • Use a lower rate for cash and short-term deposits, a middle rate for bonds or mixed funds, and test both a low and a high rate for shares.
  • To see the real value of an end balance, subtract inflation from the rate (6% return with 3% inflation is roughly 3% real growth) or check the result with the Inflation Calculator.
  • Do not use the best recent year of a fund as your rate. A long-run average after fees is a fairer base.

Investing in Pakistan: PKR, SIPs and mutual funds

Choose Rs Pakistani rupee in the currency menu and the calculator works the same way. A monthly SIP in a mutual fund is simply the Regular contribution with How often set to Every month; a one-off deposit in a savings certificate or term deposit is the Starting amount with a contribution of 0.

Returns in rupees have to be judged against prices in rupees. The Pakistan Bureau of Statistics puts average consumer price inflation at 23.41% in fiscal year 2023-24, 4.49% in 2024-25 and 7.05% in 2025-26, and 9.6% for October 2026 against a year earlier. A 12% return is a strong real gain when inflation is 4% and almost nothing when it is 10% or more.

The State Bank of Pakistan raised its policy rate to 12% from 27 October 2026, and bank deposit and lending rates broadly follow it. That makes the policy rate a useful yardstick: a low-risk product paying well below it, or a fund promising far above it with no risk, deserves a closer look. Both figures change, so check the latest release before you choose a rate.

Mutual funds in Pakistan are run by asset management companies licensed by the Securities and Exchange Commission of Pakistan (SECP). Its investor education site, Jamapunji, explains how to check a company's licence, how fund categories such as money market, income and equity funds differ, and how the expense ratio is charged. Profit rates on bank accounts and National Savings schemes change often, so take today's rate from the bank or from Central Directorate of National Savings before you run the numbers.

Tips and common mistakes

  • Start early. Ten extra years at 6% roughly doubles a lump sum on its own, which no realistic rate increase can match.
  • Match the contribution timing to reality: a salary deduction at month end is End of each month; money invested on the first day of each year is Start of each year.
  • Do not mix a monthly contribution with a yearly figure. 12,000 a year and 1,000 a month give slightly different results because of when the money goes in.
  • Check the Time needed tab before raising your risk. Waiting one or two more years is often safer than chasing a higher return.
  • If the Return rate tab gives a negative answer, your goal is smaller than the money you plan to put in, so you can lower the contributions or raise the goal.
  • Rerun the plan once a year with your real balance as the new starting amount. Projections drift; updated numbers do not.

How it works

The first tab shows what your money grows to. The other tabs work backwards from a goal: how much to add each month or year, what return you would need, how much to start with, or how long it takes. Behind every tab the balance grows month by month: the yearly return is turned into the monthly rate that gives the same growth with the compounding you choose, and contributions go in at the start or end of each month (or of each year). The tabs that solve for a value search for the one that makes the end balance land exactly on your goal.

The formula

  • Monthly rate j = (1 + r ÷ m)^(m ÷ 12) − 1, with m = compounding periods a year; continuous: j = e^(r ÷ 12) − 1
  • Each month: balance = (balance + deposit at the start) × (1 + j) + deposit at the end
  • Monthly contributions: end balance = P(1 + j)^n + C((1 + j)^n − 1) ÷ j, × (1 + j) on the C part for deposits at the start; n = years × 12
  • Contribution needed C = (goal − P(1 + j)^n) ÷ the C factor; starting amount needed P = (goal − the C part) ÷ (1 + j)^n
  • Return rate and time needed: found by narrowing the value down until the end balance equals the goal
  • Total interest = end balance − starting amount − total contributions
  • In a spreadsheet: =FV(j, n, −C, −P, 0) gives the same end balance (type 1 for deposits at the start)

A worked example

Start with $20,000 and add $1,000 at the end of each month for 10 years at 6% compounded yearly. The monthly rate is 1.06^(1/12) − 1 ≈ 0.4868%, so month 1 earns $97.35 on the $20,000. After 120 months the balance is $198,290.40: $20,000 from you at the start, $120,000 of contributions and $58,290.40 of growth.

Questions

What is an investment calculator?

It is a tool that projects how a starting amount and regular contributions grow at a yearly return over a number of years. This one also solves backwards from a goal, so you can find the contribution, return rate, starting amount or time you need.

How do I calculate investment growth over time?

Grow the starting amount by (1 + monthly rate) for each month and add each contribution as it goes in; the growth is the end balance minus everything you put in. The calculator does this month by month and lists every year in the Year by year schedule.

What is the formula for an investment with monthly contributions?

End balance = P(1 + j)^n + C((1 + j)^n − 1) ÷ j, where P is the starting amount, C the monthly contribution, j the monthly rate and n the number of months. For deposits at the start of each month, multiply the contribution part by (1 + j).

How do I calculate an investment in Excel?

Use =FV(rate, nper, pmt, pv, type) with the monthly rate, the number of months, the contribution and the starting amount entered as negative numbers. =FV(1.06^(1/12)−1, 120, −1000, −20000, 0) gives 198,290.40, matching the calculator's default result.

How do I calculate investment return percentage?

Total return is the growth divided by what you put in, so 58,290.40 ÷ 140,000 is 41.6% over the whole period. For a yearly rate on a single lump sum use (end ÷ start)^(1 ÷ years) − 1; with regular deposits, use the Return rate tab with the end balance as the goal.

What return rate should I use?

Use a rate you can expect after fees for the kind of investment you have in mind. Savings accounts and bonds return less than shares, and shares move up and down from year to year. Try a low and a high rate to see the range.

What does compounding change?

It says how often the yearly return is added to the balance. More frequent compounding of the same quoted rate grows a little faster: 6% compounded monthly grows like 6.17% a year. With yearly compounding the 6% is the actual yearly growth.

Should contributions go at the start or the end of each month?

Choose the one that matches when your money really goes in. Deposits at the start earn one extra month of growth each, so the end balance is a little higher: $199,081.24 instead of $198,290.40 in the default example.

Can I use it as a SIP or investment calculator in Pakistan?

Yes. Pick Rs Pakistani rupee as the currency, enter the monthly SIP as the regular contribution and use a return you can justify for the fund type. Compare that rate with current inflation from the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics, because high rupee returns can shrink a lot after inflation.

Why does month 1 show the starting amount as a deposit?

The schedule counts the starting amount as money put in during month 1, so the deposit column adds up to everything you put in. The totals above the schedule show the starting amount and the contributions separately.

What if the return rate needed is negative?

Your goal is less than the money you put in, so you would reach it even while losing money. That usually means the goal can be raised or the contributions lowered.

Can the years have decimals?

Yes. 7.5 years is 90 months. If the years end part way through a month, that last part month earns its share of the month's growth.
