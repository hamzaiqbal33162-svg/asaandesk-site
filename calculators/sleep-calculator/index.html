What the sleep calculator tells you

This sleep calculator suggests bedtimes for the time you need to wake up, or wake-up times for the time you go to bed, so that you sleep in whole sleep cycles. It adds the minutes you usually take to fall asleep and lists the options from 1 to 7 cycles, with the two that suit most adults at the top.

A second tab works the plain way: when to go to bed for a set number of hours, such as 8 hours, and how many cycles that is. The results are an estimate based on average cycles, not medical advice.

How to use the sleep calculator

  • Pick a tab: By sleep cycles, or By hours of sleep.
  • I want to: choose Wake up at if you know when you must get up, or Go to bed at if you know your bedtime.
  • Time: type the time, like 6:30, and pick AM or PM, or type a 24-hour time such as 22:30.
  • Time to fall asleep (cycles tab): the minutes you usually lie awake first. The default is 15; most people take 10 to 20.
  • Hours of sleep (hours tab): the amount you want, in hours and minutes.
  • Under More options you can change the length of one sleep cycle from the usual 90 minutes.

Sleep cycles and stages

Sleep is not one even state. According to the US National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI), the night is made of two phases: non-REM sleep, with stage 1 (the change from waking to sleep), stage 2 (asleep) and stage 3 (deep or slow-wave sleep), and REM sleep, when the eyes twitch, the brain is active, the muscles go limp and most dreaming happens.

The cycle through these stages starts over every 80 to 100 minutes, and there are usually four to six cycles a night. Early cycles hold more deep sleep and later ones more REM sleep. The calculator uses 90 minutes, the middle of that range; if your own cycles seem longer or shorter, change the cycle length. With 100-minute cycles, for example, a 7:00 AM wake-up gives bedtimes of 10:25 PM (5 cycles, 8 hours 20 minutes) or 8:45 PM (6 cycles, 10 hours).

Timing your alarm for the end of a cycle is meant to avoid waking in the middle of deep sleep. Real cycles vary from night to night, so treat the times as a guide, not an exact schedule.

How to calculate sleep hours, sleep debt and sleep efficiency

Sleep hours are the time from falling asleep to waking. On the hours tab, going to bed at 11:30 PM for 8 hours gives a wake-up time of 7:30 AM; the calculator also notes that 8 hours is 5.33 cycles and suggests 7 hours 30 minutes (7:00 AM) or 9 hours (8:30 AM) for whole cycles.

Sleep debt is the gap between the sleep you need and the sleep you get, added up over several nights. If you need 7 hours 30 minutes and sleep 6 hours 30 minutes on five work nights, you are 5 hours short by the weekend.

Sleep efficiency compares the time asleep with the time in bed: sleep efficiency = time asleep ÷ time in bed × 100. In bed from 11:00 PM to 6:30 AM is 7 hours 30 minutes (450 minutes); if you were asleep for 6 hours 45 minutes (405 minutes), the efficiency is 405 ÷ 450 × 100 = 90%. The minutes you take to fall asleep are called sleep latency, which is the "time to fall asleep" box here.

Naps, shift work and travel

For a nap, use Go to bed at with the time you lie down and read the 1 cycle line: lying down at 1:30 PM with 10 minutes to fall asleep gives a wake-up time of 3:10 PM after one 90-minute cycle. Set an alarm so the nap does not run into a second cycle.

Shift workers can use the calculator at any hour: type the time you must get up, even if it is in the afternoon or evening, and the bedtimes follow. When travelling across time zones, type the wake-up time you need in the local time of where you will be.

Tips for better sleep

  • Keep the same bedtime and wake-up time every day. The CDC lists a regular schedule among its basic tips.
  • Stop using phones, tablets and other screens at least 30 minutes before bed, as the CDC advises, and keep the bedroom quiet, relaxing and cool.
  • Avoid large meals, alcohol and caffeine late in the day, and get regular exercise.
  • If you often feel tired after a full night, snore loudly, gasp for air or stop breathing in your sleep, see a doctor: these can be signs of sleep apnea, which the NHLBI describes as breathing that stops and restarts many times during sleep.
  • For adults the CDC target is 7 or more hours a night, so aim for 5 or 6 cycles on most nights.

How it works

Sleep comes in cycles of light sleep, deep sleep and dream (REM) sleep that last about 90 minutes each, and waking at the end of a cycle tends to feel easier than waking in the middle of one. Choose whether you know your wake-up time or your bedtime and type it. The calculator counts back (or forward) whole cycles, adding the minutes you usually need to fall asleep, and lists the times for 1 to 7 cycles. Five or six cycles, about 7.5 to 9 hours, suit most adults. The second tab simply works out the bedtime or wake-up time for a set amount of sleep.

The formula

  • Bedtime = wake-up time − time to fall asleep − cycles × cycle length
  • Wake-up time = bedtime + time to fall asleep + cycles × cycle length
  • Set amount of sleep: bedtime = wake-up time − sleep, or wake-up time = bedtime + sleep
  • Times past midnight wrap around the 24-hour clock (12 AM is midnight)

A worked example

To wake up at 6:00 AM with 15 minutes to fall asleep and 90-minute cycles: 5 cycles are 7 hours 30 minutes, so go to bed at 6:00 AM − 15 minutes − 7 hours 30 minutes = 10:15 PM. Six cycles (9 hours) give 8:45 PM.

Questions

What time should I go to bed to wake up at 6:30 AM?

With 15 minutes to fall asleep and 90-minute cycles, go to bed at 10:45 PM for 5 cycles (7 hours 30 minutes of sleep) or 9:15 PM for 6 cycles (9 hours). Change the time and the minutes to fall asleep to match your own night.

Why 90 minutes?

During the night the brain moves through light sleep, deep sleep and REM sleep again and again. The NHLBI says one round takes about 80 to 100 minutes, so 90 minutes is a good middle value. If you know your own cycles are shorter or longer, change the cycle length under More options.

How do I calculate my sleep cycle?

Count back from your wake-up time in steps of one cycle (about 90 minutes), then take off the time you need to fall asleep. Five cycles from 6:00 AM with 15 minutes to fall asleep is 10:15 PM. The calculator lists every option from 1 to 7 cycles.

Which time should I pick?

The two times at the top, 5 or 6 full cycles, give 7.5 to 9 hours of sleep, which fits the 7 or more hours most adults need. Four cycles (6 hours) is a short night, and 1 or 2 cycles suit a nap more than a night's sleep.

How much sleep do I need for my age?

The CDC lists 7 or more hours for adults aged 18 to 60, 8 to 10 hours for teens aged 13 to 17, 9 to 12 hours for children aged 6 to 12, and more for younger children, including naps. Adults 65 and older are listed at 7 to 8 hours.

How do I calculate sleep efficiency?

Divide the time you were asleep by the time you spent in bed and multiply by 100. Asleep for 6 hours 45 minutes out of 7 hours 30 minutes in bed is 90%. A sleep tracker or a sleep diary gives the time asleep.

How do I calculate sleep debt?

Take the hours you slept each night away from the hours you need and add up the shortfalls. Needing 7.5 hours but getting 6.5 hours for five nights is a sleep debt of 5 hours.

What does time to fall asleep mean?

Most people take 10 to 20 minutes to drift off after lying down; doctors call this sleep latency. That time is added before the first cycle starts, so the bedtimes are when to be in bed, not when to be asleep.

Does a sleep calculator really work?

It gives a sensible starting point: whole cycles of about 90 minutes and enough total sleep. Real cycles vary between people and nights, so try the suggested time for a week and adjust the cycle length or the minutes to fall asleep if you still wake groggy.

Is this medical advice?

No. It is a simple guide based on average sleep cycles. If you often feel tired after a full night, snore loudly or wake many times, talk to a doctor.
