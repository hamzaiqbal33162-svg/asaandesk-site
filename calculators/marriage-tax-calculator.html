How it works

Enter each person's yearly wages or business income and any pre-tax retirement savings, the filing status each of you would use if not married, and any children. More income types, deductions, other dependents, ages and state tax rates are under the sections below. The calculator works out three federal returns with the same IRS rules: one for each of you as a single filer (or head of household), and one married filing jointly with everything combined. It compares income tax after credits, Social Security, Medicare with the 0.9% Additional Medicare tax, and the 3.8% investment income tax, and shows the difference as a marriage penalty (more tax) or a marriage bonus (less tax).

The formula

  • Each return: taxable income = income − pre-tax savings (− half of self-employment tax) − standard or itemized deduction (− QBI deduction)
  • Income tax = bracket tax (0/15/20% on qualified dividends and long-term gains) − child tax credit ($500 per other dependent) − earned income credit
  • Social Security = 6.2% of wages up to the wage base; Medicare = 1.45% + 0.9% above $200,000 single or $250,000 for a married couple
  • Penalty (+) or bonus (−) = federal tax married − (your tax single + partner's tax single)

A worked example

In 2026 one partner earns $100,000 and the other nothing. Alone, the earner's taxable income is $100,000 − $16,100 = $83,900, with $13,170 of tax. Married, it is $100,000 − $32,200 = $67,800, with $7,640 of tax, so marriage saves $5,530 a year (a bonus).

Questions

What is a marriage penalty?

It is the extra tax a couple pays on a joint return compared with what they would pay as two single people. It mostly shows up at very high incomes (the 37% bracket starts well below twice the single amount), for the 0.9% Medicare and 3.8% investment taxes, and for people who lose the earned income credit or head of household status.

Why do many couples get a marriage bonus?

Joint brackets and the joint standard deduction are twice the single ones for most incomes. When one partner earns much more than the other, the joint return spreads that income over the wider brackets, so the couple pays less than the higher earner would alone.

Does marriage change Social Security or state tax here?

Social Security is worked out per person on wages, so it stays the same. State tax uses the flat rate you enter for each person. Medicare can change: the 0.9% extra tax starts at $250,000 for a couple, not two times $200,000.

Why can the income tax be a minus number?

Refundable credits such as the additional child tax credit and the earned income credit can be more than the tax, and the IRS pays the rest. That often happens for a single parent filing as head of household, and it is a big reason marriage can cost such a parent money.
