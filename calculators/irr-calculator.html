What the IRR calculator tells you

An IRR calculator finds the internal rate of return of an investment: the single yearly rate of return that its cash flows work out to, once you account for when each amount was paid or received. It turns a project, a property, a business deal or a savings policy into one percentage you can compare with a bank rate, a loan rate or another project.

Formally, the IRR is the rate that brings the net present value (NPV) of all the cash flows down to exactly zero. If you could put your money in an account paying the IRR, the account would produce exactly the same cash flows as the investment. An investment whose IRR beats the return you require, often called the hurdle rate or cost of capital, adds value; one below it does not.

How to use the IRR calculator

There are two modes. Pick the one that matches how your cash flows look.

  • Yearly cash flows: enter the Money put in at the start (year 0), then the Cash flow in year 1, 2, 3 ... separated by commas, such as 12,000, 15,000, 18,000, 20,000. Type a minus sign for a year when you put more money in, and 0 for a year with nothing in or out, so the years stay in order.
  • Regular payments: enter the Money put in at the start, how long you held it (years and extra months) and the Value at the end. Then the Regular amount, whether each one was Taken out or Added, and How often: every year, twice a year, every quarter, every month, twice a month, every 2 weeks or every week.
  • Under Regular payments you can choose whether each payment comes at the End of each period (the usual case) or the Start of each period.
  • Choose your currency at the top; it changes only the symbol.

The IRR formula and how to calculate IRR by hand

The IRR formula is the NPV formula set to zero: 0 = CF0 + CF1 ÷ (1 + IRR) + CF2 ÷ (1 + IRR)² + … + CFn ÷ (1 + IRR)ⁿ, where CF0 is the money put in at the start (a negative number) and CF1 to CFn are the later cash flows. There is no algebraic way to solve it for more than a couple of periods, so it is found by trial: guess a rate, work out the NPV, and adjust.

Take the default project: 50,000 in, then 12,000, 15,000, 18,000 and 20,000. At 10% the NPV is +489.72, so the rate is too low. At 11% it is −678.79, too high. Linear interpolation between the two gives 10% + 489.72 ÷ (489.72 + 678.79) × 1% = 10.419%, very close to the exact answer of 10.415% that the calculator finds by repeating the process until the NPV is zero.

The same steps give the IRR formula used in capital budgeting exams: IRR ≈ r1 + NPV1 × (r2 − r1) ÷ (NPV1 − NPV2), where r1 gives a positive NPV and r2 a negative one. The closer the two trial rates, the more accurate the estimate.

How to calculate IRR in Excel or on a financial calculator

In Excel, list the cash flows in order in one column, the first one negative, and type =IRR(B2:B6). Excel needs at least one negative and one positive value, starts from a guess of 10% and returns #NUM! if it cannot settle on an answer within 20 tries; give it a different guess, such as =IRR(B2:B6, 0.2), if that happens. For the default project, =IRR({-50000, 12000, 15000, 18000, 20000}) returns 10.415%.

When the cash flows are not exactly one year apart, use =XIRR(values, dates), which works from the actual dates. For monthly cash flows, IRR gives a monthly rate; turn it into a yearly one with =(1+IRR(range))^12-1, which is what this page's regular payments mode does.

MIRR, the modified internal rate of return, is =MIRR(values, finance_rate, reinvest_rate). It assumes money paid out is financed at the finance rate and money received is reinvested at the reinvest rate, instead of at the IRR itself. At 8% for both, the default project's MIRR is 9.56%, a little lower than its 10.415% IRR.

On a BA II Plus, enter the cash flows in the cash flow worksheet (CF0, then C01, C02 and so on) and compute IRR. On a TI-84, the Finance menu has an irr( function that takes the start amount and a list of the later cash flows. Both give the same answer as this page.

Worked examples

Choosing between two projects. Both cost 60,000 and both return 90,000 in total over three years. Project A returns 40,000, 30,000 and 20,000; Project B returns 20,000, 30,000 and 40,000. A simple return on investment calls them equal at 50%, but A's IRR is 26.819% against 20.614% for B, because A pays you back sooner. At an 8% hurdle rate, A's NPV is 18,634 and B's is 15,992.

The true rate on a flat-rate loan. A lender in Pakistan advertises a loan of Rs 1,000,000 at a flat 10% a year for 3 years: Rs 300,000 of interest, repaid as 36 monthly installments of Rs 36,111.11. In the regular payments mode, enter Rs 1,000,000 at the start, 3 years, 0 at the end and Rs 36,111.11 taken out every month. The IRR is 19.465% a year (1.4931% a month), almost double the flat rate, because you pay interest on the full amount even as the balance falls. The same method works for vehicle and personal loans quoted on a flat basis.

A savings insurance policy. You pay a premium of Rs 100,000 at the start of each year for 10 years and receive Rs 1,500,000 at the end of year 10. Enter 100,000 at the start, then -100,000 for years 1 to 9 and 1,500,000 for year 10. You put in Rs 1,000,000 and get Rs 500,000 more back, a 50% gain, but the IRR is only 7.257% a year, which is the figure to compare with a term deposit.

Limits of IRR and when to use NPV instead

  • Scale: IRR ignores size. Turning 10,000 into 15,000 in a year is a 50% IRR; turning 1,000,000 into 1,200,000 is 20%. At an 8% cost of capital, the small deal adds 3,889 of value and the big one 111,111, so the lower IRR is the better project if you can fund it.
  • Reinvestment: IRR implicitly assumes the cash you receive along the way can be reinvested at the IRR itself. For high-IRR projects that is optimistic, and MIRR with a realistic reinvestment rate gives a more cautious figure.
  • Multiple rates: when the cash flows change sign more than once, more than one rate can fit. Paying 100,000, receiving 230,000 a year later and paying 132,000 the year after has IRRs of both 10% and 20%. The calculator shows the one nearest 0% and warns you; NPV at your required rate is the clearer test.
  • Risk: IRR says nothing about how certain the cash flows are. Compare it with a hurdle rate that reflects the risk of the project.

Tips and related tools

  • Put every cash flow in, including fees, taxes and the sale price or final value at the end.
  • Keep the timing right: a year with no cash flow still needs a 0 so the later years do not shift.
  • For real estate, include the purchase price and costs at year 0, each year's net rent after expenses, and the net sale proceeds in the last year.
  • Quote IRR as a yearly effective rate so it compares directly with deposit rates and loan APRs.

How it works

Every cash flow gets a time and a sign: money you put in counts as negative, money you get back as positive. The internal rate of return is the one yearly rate at which all of those amounts, each discounted back to the start, add up to exactly zero. With yearly cash flows the rate is found directly. With regular payments the calculator finds the rate per payment period first and then turns it into an effective yearly rate, so a monthly rate of 1% becomes 12.683% a year.

The formula

  • Find r so that 0 = −start amount + CF1 ÷ (1 + r) + CF2 ÷ (1 + r)² + … + CFn ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ
  • Regular payments: p payments a year, rate per period i, then IRR = (1 + i)^p − 1
  • End of period: payments at periods 1, 2, … n; start of period: at periods 0, 1, … n − 1
  • Total gain = everything you got back − everything you put in; gain % = total gain ÷ money put in

A worked example

Put in $1,000 and get back $500, $400, $300 and $200 over four years. At r = 17.805% the four amounts are worth $424.43, $288.23, $183.50 and $103.84 today, which add up to the $1,000 you put in, so the IRR is 17.805% a year. The total gain is $400, or 40% of the money put in.

Questions

What is IRR in finance?

The internal rate of return is the yearly rate at which the present value of an investment's cash inflows equals the money put in, so the net present value is zero. It is the investment's own rate of return, taking timing into account.

How do I calculate IRR?

Write each cash flow with its year, money in as negative, then find the rate that makes their discounted sum zero by trying rates and narrowing down. This calculator, Excel's =IRR() and financial calculators all do it automatically; by hand, interpolate between a rate with a positive NPV and one with a negative NPV.

How do I calculate IRR in Excel?

Put the cash flows in a column with the first one negative and use =IRR(range). Use =XIRR(values, dates) when the cash flows are on irregular dates, and convert a monthly IRR to yearly with (1 + rate)^12 − 1.

What does the IRR tell me?

It is the steady yearly rate your money earned, taking into account when each amount went in or came out. If a savings account paying that rate would have given you exactly the same cash flows, the two are equally good.

What is a good IRR?

One above your hurdle rate: the return you could get elsewhere for similar risk, or your cost of borrowing. A project with a 15% IRR is attractive if your money costs 8%, and not if safe deposits pay 16%.

How do I find the real interest rate on a loan with IRR?

Use the regular payments mode: the loan amount at the start, each installment as a regular amount taken out, and 0 at the end. The IRR is the effective yearly rate you are paying, which for flat-rate loans is far above the advertised flat rate.

How do I enter a year when I put more money in?

Type that year's amount with a minus sign, for example -10000. Money you received is typed as a plain number. A year with nothing in or out is 0, so the years stay in order.

Why does it say there is no IRR?

A rate can only balance the cash flows if money goes both ways. If every amount is money put in, or every amount is money received, no rate makes them add up to zero.

What if more than one rate fits?

When the cash flows switch between in and out more than once, two or more rates can balance them. The calculator shows the one closest to 0% and warns you; the net present value at your own required rate is often the clearer test in that case.

Is the yearly rate a simple or an effective rate?

Effective. With monthly payments the monthly rate is compounded twelve times, (1 + i)^12 − 1, so it compares directly with yearly rates elsewhere.
