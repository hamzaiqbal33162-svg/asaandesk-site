What is a home equity loan, and how does it work?

A home equity loan lets you borrow against the equity in your home, which is what the home is worth minus what you still owe on it. You receive the money as one lump sum and repay it in equal monthly payments over a fixed term, usually at a fixed interest rate. Because the home is the collateral, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau warns that if you cannot pay the loan back, the lender could foreclose.

It is often called a second mortgage, because it sits behind your first mortgage on the same home. This home equity loan calculator does two jobs: the first tab estimates how much you may be able to borrow, and the second works out the monthly payment, the total interest, the closing costs and the APR, with a yearly and monthly schedule. Besides your equity, lenders look at your credit score, your income and your other debts, and they order their own appraisal of the home before setting the final amount.

How to use the home equity loan calculator

  • How much can I borrow? tab: enter the Home value today, the Mortgage still owed (all loans on the home, or 0 if it is paid off), and the limit under Lender lends up to: 90%, 85%, 80%, 75%, 70% or 60% of the value, all loans together.
  • Monthly payment tab: enter the Amount you borrow, the Interest rate in % a year and the Loan term in years (up to 40).
  • Tick Include closing costs to add them, as a % of the loan or in dollars (2% to start), and choose whether the Closing costs are Taken out of the loan or Paid in cash.
  • Under Schedule, set the First payment date so that every row of the schedule has a real month.
  • Read the results as you type; the yearly schedule is open and the monthly schedule opens with one click.

How to calculate how much home equity you can borrow

The limit is set by the combined loan-to-value ratio (CLTV): all the loans on the home divided by its value. Multiply the value by the lender's CLTV limit, then subtract what you already owe. On a $500,000 home with $250,000 owed, the answer depends on the lender:

The value used is the lender's appraisal, not your own estimate, so a lower appraisal lowers the limit dollar for dollar. Borrowing the maximum also leaves little cushion if home prices fall, and you would owe more than the home is worth if they fell far enough.

Home equity loan payments by term, and worked examples

The term changes the payment and the total interest more than anything else. For $100,000 at 8%, a shorter loan costs much more each month but far less in total. If you are comparing home equity loan rates for 10, 20 or 30 years, run each rate with its own term, because lenders often charge different rates for different terms.

Closing costs and the APR. With 2% closing costs ($2,000) on the 15-year loan, the payment stays $955.65, the cost of the loan becomes $74,017.38 and the APR is 8.336%. Whether the $2,000 is taken out of the loan (you receive $98,000) or paid in cash (you receive $100,000 and pay $2,000 yourself), the payment and APR are the same, because the loan amount does not change.

Consolidating card debt. Suppose you owe $30,000 on credit cards at 22.99%. Clearing that in 10 years would take $640.44 a month and $46,852.40 of interest. A $30,000 home equity loan at 8% for 10 years costs $363.98 a month and $13,677.93 of interest, plus closing costs. The saving is real, but the debt is now secured by your home, and it only works if the cards are not run up again.

Closing costs, fees and your right to cancel

Home equity loans can carry closing costs similar to a mortgage: origination or application fees, an appraisal, a credit report, title search and title insurance, recording fees and sometimes points. Some lenders advertise no closing costs and make up for it with a higher rate or a fee if you repay early, so compare the APR and the total Cost of the loan, not just the monthly payment, as the CFPB also suggests.

Under Regulation Z (12 CFR 1026.23), when a loan is secured by your principal dwelling and is not the loan you used to buy or build it, you have a right of rescission: you can cancel until midnight of the third business day after the latest of closing, receiving the rescission notice and receiving the required disclosures. If the notice or the material disclosures were never given, that right can last up to 3 years.

Is home equity loan interest tax deductible?

In the US, only in some cases. IRS Publication 936 (for 2025 returns) says interest on a loan secured by your home is deductible only if the money is used to buy, build or substantially improve the home that secures the loan, and only on up to $750,000 of total home debt ($375,000 if married filing separately); higher limits of $1 million and $500,000 apply to debt taken out before 16 December 2017. You also have to itemize deductions to claim it.

So a home equity loan used for a new roof or a kitchen remodel on the same home may qualify, while one used to pay off credit cards, buy a car or pay tuition does not. Tax rules change, so check the current Publication 936 or a tax adviser before counting on the deduction. For scale, the $100,000 loan at 8% over 15 years charges $7,869.98 of interest in its first year; for someone who itemizes in the 22% federal bracket and used the money on the home, a full deduction would cut the tax bill by about $1,731 that year.

Home equity loan vs HELOC vs cash-out refinance

  • Home equity loan: one lump sum, usually a fixed rate and fixed payments. Best for a known, one-time cost such as a renovation with a fixed quote.
  • HELOC (home equity line of credit): a credit line you draw on as needed. The CFPB explains that it usually has a variable rate, a draw period during which some plans allow interest-only payments, and a repayment period with often much higher payments. Use the HELOC calculator for it.
  • Cash-out refinance: replaces your first mortgage with a bigger one and pays you the difference. It can make sense when current mortgage rates are lower than your existing rate, but it resets the whole mortgage and comes with its own closing costs.
  • Unsecured personal loan: no risk to the home and often faster, but often at a higher rate and for a smaller amount.
  • Canada: banks regulated by OSFI limit the revolving, non-amortizing part of a home equity line to 65% of the home's value; any credit above that must be amortized. Texas also has its own constitutional rules for home equity lending (Article XVI, Section 50), so ask a Texas lender how they apply.

Tips before you borrow, and related calculators

  • Borrow for things that last or save money, such as repairs, energy upgrades or replacing much higher-rate debt, rather than everyday spending.
  • Keep the payment comfortable: add it to your mortgage and other debts and compare the total with your income before tax.
  • Get quotes from several lenders on the same day, with the same amount and term, and compare APRs.
  • Ask whether there is a fee for early repayment, and whether the rate is fixed for the whole term.
  • Leave some equity untouched as a cushion against falling prices or a sudden move.

How it works

First tab: lenders let all the loans on your home together reach a share of its value, often 80% or 85% (the combined loan-to-value). Take that share of the value and subtract what you still owe on the mortgage; what is left is roughly the most you can borrow. Second tab: a home equity loan is paid back in equal monthly payments over a fixed term, like a mortgage. The payment comes from the standard loan formula, and the schedule shows how each payment splits into interest and principal. If you add closing costs, they are either taken out of the loan (you receive less) or paid in cash. Either way the APR is the yearly rate at which your payments are worth what you really got, the loan minus the costs.

The formula

  • Most you may borrow = lender's limit % × home value − mortgage owed (nothing if what you owe is already at or above the limit)
  • Monthly payment M = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), with r = yearly rate ÷ 12 and n = years × 12
  • Total interest = M × n − L; cost of the loan = total interest + closing costs
  • APR: the monthly rate a with M × (1 − (1 + a)^−n) ÷ a = L − closing costs, times 12

A worked example

With the starting figures, a home worth $500,000 with $250,000 still owed and a lender limit of 80%: 80% × $500,000 = $400,000, minus $250,000, so you may borrow up to $150,000. Borrowing $100,000 at 8% for 15 years costs $955.65 a month and $72,017.38 of interest. With 2% ($2,000) closing costs, you really get $98,000 of value from the loan and the APR is 8.336%.

Questions

How do I calculate a home equity loan payment?

Use the loan formula M = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), with L the amount, r the yearly rate ÷ 12 and n the number of months. For $100,000 at 8% over 15 years that is $955.65 a month. The Monthly payment tab does this and adds the closing costs and APR.

How much equity can I borrow?

Lenders commonly let all loans on the home together reach 80% to 85% of its value, and some up to 90%. With a $500,000 home and $250,000 owed, an 80% lender would allow about $150,000. Your credit score, income and debts also count.

How do I calculate the home equity loan amount I qualify for?

Multiply the appraised value by the lender's combined loan-to-value limit and subtract everything you owe on the home. If the answer is 0 or less, that lender cannot offer a home equity loan until you have more equity or the home is worth more.

What is a home equity loan vs a HELOC?

A home equity loan pays you one lump sum with a fixed rate and fixed payments. A HELOC is a credit line you draw on as needed, usually with interest-only payments at first and a rate that can change. Try our HELOC calculator for that.

Why is the APR higher than the interest rate?

Closing costs mean you get less value than you pay interest on. The APR spreads those costs over the loan, so it shows the true yearly cost. Without closing costs the two are the same.

Should the closing costs come out of the loan or be paid in cash?

The monthly payment and the APR are the same either way here, because the loan amount does not change. Taken out of the loan, you simply receive less cash; paid in cash, you receive the full amount.

How do I calculate home equity loan interest?

Each month's interest is the balance times the yearly rate ÷ 12, so the first month of a $100,000 loan at 8% is about $666.67. Over the whole loan, total interest = payment × number of months − amount borrowed. The schedule shows the interest for every month and year.

Is home equity loan interest tax deductible?

Only if the money is used to buy, build or substantially improve the home that secures the loan, within the $750,000 home-debt limit, and only if you itemize, according to IRS Publication 936 for 2025. Interest on money used for other purposes is not deductible.

Can I make extra payments on a home equity loan?

Usually yes, but ask the lender about any fee for paying early. To see the effect of extra payments on the schedule, use the amortization calculator with the same amount, rate and term and add the extras there.

Is a home equity loan risky?

It is secured by your home, so if you stop paying, the lender can foreclose. Borrow only what you can repay, and compare with an unsecured loan for smaller amounts.
