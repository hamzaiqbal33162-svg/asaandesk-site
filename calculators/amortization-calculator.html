What is amortization, and what does an amortization calculator show?

Amortization is the way an installment loan is paid down: you pay the same amount every month, each payment covers the interest for that month plus a slice of the amount borrowed, and the balance reaches exactly zero with the last payment. An amortization calculator works out that fixed payment and builds the amortization schedule, a table with one row per payment that shows the interest part, the principal part and the balance left afterwards.

Home loans, car loans, personal loans, most student loans and fixed-rate home equity loans all work this way. Knowing the schedule tells you what the loan really costs, how fast you build ownership in the house or car, how much you still owe if you sell early, and how much an extra payment would save.

The word has a second meaning in accounting: spreading the cost of an intangible asset, such as a patent or bought goodwill, over the years it is used. That meaning is explained in its own section below. The calculator itself handles the loan meaning.

How to use the amortization calculator, step by step

  • Loan amount: the sum you actually borrow, which is the price minus your down payment or trade-in, not the full price.
  • Loan term (in years, up to 50) and Interest rate (in % a year, to three decimals). Use the nominal yearly rate written in your loan agreement.
  • Currency: choose US dollar, Pakistani rupee, pound or another currency from the list. The maths is the same in every currency.
  • Term and dates (optional): Extra months on the term covers odd terms such as 4 years 6 months, and First payment date (today by default) puts a real month on every row of the schedule.
  • Extra payments (optional): Extra every month with the month it starts, Extra every year with the month of the first one, a One-time extra payment with the month it is paid in, and More one-time payments as a list such as 5000 2028-06; 2500 2030-01.
  • Read the results as you type, open the Yearly schedule or the Monthly schedule, and use Download CSV to keep the table in a spreadsheet.

If an extra payment is dated before the first payment, the calculator counts it from the first payment instead and shows a warning saying so. A term of zero months is refused, because there is nothing to spread the loan over.

Every figure is worked out to the cent behind the scenes, and the final payment is trimmed so the balance ends at exactly 0.00. Here is what each part of the result means.

Why the first payments are mostly interest

Interest each month is the balance times the monthly rate. At the start the balance is at its highest, so the interest part is at its highest too, and only what is left of the payment reduces the loan. As the balance falls, the interest part shrinks and the principal part grows, even though the payment never changes.

The yearly schedule for the 30,000 car loan in the example above (5 years at 7.5%, 601.14 a month) shows the shift clearly. In year 1 more than a quarter of what you pay is interest; in year 5 it is under 4%.

Worked examples: extra mortgage payments and a car loan in rupees

Extra payments on a mortgage. Take the same 350,000 mortgage at 6.5% for 30 years and add 200 under Extra every month, starting with the first payment. The payment stays 2,212.24, but the loan is cleared after 286 payments instead of 360, which is 6 years and 2 months sooner. Total interest falls from 446,405.71 to 338,308.88, so the extras save 108,096.83.

A car loan in Pakistan. Choose Pakistani rupee and enter Rs 3,000,000 borrowed over 3 years at an assumed 16% a year (use the rate on your own offer letter). The monthly installment is Rs 105,471.10. In the first month Rs 40,000 of it is interest (3,000,000 × 16% ÷ 12) and Rs 65,471.10 reduces the loan. Over 36 installments you pay Rs 796,959.57 of interest.

Compare that with a flat-rate quote of the same 16%. A flat rate charges interest on the original amount for the whole term, even though you repay part of it every month: 3,000,000 × 16% × 3 = Rs 1,440,000 of interest and an installment of Rs 123,333.33. On a reducing balance, the rate that gives the same installment is about 27.5% a year. Whenever a lender or dealer quotes a flat rate, ask for the reducing-balance rate or the full repayment schedule before you compare offers.

Extra payments, prepayment penalties and when paying early makes sense

An extra payment skips the interest that would have built up on that money for every remaining month, so the same amount saves more the earlier it is paid. A yearly bonus paid in year 2 does far more than the same bonus paid in year 20. The calculator lets you test monthly, yearly and one-time extras together and see the payoff month move.

Before you pay ahead, read your agreement for a prepayment penalty. The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains that, where a mortgage has one, it usually applies when you pay off the whole balance (for example by selling or refinancing) within a set period, often three or five years, and sometimes when you pay off a large part at once. Small extra principal payments normally do not trigger it, but the CFPB advises confirming this with your lender. In Pakistan, ask your bank for its early settlement charges before you pay ahead.

  • Ask the lender to apply extras to principal, not to future payments; otherwise the money may just sit as a prepaid installment.
  • Keep an emergency fund first. Money paid into a loan is hard to get back if you lose income.
  • Clear higher-rate debt, such as a credit card, before paying ahead on a low-rate mortgage.
  • If you want a lower monthly payment rather than an earlier payoff, ask about recasting or refinancing; extra payments alone keep the payment the same.

Loans that do not follow a standard amortization schedule

This calculator assumes a fixed rate, equal monthly payments and interest charged on the falling balance. Several common kinds of borrowing work differently, so check yours before relying on the schedule.

  • Credit cards are revolving debt: the balance can go up and down, and the minimum payment changes each month. Use the credit card calculators instead.
  • Interest-only loans charge only interest for a period, so the balance does not fall until the interest-only period ends.
  • Balloon loans have smaller regular payments and one large final payment that clears what is left. The regular payments follow a longer schedule than the real term.
  • Home equity lines of credit (HELOCs) usually have a variable rate and a draw period, during which some plans allow interest-only payments, followed by a repayment period with higher payments, according to the CFPB.
  • Variable-rate loans change their rate over time. In Pakistan, for example, Allied Bank's car finance page (2026) says its rate is 1-year KIBOR plus a fixed spread and is reset once a year. Such a schedule is exact only until the first reset; after that, rerun the calculator with the new rate, the remaining balance and the remaining months.
  • Canadian mortgages must state their rate calculated yearly or half-yearly, not in advance (Interest Act, section 6), so a schedule built from the rate divided by 12 is slightly off. Our Canadian mortgage calculator handles that rule.

Amortization in accounting: intangible assets and startup costs

In accounting, amortization spreads the cost of an intangible asset over the period it is expected to earn income, much as depreciation does for machines and buildings. The usual method is straight line: cost divided by the number of months or years. A business that buys a customer list for 90,000 and writes it off over 15 years records 500 a month, or 6,000 a year, as amortization expense.

US tax rules set the period for many such assets. The IRS instructions for Form 4562 (2025) say that section 197 intangibles, which include goodwill, going concern value, patents, licenses and trademarks acquired with a business, must be amortized over 15 years (180 months). For startup costs, section 195 of the Internal Revenue Code allows a deduction of up to 5,000 dollars in the year the business starts, reduced by any amount by which those costs exceed 50,000 dollars, with the rest deducted evenly over 180 months.

Under the international accounting standard IAS 38, an intangible asset with a finite useful life is amortized and tested for impairment, while one with an indefinite useful life is not amortized but is tested for impairment every year. Amortization is a cost that does not use cash in the year it is recorded, which is why analysts add it back, together with depreciation, in EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization).

Building the schedule yourself in Excel or Google Sheets, and common mistakes

Spreadsheets have the payment formula built in. In Excel or Google Sheets, =PMT(rate/12, months, -amount) returns the monthly payment, for example =PMT(7.5%/12, 60, -30000) gives 601.14. For each row, interest is the previous balance times rate/12, principal is the payment minus that interest, and the new balance is the previous balance minus the principal. IPMT and PPMT return the interest and principal parts of any single payment. If you only need the table, Download CSV here gives you a ready-made schedule.

  • Entering the price instead of the amount borrowed. Take off the down payment first.
  • Using an APR that includes fees as the interest rate. The schedule needs the note rate; use the APR only to compare offers.
  • Forgetting costs outside the loan. A home payment usually also carries property tax and insurance, which this payment leaves out.
  • Comparing a flat-rate quote with a reducing-balance rate as if they were the same thing.
  • Assuming the schedule holds for a variable-rate loan after the rate resets.
  • Typing the term in months into the years box. A 60-month loan is 5 years.

How it works

Type the loan amount, the loan term in years and the yearly interest rate, and pick your currency. The calculator finds the one fixed monthly payment that clears the loan by the end of the term, then walks through every month: interest is charged on the balance still owed, and the rest of the payment lowers that balance. Under Term and dates you can add extra months to the term (for a term like 20 years 6 months) and set the first payment date. Under Extra payments you can add an amount every month, once a year or once, or a list of one-time amounts with their months. Extras go straight to the balance, so the payment stays the same, the loan ends sooner and less interest is charged.

The formula

  • Monthly rate r = yearly rate ÷ 100 ÷ 12; number of payments n = years × 12 + extra months
  • Monthly payment M = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), where L is the loan amount; at 0% interest M = L ÷ n
  • Each month: interest = balance × r; principal = M − interest + any extra; new balance = balance − principal
  • The last payment only clears what is left; interest saved = interest without extras − interest with extras
  • Paid off in = first payment month + number of payments

A worked example

A 30,000 car loan over 5 years at 7.5%: r = 0.075 ÷ 12 = 0.00625 and n = 60, so M = 30,000 × 0.00625 ÷ (1 − 1.00625^−60) = 601.14. In month 1 the interest is 30,000 × 0.00625 = 187.50, so 413.64 repays the loan and the balance becomes 29,586.36. The 60 payments add up to 36,068.31, of which 6,068.31 is interest. Paying 100 extra every month from the start ends the loan after 50 payments and saves 1,049.73 of interest.

Questions

What is an amortization schedule?

It is a table with one row for every loan payment, showing how much of that payment is interest, how much reduces the loan and what balance is left. This calculator builds it by year and by month, with dates from your first payment, and you can download it as a CSV file.

How do I compute the monthly amortization of a loan?

Divide the yearly rate by 12 to get the monthly rate r, count the payments n, and use M = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n). For 30,000 over 60 months at 7.5%, that is 601.14 a month. The calculator does this for you and splits every payment into interest and principal.

What is the amortization formula?

The payment formula is M = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), where L is the loan, r the monthly rate and n the number of payments. Each month, interest is the balance times r, and the payment minus that interest lowers the balance. At 0% interest the payment is simply the loan divided by the number of months.

Can I use this as an amortization calculator for a car loan or a personal loan?

Yes. Any fixed-rate loan with equal monthly payments works: enter the amount you borrow, the term and the rate. For car-specific items such as trade-in and sales tax, the auto loan calculator adds those to the price first.

Do extra payments lower my monthly payment?

No. The scheduled payment stays the same and the extras go straight to the balance, so the loan ends sooner. The results show the new payoff month, the total of your extras and the interest you save.

Why is so much of each early payment interest?

Interest is worked out on the balance you still owe. At the start the balance is largest, so the interest part is too. Each payment lowers the balance a little, the interest part shrinks, and more of the same payment goes to principal.

How do I calculate an amortization schedule in Excel?

Use =PMT(rate/12, months, -amount) for the payment. In each row, interest is the previous balance times rate/12, principal is the payment minus interest, and the new balance is the old one minus principal. You can also download the schedule here as a CSV file and open it in Excel or Google Sheets.

What is the difference between amortization and depreciation?

Both spread a cost over time in the accounts. Amortization is used for intangible assets such as patents, licences and purchased goodwill, and depreciation for physical assets such as vehicles, machines and buildings. On a loan, amortization means paying the debt down in installments.

Does this work for a HELOC or a home equity loan?

A fixed-rate home equity loan amortizes like any installment loan, so yes. A HELOC usually has a variable rate and a draw period that may allow interest-only payments, so the schedule fits only the repayment period, and only while the rate stays the same.

Is this amortization calculator suitable for loans in Pakistan?

Yes. Pick Pakistani rupee and enter the reducing-balance rate from your offer letter. If the loan is priced on KIBOR plus a spread and resets every year, rerun the calculator at each reset with the new rate and the balance left, and compare any flat-rate quote by asking for its reducing-balance rate.

Can I download the schedule?

Yes. Open the yearly or monthly schedule and use Download CSV to keep it in a spreadsheet. The yearly schedule adds up each loan year from your first payment month.
