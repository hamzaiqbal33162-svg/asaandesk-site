How it works

This covers the paying-out years. Enter the balance you start with and the yearly return the money left in it earns. In the first tab, pick how many years the payments should last and how often you are paid: the calculator finds the equal payment that uses up the balance exactly at the end. In the second tab, enter the payment you want: it finds how long the money lasts, and the last payment is whatever is left. If each payment is no more than the return the balance earns, the money never runs out. Payments are taken at the end of each period, and the yearly return is turned into the equal rate for one payment period.

The formula

  • Payments a year k (1, 2, 4, 12, 24 or 26); rate per payment i = (1 + rate ÷ 100)^(1/k) − 1
  • Fixed time: n = years × k, payment = balance × i ÷ (1 − (1 + i)^−n); at 0%, payment = balance ÷ n
  • Fixed payment: lasts forever when payment ≤ balance × i; otherwise n = −ln(1 − balance × i ÷ payment) ÷ ln(1 + i) payments, years = n ÷ k
  • Each period: return = balance × i; balance = balance + return − payment

A worked example

Take $500,000 earning 6% a year out monthly over 10 years. The monthly rate is 1.06^(1/12) − 1 ≈ 0.4868%, and with 120 payments the formula gives $5,511.20 a month. The 120 payments add up to $661,344.16, so $161,344.16 of it is return earned while the money was paid out.

Questions

Why is the monthly rate not just the yearly rate divided by 12?

The yearly return is treated as what the money earns over a whole year. The monthly rate is the one that, earned 12 times, gives exactly that yearly growth, which is a little less than one twelfth.

What does it mean when the money lasts forever?

Each payment is no more than the return the balance earns in that period, so the balance never goes down. The result shows the most you can take each period without ever touching the starting balance.

Why is the last payment smaller?

The money rarely runs out exactly on a payment date. The last payment is whatever is left in the balance, with its return, at that point.

Does this include tax, fees or inflation?

No. Use a return after fees, and keep in mind that a fixed payment buys less each year when prices rise. Taxes on the payments depend on where you live and the type of annuity.
