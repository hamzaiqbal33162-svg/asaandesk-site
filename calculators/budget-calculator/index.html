What is a budget, and what does this budget calculator show?

A budget is a plan for your money: what comes in each month, what goes out, and what is left for saving or paying off debt. This budget calculator builds a full household budget from about 40 lines in eight groups, works out what is left over or short each month, and checks your spending against three widely used yardsticks: the debt-to-income ratio, the share of income that goes on housing, and the 50/30/20 rule.

A good budget is not about cutting everything. It shows where the money really goes, so you can decide on purpose what matters. Most people find a few surprises the first time they add up yearly costs such as insurance, school fees, gifts and holidays, which are easy to forget when you think month by month.

How to use the budget calculator, step by step

  • Salary and other pay before tax: the pay of everyone in the household, monthly or yearly. If you only know what lands in your account, type that instead and set the tax rate to 0.
  • Overall income tax rate: all income taxes together as a share of your pay. Your payslip or last tax return gives it: tax paid ÷ pay before tax × 100.
  • More income: pension and social security, interest, dividends and rent received, and other income such as support or a tax refund.
  • Spending groups: Housing and utilities, Transport, Loans and cards, Everyday living, Health, Children and education, Saving and investing, and Everything else. Open each group and fill in the lines that apply; blank lines count as 0.
  • Switch each line to Monthly or Yearly. Bills that arrive once a year, such as car insurance, property tax or holidays, are easiest to type as yearly amounts.
  • Pick your currency at the top. The results, tables and chart update as you type.

To avoid counting the same money twice, put a loan payment in only one place. For example, enter a credit card payment under Loans and cards only for the part that pays off an old balance, and keep new spending on the card in the groups where it belongs, such as groceries or eating out.

Debt-to-income and housing ratios: what lenders and agencies use

The debt-to-income ratio (DTI) is all your monthly debt payments divided by your monthly pay before tax, as the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau defines it. Its debt-to-income worksheet suggests homeowners keep the total at 36% or less, with the mortgage alone at about 28% to 35%, and notes that some lenders allow 43% or more. The calculator counts rent as a housing cost too, so renters can use the same yardstick.

The 43% figure has a history: it was the debt-to-income cap in the original US General Qualified Mortgage rule, which the CFPB replaced with limits based on the loan's price in a final rule issued in December 2020, so it is a common lender guide now rather than a legal limit. For housing alone, the US Department of Housing and Urban Development calls a household cost burdened when housing costs, including utilities, take more than 30% of income, and severely cost burdened above 50%.

The 50/30/20 rule and other ways to budget

The 50/30/20 rule splits take-home pay into about 50% for needs, up to 30% for wants and at least 20% for saving and extra debt payments. In this calculator, needs are housing, transport, loans, groceries, clothes, household supplies, health and children; wants are eating out, other living costs and the Everything else group; saving is the Saving and investing group. Whatever is left over shows as Not yet planned, and you can move it into saving.

The rule is a starting point, not a law. In a high-rent city, or on a low income, needs often take more than half, and that is a signal to protect saving where you can rather than a failure. Other methods suit different people:

  • Zero-based budget: give every unit of income a job (spending, saving or debt) until Left over is exactly 0.
  • Pay yourself first: move a fixed amount to savings on payday, then live on the rest.
  • Envelope or cash method: set a cash limit for each group, such as groceries or fuel, and stop when the envelope is empty.
  • Percentage targets per group: set your own shares, then use the Spending by group table to check them each month.

Worked example with the starting figures

The calculator opens with a sample single-earner household: 60,000 a year before tax at a 20% overall tax rate, rent of 1,200 a month, utilities of 200 a month, a car with insurance, fuel and servicing, everyday living costs, 3,000 a year of pre-tax retirement saving and 200 a month into an emergency fund.

Take-home pay is 48,000 a year, or 4,000 a month. Spending adds up to 40,120 a year, so 656.67 a month is left over. Housing is the biggest group at 1,416.67 a month (35.42% of take-home pay), followed by everyday living at 790.00 (19.75%). The 3,000 of retirement saving counts as 2,400, because paying it before tax saves 600 of tax.

The debt-to-income ratio is 24.33%, which rates as Good. The 50/30/20 check shows needs at 57.8%, wants at 15.8% and saving at 10.0%, with 16.4% not yet planned. A note points out that saving is below 15% of take-home pay. Moving most of the 656.67 left over into the emergency fund or retirement saving would lift saving above 20% without cutting anything.

Making a household budget in Pakistan (in rupees)

Choose Pakistani rupee as the currency. Most salaried people in Pakistan have income tax deducted by the employer, so the simplest way is to type your take-home salary and set the tax rate to 0. In the federal budget for 2026-27, reported by Dawn in June 2026, the yearly salary exempt from income tax stayed at Rs 600,000 and several middle tax slabs were cut, so check your new payslip and the current FBR rates if your pay is above that.

Example: a family takes home Rs 150,000 a month. Rent is Rs 35,000, utilities Rs 12,000, fuel Rs 12,000, car servicing Rs 24,000 a year, groceries Rs 38,000, school fees Rs 12,000 and other living costs, health, gifts and small extras add up to Rs 26,000 a month, including Rs 36,000 a year for gifts and donations. Saving Rs 10,000 a month into an emergency fund, the family spends Rs 147,000 and has Rs 3,000 left. Everyday living takes 34.67% of income and housing 31.33%; saving is only 6.67%, so the notes suggest raising it.

Because the tax rate is 0 here, the debt-to-income ratio (23.33%) is worked out on take-home pay, which makes it look higher than a lender would calculate it from gross pay. For comparison, the Household Integrated Economic Survey 2024-25 of the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics puts the average household's monthly income at Rs 82,179 and its monthly consumption spending at Rs 79,150, which leaves very little to save.

What to do when the budget comes out short

  • Start with the groups that are easiest to change: eating out, subscriptions, entertainment, travel and the Everything else group.
  • Compare housing with the 30% guide. If it is far above, a cheaper home, a lodger or refinancing a mortgage may do more than many small cuts.
  • Look at transport as a whole: fuel, insurance, servicing, parking and the car loan together. Selling a second car or switching to public transport can free a large amount.
  • Clear high-interest debt first. Card and personal loan payments often take money that could be saving; the debt payoff calculator shows the fastest order.
  • Raise income where you can: overtime, a second job, freelancing, or renting out unused space.
  • Re-run the budget every month for the first three months, using real bank and card statements instead of guesses.

Budget surplus, deficit and variance, and budgeting in a spreadsheet

A surplus is money left over after all spending, and a deficit is a shortfall, which this calculator shows as Short each month. Budget variance compares the plan with what really happened: variance = actual − budgeted, and variance % = variance ÷ budgeted × 100. If you planned 40,000 for groceries and spent 46,000, the variance is 6,000, or 15% over budget.

In Excel or Google Sheets, list income lines and spending lines in two columns, then use =SUM(income range) − SUM(spending range) for the amount left over, and multiply yearly items by 1/12 to get a monthly figure. This calculator does the same work instantly, and you can come back each month to compare.

How it works

Type your household's pay before tax and one overall tax rate (or type take-home pay and set the tax rate to 0), then open each spending group and fill in what you spend. Every line has its own Monthly or Yearly switch, so you can enter bills the way they arrive. The calculator turns everything into yearly amounts, takes tax off your income, adds up the spending and shows what is left, or how much you are short, each month. Saving into a retirement or college plan before tax costs you less than its face value, so it is counted after tax. It also shows how much of your income before tax goes to housing and debt (the debt-to-income ratio lenders look at) and how your take-home pay splits into needs, wants and saving.

The formula

  • Yearly amount = monthly amount × 12 (yearly lines as typed); monthly = yearly ÷ 12
  • Income after tax = income before tax × (1 − tax rate)
  • Saving group = (retirement + college saving made before tax) × (1 − tax rate) + investing + other saving
  • Left over = income after tax − all spending groups
  • Debt-to-income = (mortgage + property tax + rent + home insurance + building fee + car loan + card, student and other loan payments) ÷ income before tax
  • Housing ratio = (mortgage + property tax + rent + home insurance + building fee) ÷ income before tax
  • Group share = group total ÷ income after tax

A worked example

With the starting figures, a salary of 60,000 a year before tax and a 20% tax rate give take-home pay of 48,000. Spending comes to 40,120 a year (3,343.33 a month), so 656.67 is left each month. Rent of 1,200 a month plus 200 a year of home insurance is 14,600 of housing, which is 24.33% of income before tax: a good debt-to-income ratio.

Questions

How do I make a monthly budget based on my income?

Start with your take-home pay, list fixed costs such as rent, loans and school fees, then variable costs such as groceries and fuel, and finally saving. The calculator adds them up and shows what is left each month. If the result is short, trim wants first, then look at the big fixed costs.

What is the 50/30/20 rule?

A simple guide for take-home pay: about half for needs such as housing, transport, groceries and loans, up to 30% for wants such as eating out and holidays, and at least 20% for saving and paying debt off early. The calculator shows your own split next to these targets.

What debt-to-income ratio is good?

Under 36% of income before tax is comfortable, and the CFPB suggests homeowners aim for that. 36% to 43% is workable but tight, and many mortgage lenders look closely above 43%. Above 50% means more than half your pay goes to housing and debt.

Why is retirement saving counted at less than I put in?

Money paid into a pension, 401(k) or IRA before tax lowers your tax bill, so it costs your take-home pay only about amount × (1 − tax rate). Counting it that way keeps the budget honest when your income is entered before tax.

Can I type some bills per year?

Yes. Every line has its own Monthly or Yearly switch inside the box. Insurance, car servicing, gifts and holidays are often easier to enter as a yearly amount.

Which tax rate should I use?

Use all income taxes together as a share of your pay. If you only know your take-home pay, type that as the salary and set the tax rate to 0; in that case the debt-to-income ratio is measured on take-home pay and looks a little higher than a lender's figure.

Is there a budget calculator for Pakistan in rupees?

Yes, this one works in Pakistani rupees: pick Rs Pakistani rupee as the currency. Type your take-home salary with a 0% tax rate, enter rent, utilities, school fees and yearly items such as Eid and wedding costs, and the calculator shows what is left each month.

How do I calculate budget variance?

Subtract the budgeted amount from the actual amount: a positive result means you spent more than planned. For a percentage, divide the variance by the budget and multiply by 100, so 46,000 spent against a 40,000 plan is 15% over.

What is a budget surplus or deficit for a household?

A surplus is income left after all spending and saving; a deficit is when spending is higher than income. The calculator shows a surplus as Left over each month and a deficit as Short each month, with the yearly total underneath.

How should a college student or couple use this budget calculator?

A student can enter part-time pay, family support under Other income, and fees under School fees and supplies. A couple should enter both incomes together under salary and every shared bill once, then agree how the amount left over is split between saving and personal spending.

How much of my budget should go on rent?

A common guide is no more than about 30% of income before tax; HUD calls households above that cost burdened, and above 50% severely cost burdened. The Housing costs to income line shows your share, and the rent calculator works out a rent that fits your income and debts.
