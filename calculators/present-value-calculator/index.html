What present value means

A present value calculator tells you what money you will get in the future is worth in today's money. A promise of 10,000 in ten years is not worth 10,000 today, because money you hold now could earn interest for those ten years. The present value is the smaller sum that, invested today at your chosen rate, would grow into the future amount.

This idea, the time value of money, sits behind loan payments, bond prices, pension choices, lease accounting and investment decisions. Turning a future amount into today's value is called discounting, and the rate you use is the discount rate. Future value works the other way, growing today's money forward.

How to use the present value calculator, step by step

  • Choose your currency and a tab: One future amount for a single sum, or Regular payments for equal payments each period.
  • One future amount: enter the Amount in the future.
  • Regular payments: enter the Payment each period and choose whether Payments are made at the End of each period or the Start of each period.
  • Enter the Number of periods and the Interest rate per period. Both must use the same period: years with a yearly rate, months with a monthly rate.
  • Read the present value at the top. One future amount also shows the interest it earns, the discount factor and the working step by step; Regular payments shows the future value, the total paid in, the interest and a period-by-period table.

Present value formula and present value factors

The present value formula for one amount is PV = FV ÷ (1 + i)^N, with i the rate per period as a decimal and N the number of periods. For the default, 10,000 ÷ 1.06^10 = 10,000 ÷ 1.790848 = 5,583.95. The fraction 1 ÷ (1 + i)^N is the present value factor, or discount factor; multiply any future amount by it to get today's value.

The table lists factors for common rates and years. Read across to the rate and down to the years, then multiply: 50,000 due in 10 years at 8% is worth 50,000 × 0.4632 = 23,160 today.

Present value of an annuity: monthly and yearly payments

An annuity is a series of equal payments at regular intervals. The present value of an ordinary annuity, with payments at the end of each period, is PV = PMT × (1 − (1 + i)^−N) ÷ i. For 10 payments of 500 at 6%, that is 500 × 7.3601 = 3,680.04. When payments come at the start of each period, an annuity due, each one arrives a period sooner, so multiply by (1 + i): 3,680.04 × 1.06 = 3,900.85.

For monthly payments, use the monthly rate and the number of months. 500 a month for 5 years at 6% a year is 60 periods at 0.5%, with a present value of 25,862.78, even though the payments add up to 30,000. Dividing the yearly rate by 12 is the usual convention; if you need the exact monthly rate equal to 6% compounded once a year, it is (1.06)^(1/12) − 1 = 0.4868%.

Pension example: is a lump sum of 150,000 or 1,200 a month for 15 years better? At 5% a year (0.416667% a month), the 180 payments are worth 151,746.25 today, slightly more than the lump sum. At a higher discount rate the lump sum would win, so the answer depends on the rate you could earn and on how long the payments really last.

Present value in Excel, Google Sheets and a financial calculator

Excel and Google Sheets use =PV(rate, nper, pmt, [fv], [type]). The rate and nper must use the same period, type is 0 for payments at the end and 1 for the start, and money you pay out is negative. So =PV(6%, 10, 0, -10000) returns 5,583.95, =PV(6%, 10, -500) returns 3,680.04 and =PV(0.5%, 60, -500) returns 25,862.78.

For uneven cash flows, use =NPV(rate, value1, value2, ...). NPV treats every value as arriving at the end of a period, starting one period from now, so a cash flow that happens today must be added outside: =NPV(8%, B2:B6) + B1. Net present value is that sum of discounted inflows minus what you invest today; a positive NPV means the investment beats your discount rate. The IRR Calculator finds the rate at which NPV is zero.

On a financial calculator such as the BA II Plus, enter the number of periods as N, the rate per period as I/Y, the payment as PMT and any future amount as FV, then compute PV. Set payments per year to 1 if you enter the rate per period, and switch to the begin mode for payments at the start of each period.

Choosing the discount rate, including inflation

The discount rate is the return you give up by waiting: what the same money could safely earn elsewhere over the same time, such as a savings, deposit or government bond rate, plus something extra if the future payment is uncertain. A higher rate always makes the present value smaller.

To see a future amount in today's purchasing power, discount it at the inflation rate. 100,000 in 20 years with 3% inflation a year buys what 55,367.58 buys today. To combine both, use the real rate, (1 + nominal rate) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1. With an 8% return and 3% inflation, the real rate is 1.08 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = 4.85%; discounting 100,000 in 20 years at 4.85% gives about 38,750, against 21,455 at the full 8%.

In Pakistan, a natural choice is the profit rate you could earn on a bank deposit or a savings certificate of the same length, which changes over time, so check the current rate before you rely on the result.

Everyday uses: leases, rent, settlements and bonds

  • Lease and rent payments: Rs 25,000 a month paid in advance for 3 years, at 1% a month, has a present value of Rs 760,214.50 (choose Start of each period), against Rs 900,000 paid in total. Accounting standards measure lease liabilities with this kind of discounting, using the rate the lease rules set.
  • Lottery prizes and legal settlements: compare a lump sum offered today with the present value of the yearly payments.
  • Pensions and annuities: compare a cash-out offer with the present value of the monthly income, as in the example above.
  • Bonds: a bond price is the present value of its coupons plus its face value; the Bond Calculator works that out with real coupon dates.
  • Saving goals: the present value of a future target is what you would need to put away today, in one go, to reach it.

Tips, common mistakes and related calculators

  • Match the rate to the period. A 12% yearly rate with monthly periods must be entered as 1% a period, not 12%.
  • Pick end or start correctly. Rent and leases are usually paid in advance (start); loan payments and most pensions are paid after each period (end).
  • Count whole periods for payments. The payments tab needs a whole number of periods; one future amount accepts decimals such as 2.5 years.
  • Remember that a small change in the discount rate moves long-dated values a lot; try a range.
  • Present value ignores tax and risk unless you build them into the rate.

How it works

Money you receive later is worth less than the same money today, because money you have now can earn interest in the meantime. The present value is the amount that, invested today at the rate you enter, grows into the future money. Use the first tab for one amount you will get later. Use the second tab for equal payments, one each period: the calculator adds up what each payment is worth today and also shows what all of them grow to by the end. A period can be a year, a month or anything else, as long as the rate is the rate for one period.

The formula

  • One amount: PV = FV ÷ (1 + i)^N, with i = the rate per period ÷ 100
  • Discount factor = 1 ÷ (1 + i)^N, so PV = FV × discount factor
  • Payments at the end of each period: PV = PMT × (1 − (1 + i)^−N) ÷ i and FV = PMT × ((1 + i)^N − 1) ÷ i
  • Payments at the start of each period: both values × (1 + i)
  • At 0%: PV = FV, and for payments PV = FV = PMT × N
  • Total interest = future value − the payments (or future amount − present value)

A worked example

Rs 1,000,000 you will receive in 5 years, if you could earn 12% a year in the meantime: PV = 1,000,000 ÷ 1.12^5 = 1,000,000 ÷ 1.762342 = Rs 567,426.86. Put Rs 567,426.86 away today at 12% and it grows to Rs 1,000,000 in 5 years, so Rs 432,573.14 of the future amount is interest.

Questions

How do I calculate present value?

Divide the future amount by (1 + rate)^periods, with the rate per period as a decimal. 10,000 in 10 years at 6% is 10,000 ÷ 1.06^10 = 5,583.95. For equal payments, use PMT × (1 − (1 + i)^−N) ÷ i instead.

What is the present value formula?

For one amount, PV = FV ÷ (1 + i)^N. For equal payments at the end of each period, PV = PMT × (1 − (1 + i)^−N) ÷ i, and for payments at the start, multiply that by (1 + i).

What is a present value factor?

The number 1 ÷ (1 + i)^N, which tells you what one unit of money due after N periods is worth today. At 8% for 10 years it is 0.4632, so 1,000 then is worth 463.20 now.

How do I calculate the present value of monthly payments?

Use the monthly rate (the yearly rate ÷ 12) and the number of months. 500 a month for 5 years at 6% a year is 60 periods at 0.5%, worth 25,862.78 today.

What rate should I use?

Use the return you could earn on the money elsewhere over the same time, for example a savings or bond rate. A higher rate makes future money worth less today. The rate must be for one period: for monthly periods, use the monthly rate.

Should I choose payments at the start or the end of each period?

End of each period is the usual case, such as a pension or loan payment received after each month. Start of each period fits rent or a lease paid in advance; each payment then arrives one period sooner, so the present value is higher.

How do I calculate present value in Excel?

Use =PV(rate, nper, pmt, fv, type), with payments you make entered as negative numbers. =PV(6%, 10, 0, -10000) gives 5,583.95 and =PV(6%, 10, -500) gives 3,680.04; add 1 as the last argument for payments at the start of each period.

What is the difference between present value and net present value?

Present value is what future money is worth today. Net present value subtracts what you pay today, so it tells you whether an investment earns more than your discount rate (NPV above zero) or less.

How do I include inflation in present value?

Discount at the inflation rate to see today's buying power, or at the real rate, (1 + return) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1, to combine both. 100,000 in 20 years at 3% inflation is worth 55,367.58 in today's money.

Why is the future value of the payments shown too?

It is what all the payments add up to, with interest, by the end of the last period. The present value and the future value are the same series of payments seen from today and from the end.

Can the number of periods have decimals?

Yes for one future amount, for example 2.5 years. For regular payments it must be a whole number, because each period has one payment.
