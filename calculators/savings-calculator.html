What a savings calculator shows

A savings calculator shows what your money grows to when you make a first deposit, keep adding to it every month, and earn compound interest on the balance. This one also lets you raise your monthly deposit each year, add a yearly deposit such as a bonus, and take tax off the interest, then shows the end balance, the interest earned, a year-by-year and month-by-month schedule, and charts of how the balance builds up.

Use it to set a savings goal and see whether your plan reaches it: an emergency fund, a house deposit, a car, a wedding, a holiday or school fees. Changing one number at a time, such as the monthly deposit, the rate or the number of years, quickly shows which one matters most for your goal.

How to use the savings calculator

  • First deposit: what you put in today (0 is fine if you are starting from nothing).
  • Deposit each month: what you add at the end of every month.
  • Interest rate (% a year) and Compounded: choose how often the bank adds interest, from yearly to daily or continuously. Use the rate your bank quotes; if it quotes an APY, choose Yearly and type the APY.
  • Years of saving: from 1 to 100.
  • More deposits (optional): Raise the monthly deposit each year by a percentage, and a Deposit each year with its own yearly rise.
  • Tax (optional): the Tax on interest as a percentage, taken from each month's interest.
  • Pick your currency at the top. The results update as you type.

Deposits go in at the end of each month, and the yearly deposit at the end of each year, so a new deposit starts earning interest the following month. In the first row of the yearly schedule, the deposits include the first deposit.

The savings formula with monthly deposits

With a fixed monthly deposit and no tax, the end balance has a closed formula: FV = P × (1 + i)^n + D × ((1 + i)^n − 1) ÷ i, where P is the first deposit, D the monthly deposit, i the monthly rate and n the number of months. For the starting figures, i = 0.04 ÷ 12 and n = 120, so (1 + i)^n is about 1.4908. The first deposit grows to about $7,454 and the deposits to about $29,450, which together give the $36,904.12 shown.

In Excel or Google Sheets, =FV(rate/12, years*12, −monthly, −first) gives the same answer for end-of-month deposits. Once you add yearly rises, tax or a yearly deposit, there is no simple formula, so the calculator works month by month instead.

Your savings rate is a different idea: what you save as a share of your income. Saving $500 a month from $4,000 of take-home pay is a 12.5% savings rate (500 ÷ 4,000 × 100). Economists measure the same thing for a whole country; the US Bureau of Economic Analysis defines the personal saving rate as personal saving as a percentage of disposable personal income.

What makes the biggest difference: rate, time, deposits or compounding?

Changing one input at a time on the starting figures ($5,000 first, $200 a month, 4%, 10 years) shows what matters most. The interest rate and the length of time have the largest effect, then the size and growth of your deposits. How often interest compounds matters least.

Worked example: an emergency fund

Saving $300 a month from zero at 4.5% compounded monthly builds $11,539.83 in 3 years: $10,800 of your own money and $739.83 of interest. A common way to set the target is to add up a few months of essential costs, such as rent, food, transport and loan payments, using the budget calculator, then work backwards: try different monthly deposits here until the balance reaches the target in the time you want.

Keep an emergency fund where you can reach it quickly and safely. In the US, the FDIC insures deposits up to $250,000 per depositor, per FDIC-insured bank, for each account ownership category, and that covers savings, checking, money market deposit accounts and CDs, but not stocks, bonds, mutual funds or crypto assets. The old Regulation D limit of six convenient withdrawals a month from a savings account was deleted by the Federal Reserve in April 2020, although a bank may still set its own limits.

Savings in Pakistan: National Savings certificates and bank accounts

In Pakistan, many savers use National Savings schemes run by the Central Directorate of National Savings (CDNS), as well as bank savings accounts. Rates change often, sometimes several times a year, so always check the latest table on savings.gov.pk before you invest.

Two common schemes, from the CDNS product pages and rate history: Regular Income Certificates (RIC) run for 5 years, start at Rs 50,000 and pay profit every month; from 21 October 2026 the rate was 12.12% a year, or Rs 1,010 a month on each Rs 100,000. Behbood Savings Certificates (BSC), for senior citizens aged 60 or over, widows and persons with disabilities, run for 10 years, pay monthly and paid 12.96% a year (Rs 1,080 a month on each Rs 100,000) from the same date; CDNS updated its summary again on 18 November 2026. For RICs, CDNS deducts withholding tax of 15% of the profit for people on the FBR Active Taxpayers List and 30% for others.

Worked example in rupees: choose Pakistani rupee, a first deposit of Rs 1,000,000, no monthly deposit, 12.12% compounded monthly, 5 years and 15% tax on interest. If every month's profit is reinvested at that rate, the balance reaches Rs 1,670,130.07, with Rs 670,130.07 of profit after Rs 118,258.25 of tax. A RIC itself pays the profit out instead: Rs 10,100 a month before tax, or Rs 8,585 after 15%, which adds up to Rs 515,100 over 5 years if you spend it. Saving Rs 10,000 a month for 10 years at an assumed 12% with 15% tax grows to Rs 2,072,078.10 from Rs 1,200,000 of deposits.

Tips for saving more, and related calculators

  • Set up an automatic transfer on payday, so saving happens before spending.
  • Raise the monthly deposit whenever your pay rises; the 3% yearly rise in the table adds almost $4,000 over 10 years.
  • Compare the APY, not just the headline rate, when choosing between accounts with different compounding.
  • Count tax: interest is usually taxable, and the Tax on interest field shows what is left.
  • Watch inflation: a balance that grows more slowly than prices loses buying power, even if the number rises.

How it works

Enter your first deposit, what you will add each month, the yearly interest rate with how often it compounds, and how many years you will save. Under More deposits you can raise the monthly deposit by a percentage once a year and add a yearly deposit that can rise too; under Tax you can enter a tax rate on interest. The calculator follows the account month by month: interest is added on the balance, any tax on it is taken out, then that month's deposit goes in at the end of the month.

The formula

  • Monthly rate j = (1 + r ÷ m)^(m ÷ 12) − 1, where r is the yearly rate and m the times it compounds a year (daily = 365.25); continuous: j = e^(r ÷ 12) − 1
  • Each month: interest = balance × j; tax = interest × tax rate; balance = balance + interest − tax + deposit
  • In year y (counting from 0), the monthly deposit is D × (1 + rise)^y; the yearly deposit Y × (1 + rise)^y is paid at the end of the year
  • End balance = first deposit + all later deposits + interest after tax
  • APY = (1 + r ÷ m)^m − 1 (continuous: e^r − 1)

A worked example

With the starting figures, $5,000 first and $200 at the end of every month at 4% compounded monthly for 10 years: the monthly rate is 0.04 ÷ 12 = 0.333%, so month 1 earns $16.67 of interest and ends at $5,216.67 after the first $200. After 10 years you have put in $29,000 and earned $7,904.12 of interest, for an end balance of $36,904.12. The same plan at 0.39% would end at only $29,668.91.

Questions

How do I calculate savings with monthly deposits?

Use FV = P × (1 + i)^n + D × ((1 + i)^n − 1) ÷ i, with P the first deposit, D the monthly deposit, i the monthly rate and n the months. $5,000 plus $200 a month at 4% for 10 years gives $36,904.12. The calculator does the same month by month and also handles rising deposits and tax.

How do I calculate savings account interest?

Each month, interest is the balance times the yearly rate divided by 12 for monthly compounding. On $10,000 at 4%, that is about $33.33 in the first month. Over a year, monthly compounding turns 4% into an APY of 4.074%.

When do the deposits go in?

At the end of each month, and the yearly deposit at the end of each year, like a salary or a yearly bonus. A deposit made at the end of a month starts earning interest the next month. If you want deposits at the start of the month, use the Interest Calculator, which has that switch.

Why raise the deposit every year?

Pay usually rises over time, and so do prices. Raising your monthly deposit by a few percent each year keeps your savings in step and makes a large difference over 10 or 20 years, because the bigger deposits also earn interest.

How much does compounding frequency change the result?

Not much. A 4% rate compounded yearly earns 4% a year; compounded monthly it earns 4.074%, and daily 4.081%. The rate and how much you save each month matter far more.

How is tax on interest counted?

The share you enter is taken from each month's interest straight away, so only the interest after tax keeps earning. If your tax is paid once a year from other money, your balance will come out a little higher than shown.

How do I calculate my savings percentage?

Divide what you save in a month by your take-home pay and multiply by 100. Saving $600 from $5,000 is a 12% savings rate. To see where that money could go, enter the monthly amount here.

Can I use this as a savings calculator for Pakistan?

Yes. Choose Pakistani rupee, type the profit rate of your bank account or National Savings scheme, and set the tax on interest to the withholding rate that applies to you, such as 15% for filers on Regular Income Certificates. Remember that many National Savings certificates pay profit out monthly instead of compounding it.

Can I make withdrawals in this savings calculator?

Not directly; it models deposits only. To see the effect of taking money out regularly, lower the monthly deposit, or use the annuity payout calculator for a balance you draw down over time.

What is a savings account?

It is a bank deposit account that pays interest on money you do not need right away, usually at a higher rate than a checking or current account. In the US, deposits at FDIC-insured banks are protected up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, for each ownership category.
