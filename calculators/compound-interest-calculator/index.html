What compound interest is

Compound interest means earning interest on your interest. Each time interest is added, the next period's interest is worked out on the bigger balance, so money grows faster and faster over time. A compound interest calculator shows what a starting amount, and any regular deposits, will grow to at a given rate and compounding.

In simple words: with simple interest, 10,000 at 6% earns 600 every year. With compound interest it earns 600 in the first year, 636 in the second and 674.16 in the third, because each year's interest is 6% of a larger balance. After 30 years the compounded balance is 57,434.91, more than twice the 28,000 that simple interest gives.

The same force works against you on a loan or a credit card: unpaid interest is added to what you owe and is then charged interest itself. At a card rate of 24% a year charged monthly, a balance of 1,000 grows to 1,268.24 in a year if nothing is paid.

How to use the compound interest calculator

The Grow money tab works out the future balance. Fill in these fields:

  • Starting amount: the money you begin with (10,000 by default).
  • Interest rate and Compounded: the yearly rate and how often interest is added: yearly, twice a year, quarterly, monthly, twice a month, every two weeks, weekly, daily or continuously.
  • Years and months: how long the money grows.
  • Regular deposits: Add each month puts in a fixed amount every month, and Deposits go in at the chooses the start or the end of each month (the end by default).

The results show the final balance, the total interest, the total you put in, how many times your money grew, the APY for your rate, the formula worked out with your own numbers, year-by-year and month-by-month tables and two charts.

The Convert a rate tab takes a rate, how it is compounded and the compounding you want it shown in. It gives the rate that grows money by exactly the same amount in a year, to five decimals, plus the rate for a single period. Use it to turn a monthly APR into an APY, or a quoted APY into a monthly rate.

The compound interest formula, with a worked solution

The formula is A = P × (1 + r ÷ m)^(m × t). A is the final amount, P the starting amount (the principal), r the yearly rate as a decimal, m the number of times interest is added each year and t the number of years. The compound interest earned is A − P. For continuous compounding the formula becomes A = P × e^(r × t).

A typical school maths question, with its solution: find the amount on Rs 50,000 at 10% a year compounded half-yearly for 3 years. Here P = 50,000, r = 0.10, m = 2 and t = 3, so each half year earns 10% ÷ 2 = 5% and there are 2 × 3 = 6 periods. A = 50,000 × 1.05^6 = 50,000 × 1.3400956 = Rs 67,004.78, so the compound interest is Rs 17,004.78.

Compounded yearly instead, the same money would reach 50,000 × 1.10^3 = Rs 66,550.00, so the half-yearly version earns Rs 454.78 more. The calculator shows these steps under the result for whatever numbers you enter.

In Excel or Google Sheets, =FV(10%/2, 6, 0, -50000) gives the same 67,004.78. With a monthly deposit as well, use =FV(rate/12, months, -deposit, -start), and add 1 as a last argument if the deposits go in at the start of each month; money you pay in is entered as a negative number.

Monthly contributions and the value of starting early

Regular deposits often matter more than the starting amount. 10,000 plus 500 a month, added at the end of each month, at 7% compounded monthly for 20 years grows to 300,850.72. You put in 130,000.00, so 170,850.72 is interest; the starting 10,000 on its own would have grown to 40,387.39.

APR vs APY, continuous compounding and a little history

A rate quoted with one compounding can be written with another that grows money by exactly the same amount. The APY (annual percentage yield) is the real growth in one year after compounding; the rate before compounding, as loans and cards usually quote it, is the nominal rate or APR. In the US, Regulation DD sets how banks must work out the APY they advertise on deposits.

Continuous compounding is the limit of adding interest more and more often. While studying compound interest in 1683, Jacob Bernoulli looked at (1 + 1/n)^n as n grows and showed that the limit lies between 2 and 3. That limit is the number e, about 2.71828, a letter Euler first used for it in 1731. Money growing continuously at a yearly rate r multiplies by e^r each year.

Compound interest in Pakistan, tips and related tools

Choose PKR at the top and the calculator works the same way for rupees. If your account adds its profit back to the balance every month, choose monthly compounding; if the profit is paid out to you instead, the growth is simple interest. Profit on bank deposits is taxed at source, at 20% for active taxpayers on the FBR's tax year 2027 rate card, so the growth you keep is lower; the Interest Calculator has a tax field for that.

Example: Rs 1,000,000 at 12% a year for 5 years grows to Rs 1,762,341.68 compounded yearly and to Rs 1,816,696.70 compounded monthly, a difference of Rs 54,355.02 from the compounding alone.

  • Use the compounding your bank or fund uses, or convert its rate first on the Convert a rate tab.
  • Compare products by APY, which puts every compounding on the same footing.
  • On a loan, compounding works against you; paying interest as it falls due stops it from compounding.
  • Test lower rates too; a long-term average return is not a promise.

How it works

On the Grow money tab, enter a starting amount, the yearly rate, how often it compounds and the number of years; under Regular deposits you can also add a deposit every month. The result uses the compound interest formula, shows it worked out with your numbers and gives the balance year by year. On the Convert a rate tab, enter a rate and how it is compounded, then pick another compounding: the calculator finds the rate that grows money by exactly the same amount in a year, for example a monthly APR as a yearly APY, and the rate for a single period.

The formula

  • Growth: A = P × (1 + r ÷ m)^(m × t), with P the starting amount, r the yearly rate, m the times it compounds a year and t the years; continuous: A = P × e^(r × t)
  • One year's growth factor F = (1 + r ÷ m)^m (continuous: e^r); APY = F − 1
  • The same growth with m₂ compoundings a year: r₂ = m₂ × (F^(1 ÷ m₂) − 1); continuous: r₂ = ln F
  • Rate for one period = r₂ ÷ m₂; a day is 1/365.25 of a year

A worked example

6% compounded monthly grows money by (1 + 0.06 ÷ 12)^12 = 1.0616778 in a year, so it equals 6.16778% compounded yearly (the APY). Going the other way, an 8% APY is 12 × (1.08^(1/12) − 1) = 7.72084% compounded monthly, or 0.64340% a month. $10,000 at 6% compounded monthly for 10 years grows to $10,000 × 1.005^120 = $18,193.97.

Questions

How do I calculate compound interest?

Use A = P × (1 + r ÷ m)^(m × t): P is the starting amount, r the yearly rate as a decimal, m the compoundings per year and t the years. The interest is A − P. 10,000 at 6% compounded monthly for 10 years is 10,000 × 1.005^120 = 18,193.97, so 8,193.97 is interest.

What is compound interest in simple words?

It is interest on interest. Each period the interest is added to your balance, and the next period's interest is worked out on that larger balance, so savings grow faster every year. On a loan the same thing makes unpaid interest grow.

How do I calculate compound interest monthly?

Divide the yearly rate by 12 and multiply the years by 12: A = P × (1 + r ÷ 12)^(12 × t). For 10,000 at 6% over 10 years that is 10,000 × 1.005^120 = 18,193.97. Choose Monthly under Compounded to get this result.

What is the difference between APR and APY?

APR is the quoted yearly rate before compounding; for most loans and cards it is the monthly rate times 12. APY is what the money really grows by in one year once the interest earns interest. A 6% APR charged monthly is a 6.168% APY. Savings accounts usually quote APY, loans usually quote APR.

How do I convert APR to APY?

Open Convert a rate, enter the APR, choose Monthly (or however often it compounds) and show it compounded Yearly (APY). The formula is APY = (1 + APR ÷ 12)^12 − 1 for monthly compounding.

Is daily compounding much better than monthly?

Only slightly. 5% compounded monthly is a 5.116% APY; compounded daily it is 5.127%, and even continuous compounding only reaches 5.127%. The rate itself matters far more than how often it compounds.

What is continuous compounding?

It is the limit of compounding more and more often, every instant. Money then grows by e^r in a year (e ≈ 2.71828), so 5% continuous is e^0.05 − 1 = 5.127% a year. Some bonds and many textbook problems use it.

How do I calculate compound interest in Excel?

Use =FV(rate/m, m*years, -deposit, -start). For 10,000 at 6% compounded monthly for 10 years with no deposits, =FV(6%/12, 120, 0, -10000) returns 18,193.97. Add 1 as the last argument if deposits go in at the start of each period.

Can I add monthly contributions?

Yes. Open Regular deposits, enter the amount under Add each month and choose whether it goes in at the start or the end of each month. 10,000 plus 500 a month at 7% compounded monthly grows to 300,850.72 in 20 years.

Why does the converter use 365.25 days?

To average in the leap day every fourth year. It changes the result only in the far decimal places; 12% compounded daily is 12.02807% compounded twice a month either way to five decimals.
