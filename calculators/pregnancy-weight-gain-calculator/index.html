How much weight should you gain during pregnancy?

The healthy amount of pregnancy weight gain depends mainly on your weight before you became pregnant. This pregnancy weight gain calculator uses the US Institute of Medicine (IOM) guidelines from 2009, which set a total gain for each pre-pregnancy BMI group, and turns them into a healthy range for every week from 1 to 40, for one baby or for twins.

Gaining weight in pregnancy is normal and necessary. Part of it is the baby, and the rest supports the pregnancy and prepares your body for feeding: the placenta, amniotic fluid, a larger womb and breasts, extra blood and fluid, and fat that the NHS says your body stores ready to make breast milk.

The numbers are a guide, not a target you must hit each week, and they are not medical advice. Your midwife or doctor knows your health and your baby's growth and may suggest a different range.

How to use the pregnancy weight gain calculator

  • Choose Metric (cm and kg) or US units (feet and inches, lb).
  • Week of pregnancy now: from 1 to 40. Pregnancy weeks count from the first day of your last period, so use the week your scan or the Pregnancy Calculator gives you.
  • Height and Weight before pregnancy: your usual weight just before you became pregnant, which sets your BMI group.
  • Weight now: a recent weight on the same scale if possible, without shoes or heavy clothes.
  • Tick Expecting twins if you are carrying two babies, then read the results.

Understanding your results

  • Healthy weight at week N: your weight before pregnancy plus the healthy gain for that week, shown as a range.
  • Your weight now: In the healthy range, or how many kg or lb you are below or above it. A gauge shows where your weight sits between the two limits.
  • Healthy weight at 40 weeks: where the range ends if you carry to term.
  • BMI before pregnancy and its group (underweight, normal weight, overweight or obese), the healthy gain by this week, your gain so far and the healthy total gain by 40 weeks.
  • A line chart of the lower and upper limits for all 40 weeks, and a week-by-week table (tap to open) with the healthy weight and gain for each week.

Worked examples

  • Lahore, normal BMI: a woman of 160 cm who weighed 55 kg before pregnancy (BMI 21.5) should weigh 59.9 to 62.7 kg at week 24. At 61 kg she is in the range; at 58 kg she would be 1.9 kg below it. Her healthy weight at 40 weeks is 66.3 to 70.9 kg.
  • US units, overweight: at 5 ft 4 in and 165 lb before pregnancy (BMI 28.3), the healthy weight at week 22 is 170.7 to 176.3 lb. At 172 lb she is on track; at 180 lb she is 3.7 lb above the range, having gained 15 lb against a healthy 5.7 to 11.3 lb so far.
  • Obese BMI, early pregnancy: at 162 cm and 88 kg (BMI 33.5), the healthy gain by week 12 is only 0.5 to 1.8 kg, and 5 to 9.1 kg in total. A 1 kg gain at week 12 is in the range.
  • Underweight: at 165 cm and 48 kg (BMI 17.6), the healthy gain by week 16 is 1.9 to 3.8 kg and the total is 12.7 to 18.1 kg, the largest of the four groups.
  • Twins: at 168 cm and 62 kg (BMI 22.0), the healthy weight at week 26 is 70.3 to 74.8 kg and the total gain is 16.8 to 24.5 kg. In US units, a twin mother of 5 ft 6 in and 135 lb who weighs 155 lb at week 30 is 3.7 lb below her 158.7 to 170.6 lb range.

Gaining too much or too little

Many women end up outside the ranges. The CDC reports a study in which only about 1 in 3 women gained the recommended amount, about 1 in 5 gained less and about 1 in 2 gained more.

The NHS says that gaining too much can raise your blood pressure and is linked with gestational diabetes, which increases the chance of a large baby, and with pre-eclampsia. It also says pregnancy is not the time to diet, because that may harm the baby. If you are below the range, do not try to catch up quickly either; your midwife may check your diet, any sickness and the baby's growth.

Most women do not need to eat for two. The CDC says the first trimester usually needs no extra calories, and that about 340 extra calories a day in the second trimester and about 450 in the third are typical. A healthy snack or a small extra meal is much closer to that than doubling your portions.

UK, NHS and other guidance

The ranges here are the US IOM guidelines, which the CDC publishes. The NHS does not set BMI-based targets on its weight gain page; instead it says most pregnant women gain between 10 kg and 12.5 kg (22 lb to 28 lb), mostly after week 20. That sits inside the IOM range for a normal BMI.

Wherever you live, including Pakistan, your antenatal team may weigh you at visits and set a goal that fits your health. Use this calculator to understand where a number comes from and to bring better questions to your next check-up.

Tips for a healthy weight gain

  • Weigh yourself on the same scale, at the same time of day, every week or two, and compare with the week-by-week table rather than day to day.
  • Choose filling, nutritious foods: vegetables, fruit, whole grains, pulses, eggs, milk or yoghurt and lean meat or fish.
  • Stay active if your doctor agrees; walking and swimming are good everyday choices.
  • Contact your midwife or doctor straight away about sudden swelling of the face, hands or feet, a severe headache, blurred vision or flashing lights, or pain below the ribs, which the NHS lists as signs of pre-eclampsia, and about weight loss from severe vomiting.

How it works

Enter the week of pregnancy you are in, your height, your weight before pregnancy and your weight now, and say whether you are expecting twins. Your BMI before pregnancy puts you in one of four groups, and each group has a recommended total gain by 40 weeks. In the first 13 weeks only a small gain is expected; after that the gain builds steadily to the total. The calculator adds that gain to your weight before pregnancy and shows whether your weight now is inside the healthy range for this week.

The formula

  • BMI before pregnancy = weight in kg ÷ (height in m)²; under 18.5 underweight, 18.5-24.9 normal, 25-29.9 overweight, 30+ obese
  • Total gain by 40 weeks, one baby: underweight 28-40 lb (12.7-18.1 kg), normal 25-35 lb (11.3-15.9 kg), overweight 15-25 lb (6.8-11.3 kg), obese 11-20 lb (5.0-9.1 kg)
  • Twins: normal 37-54 lb (16.8-24.5 kg), overweight 31-50 lb (14.1-22.7 kg), obese 25-42 lb (11.3-19.1 kg)
  • Weeks 1-13: gain = (1.1 to 4.4 lb) × (week − 1) ÷ 12
  • Weeks 14-40: gain = week-13 gain + (total − week-13 gain) × (week − 13) ÷ 27
  • Healthy weight this week = weight before pregnancy + gain

A worked example

A woman 160 cm tall who weighed 55 kg before pregnancy has a BMI of 21.5, so the normal range of 11.3 to 15.9 kg applies. At week 24 the healthy gain is 4.9 to 7.7 kg, so a healthy weight is 59.9 to 62.7 kg. At 61 kg she is in the range, and by week 40 a healthy weight would be 66.3 to 70.9 kg.

Questions

How much weight should I gain in pregnancy?

It depends on your BMI before pregnancy. With a normal BMI the IOM advice is 11.3 to 15.9 kg (25 to 35 lb) for one baby; less if you started overweight or obese, and more if you started underweight or are carrying twins.

How much weight should I gain each week?

Very little in the first 13 weeks: 0.5 to 2 kg in total. After that, with a normal BMI, about 0.4 to 0.5 kg (0.9 to 1.1 lb) a week; less with a higher BMI and a little more if you started underweight.

How do I calculate my pregnancy weight gain?

Subtract your weight before pregnancy from your weight now, then compare it with the healthy gain for your week and BMI group. The calculator does both: for example, a 6 kg gain at week 24 with a normal BMI is within the 4.9 to 7.7 kg range.

What is a normal pregnancy weight gain in kg?

For one baby, 11.3 to 15.9 kg with a normal BMI, 12.7 to 18.1 kg if underweight, 6.8 to 11.3 kg if overweight and 5.0 to 9.1 kg if obese. The NHS says most women gain 10 to 12.5 kg.

How much weight should I gain with twins?

With a normal BMI the IOM range is 16.8 to 24.5 kg (37 to 54 lb); it is 14.1 to 22.7 kg if overweight and 11.3 to 19.1 kg if obese. Tick Expecting twins and the calculator uses these totals.

Why is the first-trimester gain so small?

In the first 13 weeks the baby is still very small, so a gain of about 0.5 to 2 kg is enough. Some women lose a little because of nausea. Most of the gain comes in the second and third trimesters.

I am outside the range. Should I worry?

Being a little outside the range now and then is common. Do not start a diet or try to gain fast on your own; talk to your doctor or midwife, who can look at your health and the baby's growth together.

Do I need to eat for two?

No. The CDC says you usually need no extra calories in the first trimester, about 340 extra a day in the second and about 450 in the third, which is a small extra meal rather than double portions.

Why is there no twin range for underweight women?

The 2009 guidelines did not have enough data to set one. This calculator uses the normal-weight twin range instead; the CDC lists 50 to 62 lb from another source, and your doctor may suggest a different target.

Does the NHS use the same weight gain ranges?

The NHS does not give ranges by BMI on its weight gain page. It says most women gain 10 to 12.5 kg (22 to 28 lb), mostly after week 20, which fits the IOM range for a normal BMI.
