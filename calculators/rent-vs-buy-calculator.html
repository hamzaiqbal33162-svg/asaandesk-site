What the rent vs. buy calculator answers

A rent vs. buy calculator answers the question behind every home search: is it cheaper to rent or to buy, and how long would you need to stay for buying to win? It adds up every cost of each choice for stays of 1 to 30 years, takes off what you get back when you sell, and shows the average cost a month and a year side by side, with the break-even stay where the two cross.

Comparing rent with a mortgage payment is not enough. A mortgage payment partly builds equity that you get back when you sell, while property tax, insurance, upkeep, interest, closing and selling costs are gone for good. Renting has its own hidden cost: nothing, except the return your savings could earn if you did not put them into a down payment. This calculator counts all of these so that the comparison is fair.

The answer depends heavily on a few guesses about the future, especially home prices, rents and investment returns. Treat the break-even as an estimate and try a cautious and a hopeful set of numbers before deciding.

How to use the rent vs. buy calculator

  • Home price, Down payment (as a % of the price), Mortgage rate and Loan term: the purchase you are considering. Defaults: $400,000, 20%, 6.5% and 30 years.
  • Rent a month: what a comparable home rents for, $2,200 by default.
  • Years you plan to stay: the stay the monthly figures are shown for, 7 years by default. The table and the chart still cover every stay from 1 to 30 years.
  • Buying costs (under More options): closing costs (3% of the price), property tax (1.2% of the price a year, rising 3% a year), home insurance ($1,500 a year), HOA fee, how fast insurance and HOA rise, upkeep and repairs (1% of the value a year), home value growth (3% a year) and selling costs (6% of the sale price).
  • Renting costs: rent rise (3% a year), renter's insurance ($15 a month), security deposit ($2,200) and one-time renting costs such as application or broker fees ($100).
  • Your money and taxes: the return you could earn on savings (5% a year), your federal tax bracket (22%) and state rate, your filing status and the tax year (2026 or 2027).

With less than 20% down the calculator warns you that a lender usually adds mortgage insurance (PMI), which is not included, so the real cost of buying would be a little higher.

How the comparison is worked out

Buying is counted as everything you put in minus everything you get out. You put in the down payment and closing costs, every mortgage payment, property tax, insurance, HOA and upkeep. You also give up the return the down payment and closing costs could have earned in savings, the opportunity cost. You get out the tax you save each year and, at the end, the sale price minus selling costs and the loan still owed.

Renting is simpler: rent rising each year, renter's insurance, one-time fees and the return the deposit could have earned. The deposit itself comes back, so only its lost return counts. The table shows the default 7-year stay item by item.

Why the length of your stay matters most

Buying carries large one-time costs: closing costs when you buy and selling costs when you leave, together about 9% of the price with the defaults. Over a short stay those costs swamp everything else. Over a long stay they are spread thin, while the home's value growth and the shrinking loan work in your favour and rent keeps rising.

Taxes: when owning saves you money

In the US you can deduct mortgage interest and property tax only if you itemize, and itemizing only helps when your itemized deductions beat the standard deduction. For 2026 the standard deduction is $32,200 for married couples filing jointly and $16,100 for single filers (IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32). Mortgage interest counts on up to $750,000 of home debt taken out after 15 December 2017, or $375,000 if married filing separately (IRS Publication 936), and property tax counts within the state and local tax (SALT) cap.

That is why the default result shows no tax saving: a married couple's first-year interest of about $20,700 plus $4,800 of property tax is below $32,200. Change the filing status to Single and the same home saves $13,929 of tax over 7 years, and the break-even falls from 7.1 to 5.6 years. The calculator uses only mortgage interest and property tax; other itemized deductions you have, and state income tax within the SALT cap, would make itemizing more likely.

Rules of thumb and their limits

A popular shortcut, the 5% rule, puts the yearly unrecoverable cost of owning at about 5% of the home's value: roughly 1% property tax, 1% upkeep and 3% for the cost of the money tied up in the home. For the default $400,000 home that is $20,000 a year, or about $1,667 a month, well below the $2,200 rent, so the rule would say buy.

This calculator gives a more careful answer, a 7.1-year break-even, because it also counts closing and selling costs, insurance, the 6.5% mortgage rate and the timing of every cost. Rules of thumb ignore how long you stay, which is often the deciding factor.

Another quick check is the price-to-rent ratio: the home price divided by a year's rent. The default home is $400,000 ÷ $26,400 = about 15. A higher ratio means homes are expensive compared with rents, which tends to favour renting; a lower one tends to favour buying. Use it to compare neighbourhoods, then run the full numbers here.

Using it outside the US: Canada, UK, India and Pakistan

The tax part of this calculator follows US federal rules, and the results show a dollar sign. Everything else, from rent rises and home growth to closing and selling costs and opportunity cost, works the same anywhere. For another country, set the federal and state tax rates to 0 to switch off the US deduction, then type local values in your own currency and read the dollar sign as your currency.

Adjust the buying costs to local practice. Instead of a US closing cost, use your country's purchase costs, such as stamp duty or transfer taxes, legal fees and registration charges, as a share of the price; set property tax to the local rate; and use a selling cost that matches agents' fees where you live. In places where home loans are less common or rates are high, try a larger down payment and the rate a lender actually quotes you.

What the numbers leave out, and tips

  • Mortgage insurance with less than 20% down, large one-off repairs such as a new roof, and the cost of furnishing a home are not included.
  • The renter is assumed to keep paying rent rather than investing the difference each month; a disciplined renter who invests the monthly savings can do better than shown.
  • Owning gives stability, freedom to renovate and protection from rent rises; renting gives flexibility, less risk and no repair bills. Weigh these alongside the money.
  • Use real numbers: the rent of a comparable home, a lender's actual rate quote and your local property tax rate.
  • Test a bad case as well as a good one: low home growth, higher rates and a shorter stay than you plan.

How it works

For each stay from 1 to 30 years the calculator adds up what each choice really costs you. Buying: the down payment and closing costs, every mortgage payment, property tax, insurance, HOA and upkeep, minus the income tax you save by deducting mortgage interest and property tax (only the part above the standard deduction counts), plus the return your down payment and closing costs could have earned in savings. When you sell, you get back the home's value minus selling costs and the loan still owed, and that is subtracted. Renting: the rent, renter's insurance, one-time fees, and the return the deposit could have earned. Dividing each total by the years gives the average cost a year. Buying usually loses on short stays, because closing and selling costs are spread over few years; the break-even stay is where the two lines cross.

The formula

  • Buying over T years = down + closing + Σ (payments + property tax + insurance + HOA + upkeep − tax saved) + (down + closing) × ((1 + i)^T − 1) − (value after T years × (1 − selling %) − loan balance)
  • Value after y years = price × (1 + growth)^y; upkeep in year y = upkeep % × value at the start of the year
  • Tax saved in a year = max(0, mortgage interest + property tax (up to the SALT cap) − standard deduction) × (federal + state rate)
  • Renting over T years = one-time costs + Σ (12 × rent × (1 + rent rise)^(y−1) + 12 × renter's insurance) + deposit × ((1 + i)^T − 1)
  • Average a year = total ÷ T; break-even = the first stay where buying ≤ renting, read in a straight line between whole years

A worked example

A $400,000 home with 20% down at 6.5% for 30 years, 3% closing and 6% selling costs, against $2,200 rent: for a one-year stay buying costs $60,315 net, renting $26,790. Over 7 years buying averages $2,443 a month and renting $2,435, almost a tie; buying is cheaper from about 7.1 years.

Questions

How do I calculate rent vs buy?

Add up every cost of owning over your stay (down payment, closing costs, mortgage payments, property tax, insurance, upkeep and the return your cash could have earned), subtract tax saved and what the sale gives back, and compare it with the rent you would pay. Divide both by the years to compare averages. The calculator does this for every stay from 1 to 30 years.

Is it better to rent or buy a house?

It depends mostly on how long you stay, local prices compared with rents, and the mortgage rate. With the defaults, renting is cheaper for stays under about 7 years and buying is cheaper after that. Put in your own numbers to see your break-even.

How do I compare rent with a mortgage payment?

Not directly. Part of a mortgage payment repays the loan and comes back to you when you sell, while property tax, insurance, upkeep and interest do not. Compare the full net cost of owning with rent, which is what this calculator shows.

Why is buying so expensive for a short stay?

Closing costs when you buy and selling costs when you leave (often 6% to 7% of the price) are paid once, however long you stay. Over one or two years they dominate; over ten years they are spread thin, while the home's growth and the paid-down loan add up.

Does the down payment count as a cost?

Not by itself: it comes back in the sale proceeds. What it does cost is the return it could have earned in savings or investments, which is counted as lost return. The same goes for the renter's deposit.

What is the 5% rule for rent vs buy?

It is a shortcut that puts the yearly unrecoverable cost of owning at about 5% of the home's value, made up of property tax, upkeep and the cost of the money tied up. If a year's rent is below that figure, renting is likely cheaper. It ignores closing and selling costs and how long you stay, so check the answer with the full calculation.

How is the tax saving worked out?

Mortgage interest and property tax only help if, together with your other itemized deductions, they beat the standard deduction ($32,200 for married couples filing jointly in 2026). Only the part above it saves tax, at your federal and state rates. Many buyers save little or nothing.

Does it include opportunity cost and investment returns?

Yes. The return you could earn on savings is applied to the down payment and closing costs (for buying) and to the security deposit (for renting). Raise it to see how a strong investment alternative makes renting look better.

What assumptions matter most?

Home value growth, rent rises, how long you stay and the return you could earn instead. Try a lower growth or higher return to see how quickly the answer flips.

Can I use it in Canada, the UK, India or Pakistan?

Yes, with care. Set the federal and state tax rates to 0 because the deduction rules are American, type your local prices, purchase costs and property tax in your own currency, and read the dollar sign as your currency.

Is this the whole story?

No. It leaves out mortgage insurance with under 20% down, the chance of large repairs, and how the money a renter saves each month could grow. It is a clear starting point, not a final answer.
