What an annuity calculator shows

An annuity calculator works out what a series of regular payments grows to. In finance, an annuity is any stream of equal payments made at fixed intervals, such as 500 every month. The word is also the name of a contract you buy from an insurance company: you pay money in, it grows, and later the insurer pays you an income.

Most annuity contracts have two phases. In the accumulation phase you pay in, either as one lump sum or as many smaller payments, and the money grows. In the payout phase, also called annuitization, the balance is turned into regular income. This page covers the first phase: it shows the future value of your starting amount plus yearly and monthly additions at a growth rate you choose.

For the second phase, take the end balance from here into the Annuity Payout Calculator, which shows how much a balance can pay each month or how long a set payment will last. Together the two pages answer the questions most people have about an annuity for retirement: how much will I have, and what income will it give me?

How to use the annuity calculator, step by step

  • Starting amount: the money already in the annuity or the lump sum you put in on day one. Leave it at 0 if you start from nothing.
  • Added every year: a payment made once a year, such as a yearly bonus you invest. Leave it at 0 if you only add monthly.
  • Added every month: a regular monthly payment. You can use both additions together, for example 300 a month plus 2,000 a year.
  • Growth rate (% a year): the yearly return you expect, from −99% to 100%. For a fixed annuity this is the credited rate in the contract; for a variable or indexed product it is only an assumption.
  • Years: how long you keep paying in and growing, from 1 to 100 years.
  • Currency: pick US dollar, Pakistani rupee, British pound, euro, Indian rupee, UAE dirham, Saudi riyal, Canadian or Australian dollar. The maths is the same in every currency.
  • Timing (under the advanced options): choose whether additions are made at the start of each period or at the end. The setting applies to both the yearly and the monthly addition.

The result updates as you type. At least one of the three money fields must be above 0, otherwise there is nothing to grow and the calculator asks you to enter a starting amount or an addition.

The annuity formula, and how to do it in Excel

The future value of an annuity is the sum of every payment plus the growth each payment earns until the end. For payments made at the end of each period, called an ordinary annuity, the formula is FV = PMT × ((1 + r)^n − 1) ÷ r, where PMT is the payment, r the rate per period and n the number of periods. For payments at the start of each period, called an annuity due, every payment earns one extra period of growth, so the result is multiplied by (1 + r).

A starting lump sum simply grows on its own as P × (1 + r)^n and is added on top. The calculator adds all three parts together: the lump sum, the yearly additions at the yearly rate and the monthly additions at the monthly rate.

The part in brackets, ((1 + r)^n − 1) ÷ r, is the future value annuity factor: what 1 paid every period grows to. Multiply it by your payment and you have the answer. Excel and Google Sheets use the FV function: FV(rate, nper, pmt, pv, type). Rate is the rate per period, nper the number of periods, pmt the payment each period, pv the starting lump sum, and type is 0 for payments at the end of each period or 1 for the start. Money you pay in is entered as a negative number, so the answer comes out positive.

To match this calculator, use the monthly rate it uses rather than the yearly rate ÷ 12. For Example 1 that is =FV((1+5.5%)^(1/12)-1, 240, -500, -15000, 1), which returns 259,158.00, the same end balance as above. Using 5.5%/12 instead treats the rate as compounded monthly and gives a slightly higher answer.

If your contract quotes a rate compounded monthly, convert it to the yearly rate this page expects: (1 + nominal rate ÷ 12)^12 − 1. A rate of 6% compounded monthly is about 6.168% a year; enter that and 100 grows to 106.17 in a year, where 6% gives 106.00.

Worked annuity examples

Example 1, in US dollars: you put $15,000 into a deferred annuity and add $500 at the start of every month for 20 years at 5.5% a year. The calculator shows an end balance of $259,158.00. You paid in $135,000 in total ($15,000 plus 240 payments of $500, which is $120,000 of additions), so $124,158.00 is growth.

The yearly table shows how the growth speeds up. In year 1 the account earns $1,002.29. In year 12 the growth ($6,618.77) is larger than the $6,000 you add that year for the first time, and in year 20 it reaches $13,365.86, more than twice the year's additions.

Example 2, start or end: with the same numbers but additions at the end of each month, the end balance is $258,199.12. Paying in at the start is worth $958.88 more, because each of the 240 payments grows for one extra month.

Example 3, in Pakistani rupees: you open with Rs 500,000 and add Rs 20,000 at the start of every month for 15 years at an assumed 12% a year. The end balance is Rs 12,255,410.86. Your own money is Rs 4,100,000 (the opening Rs 500,000 plus Rs 3,600,000 of additions) and Rs 8,155,410.86 is growth, because at a higher rate compounding does most of the work. Real returns on pension funds and savings products in Pakistan vary, so treat 12% as a scenario, not a promise.

The growth rate is the input you know least about, so it pays to try a range. The table keeps Example 1 the same ($15,000 to start, $500 a month at the start of each month, 20 years, $135,000 paid in) and changes only the rate.

A rate below 0 is allowed, because a variable annuity can lose value. At −2% a year the end balance is $108,648.12, a loss of $26,351.88 on the money paid in, and the chart switches to a single line of the balance because a loss cannot be shown as a slice.

Types of annuity, and their pros and cons

A fixed annuity guarantees the interest rate and the income. The rate is often fixed for a number of years and then reset, and a deferred fixed annuity promises not to credit less than a stated minimum. A fixed annuity is the closest match to this calculator, because you can enter its credited rate directly.

A variable annuity invests your money in funds of stocks, bonds or money market instruments that you choose, so the value goes up and down with the markets and you can lose money. Enter a cautious rate for a variable annuity and try a lower and a higher one to see the range.

An indexed annuity sits in between. Its return is linked to a market index, but the insurer guarantees a minimum, and some newer versions use a buffer or a floor that limits losses while also capping gains. Because the credited return depends on those caps and limits, a single growth rate is only an approximation.

Immediate annuities start paying soon after you buy them, usually with one lump sum. Deferred annuities wait until a future date, and the time between your first payment and the first income is the accumulation period this calculator models.

An annuity is an insurance product as much as an investment, so judge it on both counts. What you buy is mainly protection: a guaranteed rate or a guaranteed income that you cannot outlive, and in many contracts a death benefit for your heirs.

On a variable annuity, the SEC explains, the death benefit is usually the greater of the account value and a guaranteed minimum such as the payments made minus withdrawals. If you paid in 80,000, took out 10,000 and the account fell to 62,000, your beneficiary would receive 70,000 rather than 62,000.

  • For: tax-deferred growth in the United States, with no yearly contribution limit on non-qualified annuities.
  • For: a fixed annuity gives a known rate, which suits savers who cannot afford a fall in value close to retirement.
  • For: the payout phase can turn savings into income for life, which no ordinary savings account does.
  • Against: charges for insurance, administration, funds and riders often add up to well over 1% a year, and commissions can be high.
  • Against: surrender charges and early-withdrawal taxes tie your money up for years.
  • Against: contracts are complex, and a guaranteed income that does not rise loses buying power to inflation.

Annuity fees and surrender charges

Fees are the biggest reason an annuity grows more slowly than the markets it follows. The US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) lists the usual charges on a variable annuity: a mortality and expense risk charge, typically about 1.25% of the account value a year; an administrative fee of about $25 to $30 a year or around 0.15% of the value; fees of the underlying funds; and extra charges for optional riders such as guaranteed income.

A surrender charge applies if you take money out, or cancel, during the first years of the contract. In the SEC's example the charge starts at 7% in the first year after a payment and falls by 1 percentage point each year. Many contracts let you take about 10% of the value each year without the charge. Most contracts also have a free-look period, set by state law at generally 10 to 30 days, during which you can cancel without a surrender charge.

Taxes on annuities in the United States and Pakistan

In the United States, earnings inside an annuity grow tax-deferred: you pay no tax until you take money out. Payments into a non-qualified annuity are made with after-tax money and are not deductible, and unlike an IRA or a 401(k) there is no yearly contribution limit. When you withdraw, the gains are taxed as ordinary income rather than at capital gains rates, and money taken out before age 59½ may face an extra 10% federal tax.

Holding an annuity inside an IRA or 401(k) adds no further tax benefit, because those accounts are already tax-deferred. You can swap one annuity for another without tax under a Section 1035 exchange, but the SEC warns that a new surrender period usually starts with the new contract.

In Pakistan the closest equivalent is the Voluntary Pension System (VPS) regulated by the Securities and Exchange Commission of Pakistan (SECP). Under section 63 of the Income Tax Ordinance, 2001, a salaried or business person who pays into an approved pension fund gets a tax credit on contributions of up to 20% of taxable income. At retirement, up to 50% of the accumulated balance can be taken out tax-free; the rest can go into an approved income payment plan run by a pension fund manager or buy an approved annuity plan from a life insurance company registered with the SECP. Money taken out before retirement, or above 50% at retirement, is taxed. These rules are as amended up to 30 June 2026 and can change in any budget, so check the current Ordinance or your pension fund manager before you decide.

As an illustration, if the Rs 12,255,410.86 from Example 3 were a VPS balance at retirement, up to half of it, Rs 6,127,705.43, could be taken as a tax-free lump sum, and the other Rs 6,127,705.43 would go into an income payment plan or an annuity. Tips, common mistakes and related calculators

  • Use a net rate. Take fund fees, insurance charges and riders off the growth rate, or the end balance will look better than it will be.
  • Plan on the guaranteed rate as well. A fixed annuity rate is often guaranteed only for a few years and then reset, so run the numbers again at the contract minimum to see the cautious case.
  • Do not mix up the phases. This page is the saving phase; the income an annuity pays is a separate calculation in the payout calculator.
  • Check the timing. Most savings plans debit contributions at a set date each month; if your money goes in at the start, keep the default, otherwise choose the end.
  • Read the contract summary first. The rate, the surrender schedule, every yearly charge and the free-look period should all be written there; if one is missing, ask for it in writing before you sign.
  • Think about inflation. A balance 20 or 30 years away buys less than the same number today; the Inflation Calculator shows by how much.
  • Keep some money you can reach. Surrender charges and early-withdrawal taxes make an annuity a poor place for an emergency fund.
  • Compare with plain investing. The Future Value Calculator and the Compound Interest Calculator show the same growth without the insurance wrapper, and the Retirement Calculator puts the annuity in your wider plan.

How it works

This covers the saving years of an annuity, before any payout. Enter what you start with, what you add each year and each month, the yearly growth rate and the number of years. The rate is a yearly rate that is turned into the monthly rate that gives exactly the same growth over a year (its 12th root), and the balance grows month by month. Additions made at the start of a period earn that period's growth; additions at the end start earning in the next one. A yearly addition goes in in the first month of each year (start) or after the twelfth month (end).

The formula

  • Monthly rate j = (1 + rate ÷ 100)^(1/12) − 1
  • Each month: balance = (balance + additions at the start) × (1 + j) + additions at the end
  • Closed form: end balance = P(1 + R)^N + A((1 + R)^N − 1) ÷ R + M((1 + j)^12N − 1) ÷ j, each addition term × (1 + R) or (1 + j) when made at the start; R = rate ÷ 100
  • Total additions = N × (A + 12 × M); total return = end balance − starting amount − total additions

A worked example

Start with $15,000 and add $500 at the start of every month for 20 years at 5.5% a year. The monthly rate is 1.055^(1/12) − 1 ≈ 0.4472%, so the first month's $15,500 earns $69.31. After 20 years the end balance is $259,158.00: $15,000 you started with, $120,000 of additions and $124,158.00 of growth. Making the same additions at the end of each month gives $258,199.12, which is $958.88 less.

Questions

How do I calculate an annuity's future value?

Add the growth of three parts: your starting amount grows as P × (1 + r)^n, and each stream of regular payments grows as PMT × ((1 + r)^n − 1) ÷ r, multiplied by (1 + r) if the payments are made at the start of each period. This calculator does it month by month and shows every step in the tables.

What is the difference between an annuity due and an ordinary annuity?

In an annuity due each payment is made at the start of the period, so it earns growth in that period too. In an ordinary annuity payments come at the end and start earning one period later. With the same payments, an annuity due always ends with more; in our 20-year example the gap was $958.88.

Is the growth rate compounded monthly?

The rate is a yearly rate. It is turned into the monthly rate that gives the same yearly growth, so $100 with no additions grows to exactly $106 in a year at 6%. A rate quoted as 6% compounded monthly grows a little more, about 6.168% a year.

What is an annuity factor?

It is the number you multiply a regular payment by to get its value. The future value factor ((1 + r)^n − 1) ÷ r tells you what 1 a period grows to; at 6% for 20 years it is about 36.79, so 1,000 a year grows to 36,785.59. The present value factor, used for payouts and loans, tells you what a stream of payments is worth today.

How are annuity rates calculated?

For a fixed annuity, the insurer declares the credited rate in the contract; it is often fixed for a number of years and then adjusted. Variable annuities have no set rate: the return is whatever the chosen funds earn minus the charges. Income rates in the payout phase also depend on your age and how long the insurer expects to pay.

How do I calculate an annuity in Excel?

Use =FV(rate, nper, pmt, pv, type) with the payments and starting amount as negative numbers and type 1 for payments at the start. To match this page, use the monthly rate (1 + yearly rate)^(1/12) − 1 and the number of months as nper.

How do I calculate an annuity on a financial calculator?

Enter the number of periods (N), the rate per period (I/Y), the starting amount as a negative present value (PV) and the regular payment as a negative PMT, then compute FV. Switch the calculator to begin mode for payments at the start of each period, and use the monthly rate this page shows if you want the same answer.

Can I add both yearly and monthly amounts?

Yes. Both use the same timing, and both appear in the schedules. Leave either one at 0 if you do not use it.

Is there a limit on how much I can put into an annuity?

In the United States, non-qualified annuities have no yearly contribution limit, unlike IRAs and 401(k) plans. In Pakistan, the tax credit for Voluntary Pension System contributions only covers contributions up to 20% of your taxable income for the year.

Does the annuity calculator work in Pakistani rupees?

Yes. Choose Pakistani rupee in the currency list and every figure is shown in rupees. You can use it to project a Voluntary Pension System account or any regular savings plan, using a growth rate you consider realistic.

What happens if I take money out of an annuity early?

During the surrender period the insurer usually keeps a percentage of what you take out, often falling from about 7% in the first year to nothing after several years. In the United States, withdrawals of gains before 59½ may also face an extra 10% federal tax on top of income tax.

What happens after the saving years?

That is the payout phase. Use the Annuity Payout Calculator with the end balance from here to see how much you can take out each month, or how long a payment will last.
