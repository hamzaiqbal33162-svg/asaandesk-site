What are macros, and what does this macro calculator do?

Macros, short for macronutrients, are the three nutrients that supply almost all of your food energy: protein, carbohydrate and fat. This macro calculator works out how many calories you need for your goal and then splits them into grams of each macro, so you can plan meals for weight loss, maintenance or muscle gain.

The split matters because each macro does a different job. Protein builds and repairs muscle, skin and enzymes and keeps you full; carbohydrate is the main fuel for the brain and for hard exercise; fat carries vitamins A, D, E and K and is needed for hormones and cell walls. Per gram, protein and carbohydrate give about 4 kcal and fat about 9 kcal; alcohol gives about 7 kcal but is not a nutrient you need.

Counting macros is sometimes called flexible dieting or IIFYM (if it fits your macros): any food is allowed as long as the day's totals land near the targets. That works best when most of the grams come from whole foods, because the calculator cannot judge vitamins, fibre or salt. These are general guidelines for healthy adults, not medical advice.

How to use the macro calculator

  • Choose Metric or US units, pick Male or Female and enter your age (18 to 80), weight and height.
  • Select how active you are, from BMR only to extra active. Under Formula you can change the BMR formula; Katch-McArdle asks for your body fat percentage.
  • Pick your goal: Keep my weight, lose 0.5, 1 or 2 lb (0.25, 0.5 or 1 kg) a week, or gain the same amounts.
  • Pick a diet style: Balanced (25% protein, 50% carbs, 25% fat), Low fat (27.5 / 52.5 / 20), Low carb (30 / 40 / 30), High protein (35 / 42.5 / 22.5), or My own split.
  • With My own split, type your protein, carb and fat percentages. They should add up to 100%; the calculator warns you if they do not and if any macro falls outside its suggested range.
  • Read your daily energy in kcal and kJ, the grams of protein, carbs and fat with their suggested ranges, the upper limits for free sugar and saturated fat, a table of grams for every diet style, and a chart of where the calories come from.

How much protein, carbs and fat do you need?

Official ranges are wide on purpose, so that many healthy eating patterns fit. The calculator's suggested ranges sit inside them: protein from 1 g per kg of body weight up to 35% of calories, carbs from 40% of calories up to what remains after the protein and fat minimums, and fat from 20% to 35%.

Because the diet styles are percentages, very high calorie targets can give more protein than you need. At 3,144 kcal the balanced plan already gives 197 g of protein, about 2.9 g per kg for a 68 kg man. That is more than most people need, so you could move some of it to carbs to fuel training.

Worked examples: macros for weight loss and muscle gain

  • Weight loss, Islamabad: a 29-year-old woman, 160 cm and 70 kg, with light activity, aiming to lose 0.5 kg a week on the high protein style. Her target is 1,417 kcal a day (5,933 kJ): 124 g protein, 151 g carbs and 35 g fat. Her protein range is 70 to 124 g, so this plan sits at the top, about 1.8 g per kg, which helps keep muscle while she loses fat. Free sugar should stay under 35 g and saturated fat under 16 g.
  • Muscle gain: a 24-year-old man, 178 cm and 68 kg, training hard 6 to 7 times a week, choosing mild gain (0.25 kg a week) and the balanced style. His target is 3,144 kcal: 197 g protein, 393 g carbs and 87 g fat.
  • Own split, US units: a 35-year-old man, 5 ft 11 in and 190 lb, daily exercise, losing 1 lb a week with 30% protein, 35% carbs and 35% fat. His target is 2,319 kcal: 174 g protein, 203 g carbs and 90 g fat. The calculator warns that 203 g of carbs is under the suggested 232 to 378 g; fat is at the top of its range.

A quick way to reach a protein target is to put one protein serving or more in every meal: eggs at breakfast, dal or chicken at lunch, yogurt or milk with snacks, and fish, meat or chickpeas at dinner.

How to calculate macros yourself, including recipes

  • Find your calories for the goal (this calculator, or the Calorie Calculator).
  • Choose your shares, for example 30% protein, 40% carbs and 30% fat.
  • Multiply calories by each share, then divide by 4 for protein and carbs and by 9 for fat. On 1,800 kcal: protein 1,800 × 0.30 ÷ 4 = 135 g, carbs 1,800 × 0.40 ÷ 4 = 180 g, fat 1,800 × 0.30 ÷ 9 = 60 g.
  • For a homemade dish, add up the protein, carbs and fat of every raw ingredient from labels or a food database, including the oil, then divide by the number of servings you cut it into.
  • Weigh cooked food the same way each time; rice and pulses absorb water, so 100 g cooked holds far fewer grams of carbs than 100 g raw.
  • Check your progress after 2 to 3 weeks and adjust calories first, macros second.

Tips, common mistakes and related tools

  • Hit protein and calories first; carbs and fat can float a little from day to day.
  • Count the oil, ghee, sugar in tea and sauces: 30 g of oil alone is about 270 kcal of fat.
  • Spread protein over meals rather than eating it all at dinner; the ISSN suggests doses of about 0.25 g per kg, or 20 to 40 g, every 3 to 4 hours for people who train.
  • Do not drop carbs so low that training suffers or fibre disappears; whole wheat, pulses, vegetables and fruit cover both.
  • If you are pregnant, an athlete preparing for competition, or have diabetes or kidney disease, ask a doctor or dietitian for your own targets.

How it works

The calculator first estimates the calories you burn in a day (your BMR, by default from the Mifflin-St Jeor formula, times your activity factor) and adds or takes away 250, 500 or 1,000 kcal for your goal. It then splits those calories between protein, carbohydrate and fat by the diet style you choose, and turns each share into grams with the standard energy values: 4 kcal in a gram of protein or carbohydrate and 9 kcal in a gram of fat. The ranges come from widely used guidelines: at least 1 g of protein per kg of body weight and no more than 35% of calories, at least 40% of calories from carbs, and 20 to 35% from fat. If a weight-loss goal would take you under 1,200 kcal (women) or 1,500 kcal (men) a day, no plan is shown.

The formula

  • Daily calories C = BMR × activity factor (rounded) + goal change (−1,000 to +1,000 kcal)
  • Grams of protein = C × protein share ÷ 4; carbs = C × carb share ÷ 4; fat = C × fat share ÷ 9
  • Balanced 25 / 50 / 25%, low fat 27.5 / 52.5 / 20%, low carb 30 / 40 / 30%, high protein 35 / 42.5 / 22.5% (protein / carbs / fat)
  • Protein range: 1 g × body weight (kg) to 35% of C ÷ 4; fat range: 20% to 35% of C ÷ 9
  • Carb range: 40% of C ÷ 4 to (C − protein minimum × 4 − 20% of C) ÷ 4
  • Free sugar under 10% of C ÷ 4; saturated fat under 10% of C ÷ 9; 1 kcal = 4.1868 kJ

A worked example

A 25-year-old man, 5 ft 10 in and 165 lb, who exercises 4 to 5 times a week needs about 2,549 kcal a day to keep his weight. On the balanced plan: protein 2,549 × 25% ÷ 4 ≈ 159 g, carbs 2,549 × 50% ÷ 4 ≈ 319 g and fat 2,549 × 25% ÷ 9 ≈ 71 g a day. His protein range is 75 g (1 g per kg of his 74.8 kg) to 223 g.

Questions

How do I calculate macros for weight loss?

Find the calories that lose 0.25 to 0.5 kg a week (the calculator does this from your goal), then split them, for example 30 to 35% protein, 40 to 45% carbs and the rest fat. Divide the protein and carb calories by 4 and the fat calories by 9 to get grams. Keeping protein high helps you keep muscle while you lose fat.

What macros should I use for muscle gain?

Eat a small surplus, such as 250 kcal a day, and aim for about 1.4 to 2.0 g of protein per kg of body weight with enough carbs to fuel training. The balanced or high protein style usually covers this. Strength training is what turns the extra food into muscle.

Which diet style should I pick?

Balanced suits most people. High protein helps keep muscle while losing weight or when training hard. Low carb can make it easier to eat less for some people, and low fat suits people who prefer starchy foods. The best plan is the one you can keep up; total calories matter most for weight.

Are macros different for women and men?

The percentages work the same for everyone, but women usually need fewer calories, so the grams are lower. Protein targets are best set per kg of body weight, which already adjusts for size. Pregnant and breastfeeding women need more energy and protein and should follow their doctor's advice.

Why 4 and 9 calories per gram?

These are the standard average energy values for food: a gram of protein or carbohydrate gives about 4 kcal and a gram of fat about 9 kcal. Food labels use the same values. Some calculators use slightly different factors, so their grams can differ by a few percent.

How do I calculate macros in homemade food?

Add up the protein, carbs and fat of each raw ingredient, including oil and sugar, using labels or a food database. Divide by the number of portions, or weigh the whole pot and your plate to find your share. Recipes finished in a lot of oil, and fried snacks, gain most of their energy from fat.

What does My own split do?

Enter your own shares of protein, carbs and fat in percent. The calculator shows the grams, warns if the shares do not add up to 100%, and tells you when a nutrient falls outside its suggested range.

Why is no plan shown for my weight-loss goal?

If the goal would put you under 1,200 kcal a day for women or 1,500 kcal for men, it is hard to get enough nutrients, so the calculator suggests a slower rate instead. Very low calorie diets should only be done with a doctor.

Is free sugar the same as all sugar?

No. Free sugar means sugar added to food and drinks plus the sugar in honey, syrups and fruit juice. The sugar inside whole fruit, vegetables and milk does not count towards the 10% limit.

Do I have to hit my macros exactly every day?

No. Small misses from day to day matter little, and weekly averages matter more than single days. Get calories and protein right first, then fine-tune the rest.
