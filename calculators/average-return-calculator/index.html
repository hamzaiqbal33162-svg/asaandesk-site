What an average return calculator tells you

An average return calculator turns a messy investment history into one number: the steady yearly rate that would have produced the same result. That single annualized return lets you compare a fund you held for 7 months with a stock you held for 4 years, or check whether your savings beat inflation and a bank deposit.

This page does it two ways. Use the first tab when you know what an account was worth at the start and at the end and when you added or took out money. Use the second tab when you have a list of returns, such as a fund's yearly results from a factsheet, and want their true compound average and the cumulative return over the whole time.

How to use the average return calculator

  • Pick the currency at the top. It only changes the symbol, so the calculator works for dollars, rupees or any other money.
  • My account's yearly return: enter the Balance at the start and its date, then the Balance at the end and its date (today by default).
  • Under Deposits and withdrawals, type each one as a date and an amount, separated by semicolons, such as 2024-01-15 2000; 2025-06-01 -1000. A minus sign means money taken out. Leave it empty if nothing moved.
  • Average of several returns: type the returns in order, such as 10, -2, 15, 8 for +10%, −2%, +15% and +8%.
  • Choose what each return is for: One year, One quarter or One month (each its own return for that period), or A yearly rate held for some months. With the last option, list the months each return lasted, so 14 means 1 year 2 months.

Why a simple average of returns misleads

Adding up yearly returns and dividing by the number of years gives the arithmetic mean. It ignores compounding, so it overstates what you actually earned whenever returns go up and down. The geometric (compound) average fixes that: multiply the growth factors (1 + r) together, take the root for the number of years and subtract 1.

The classic case is a gain of 50% followed by a loss of 50%. The plain average is 0%, yet 100 becomes 150 and then 75, a 25% loss. The compound average is 0.75^(1 ÷ 2) − 1 = −13.40% a year, which is what the calculator shows. The bigger the swings, the bigger the gap between the two averages.

So when someone asks what the average return of the S&P 500 is, the honest answer is about 10% a year compounded over that history, not the 11.9% simple average, and before inflation. Treasury bills barely swing, so their two averages are almost the same. Past returns never promise future ones.

Worked examples

Four years of fund returns. With the default list of 10%, −2%, 15% and 8%, each for one year, $100 becomes $110.00, $107.80, $123.97 and finally $133.89. The cumulative return is 33.89%, and the compound average is 1.3389^(1 ÷ 4) − 1 = 7.57% a year. The plain average, 7.75%, is a little too high.

An account with a deposit and a withdrawal. Start with $10,000 on 1 January 2023, deposit $2,000 on 15 January 2024, withdraw $1,000 on 1 June 2025 and finish with $15,000 on 1 January 2026. The account made $4,000, and the money-weighted average return is 10.83% a year over the 3.00 years.

A rupee savings plan in Pakistan. You put PKR 100,000 into a mutual fund on 1 January 2025, add PKR 15,000 on 1 April, 1 July and 1 October, and the account is worth PKR 165,000 on 1 January 2026. The gain is PKR 20,000. Dividing that by the PKR 145,000 you put in gives 13.8%, but that understates your return because the later deposits were invested for only part of the year. The calculator gives 16.40% a year, which is the figure to compare with a bank deposit or a fund's published return.

Monthly returns. Enter 2, -1.5, 3, 0.5, -2, 1.8 and choose One month. The six months together give 3.76%, and the average is shown as a yearly rate of 7.65%, because the calculator always annualizes.

Average return formulas and how to do them in Excel

Annualized return formula for a list of returns: (1 + r1) × (1 + r2) × … × (1 + rn), raised to the power 1 ÷ years, minus 1. If each return covers a month, the years are months ÷ 12. In Excel, with returns as decimals in B2:B5, type =GEOMEAN(1+B2:B5)-1 for yearly returns, or =PRODUCT(1+B2:B5)^(1/COUNT(B2:B5))-1, which does the same. GEOMEAN gives an error if any value is zero or negative, which only happens with a loss of 100% or more.

Rate of return with contributions: when money goes in and out, use Excel's XIRR. Put every amount in one column, deposits and the start balance as negative numbers and withdrawals and the end balance as positive numbers, and the dates in the next column; then =XIRR(values, dates). XIRR needs at least one positive and one negative value and counts time on a 365-day year. This calculator counts whole years from the start date plus a fraction of the current year, so the two can differ by a few hundredths of a percent over long periods.

Money-weighted, time-weighted and accounting returns

The first tab gives a money-weighted return. It depends on how much money was in the account and when, so a large deposit just before a strong year raises it. That makes it the right figure for your own experience as an investor.

Fund companies usually publish time-weighted returns, which split the history at every deposit and withdrawal and compound the pieces, so the timing of investors' money does not affect the figure. The second tab gives you that kind of average when you enter the period returns.

The accounting rate of return (ARR), used for business projects, is a different measure again: average yearly accounting profit divided by the money invested. It ignores the timing of cash and compounding, so for investment decisions an internal rate of return or a payback period usually tells you more.

Tips and related tools

  • Compare like with like: an annualized return over 6 months can look huge or awful, because a short period is stretched to a full year.
  • Count dividends that were reinvested, and subtract fees, or the return will look better than it was.
  • Subtract inflation for a real return. A 12% return in a year with 10% inflation is a real gain of about 1.8%.
  • For a sum that grows at a fixed rate, the Compound Interest Calculator is simpler. For projects with uneven cash flows, use the IRR Calculator; for one purchase and sale, the ROI Calculator; and to strip out rising prices, the Inflation Calculator.

How it works

The first tab looks at a real account. The start balance and every deposit count as money in, every withdrawal and the end balance as money out, each on its own date. The calculator finds the one yearly rate that makes all of them balance (a money-weighted return, the same idea as a spreadsheet's XIRR), counting time in years from the start date. The second tab compounds a list of returns: each return grows the money for as long as it was held, and the average is the steady yearly rate that gives the same total growth.

The formula

  • Account: find r so that −start balance − Σ deposit ÷ (1 + r)^t + Σ withdrawal ÷ (1 + r)^t + end balance ÷ (1 + r)^T = 0
  • t = whole years from the start date + days since the last anniversary ÷ days in that year
  • Several returns: growth G = (1 + r1)^t1 × (1 + r2)^t2 × …, with each t in years
  • Cumulative return = G − 1; average return = G^(1 ÷ total years) − 1

A worked example

Returns of 10% for 1 year 2 months, −2% for 5 months and 15% for 2 years 3 months: $100 grows to $111.76, then $110.82, then $151.78. The cumulative return is 51.78% over 3 years 10 months, and 1.5178^(1 ÷ 3.833) − 1 = 11.50% a year.

Questions

How do I calculate average return per year?

Multiply the growth factors (1 + each return), raise the product to 1 ÷ the number of years and subtract 1. For returns of 10%, −2%, 15% and 8%, that is 1.3389^(1/4) − 1 = 7.57% a year. The second tab does this for any number of returns.

Why not just add up the returns and divide?

Because losses and gains compound. A 50% gain followed by a 50% loss leaves you with 75% of your money, a 13.40% loss a year, even though the plain average of +50 and −50 is 0%.

How do I calculate annualized return with contributions?

Use the first tab: enter the start and end balances with their dates and type every deposit and withdrawal with its date. The calculator finds the money-weighted yearly return, the same idea as Excel's XIRR.

How do I enter deposits and withdrawals?

Type a date and an amount for each one, separated by semicolons, for example 2024-01-15 2000; 2025-06-01 -1000. A minus sign means money taken out. Leave the box empty if you added or took out nothing.

How do I calculate average return in Excel?

For a list of yearly returns in B2:B5, use =GEOMEAN(1+B2:B5)-1. For an account with dated deposits and withdrawals, put the amounts and dates in two columns and use =XIRR(values, dates), with money you put in as negative numbers.

What is the average return of the S&P 500?

Using Damodaran's NYU Stern data for 1928 to 2025, the S&P 500 with dividends returned about 10.0% a year compounded, or 11.9% as a simple average, before inflation. Single years ranged from large losses to large gains, so a long average says little about any one year.

What does money-weighted mean?

The return counts how much money was in the account and for how long. A big deposit just before a good year lifts the result more than a small one, which makes it the return you personally earned.

What is the difference between cumulative and annualized return?

Cumulative return is the total growth over the whole time, such as 33.89% over 4 years. Annualized return spreads it into a steady yearly rate, 7.57% in that case, so periods of different lengths can be compared.

Can I use monthly or quarterly returns?

Yes. In the second tab choose that each return is for one month or one quarter. The average is still shown as a yearly rate, so 1% a month for a year shows as 12.68% a year.

How do I work out the return on a monthly SIP?

Enter your first investment as the start balance, each later monthly instalment as a dated deposit and today's value as the end balance. The money-weighted result is your yearly return, which accounts for each instalment being invested for a different length of time.
