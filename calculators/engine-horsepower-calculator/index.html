How it works

A quarter-mile run on a drag strip says a lot about an engine: the more power a car of a given weight has, the sooner it covers the 402 metres and the faster it is going at the end. Enter the car's weight with the driver and either the elapsed time (ET) or the trap speed from the time slip. Two well-known drag-racing rules of thumb turn those into engine horsepower; the Both tab shows the two estimates side by side with their average. The trap-speed figure is usually the steadier one, because a poor launch or wheelspin slows the elapsed time much more than the speed at the line.

The formula

  • From the elapsed time: hp = weight (lb) ÷ (ET in seconds ÷ 5.825)³
  • From the trap speed: hp = weight (lb) × (speed in mph ÷ 234)³
  • Watts = hp × 745.69987 (1 hp = 550 foot-pounds per second); metric hp (PS) = watts ÷ 735.49875
  • 1 kg = 2.20462 lb; 1 US ton = 2,000 lb; 1 mile per hour = 1.609344 km/h

A worked example

A 3,500 lb car with its driver runs the quarter mile in 14 seconds: 14 ÷ 5.825 = 2.403, cubed is 13.88, and 3,500 ÷ 13.88 ≈ 252 hp. If it crosses the line at 100 mph, 100 ÷ 234 = 0.4274, cubed is 0.0781, and 3,500 × 0.0781 ≈ 273 hp. The average of the two is about 263 hp, or 196 kW.

Questions

How accurate are these estimates?

They are rules of thumb fitted to many real drag-strip runs, and usually land within about 10% of a dyno figure for a car with good traction. Very light or very heavy cars, poor launches, headwinds and high altitude all push the estimate off.

Is this power at the engine or at the wheels?

At the engine (flywheel). Power at the wheels is lower, often by 10% to 20%, because of losses in the gearbox, driveshaft and tires.

Which weight should I use?

The weight of the car as it ran, with the driver and the fuel on board. A bathroom-scale estimate of the driver plus the car's kerb weight from the handbook is close enough.

Why do the two methods give different numbers?

The elapsed time includes the launch, so wheelspin or a slow start makes it look like less power. The trap speed depends mostly on how hard the car pulls at the top end, so it is usually the better guide. A big gap between the two often means traction was lost.
