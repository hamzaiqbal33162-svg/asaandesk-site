What this engine horsepower calculator estimates

This engine horsepower calculator estimates the power of a car's engine from how it performs over a quarter mile (402 metres) on a drag strip. You need just two numbers: the weight of the car with the driver, and either the elapsed time (ET) or the trap speed printed on the time slip.

Horsepower is a rate of doing work. The unit goes back to James Watt, who rated his steam engines against horses; one mechanical horsepower is 550 foot-pounds of work per second, which NIST puts at 745.7 watts. A car that turns more horsepower into motion, for the same weight, covers the quarter mile sooner and crosses the line faster, and the two rules used here work backwards from that.

How to use the calculator

  • Pick a tab: "From the time" uses the elapsed time, "From the trap speed" uses the speed at the line, and "Both" shows the two estimates with their average.
  • Car weight with the driver: the weight as the car ran, in lb, kg, US tons (2,000 lb) or tonnes (1,000 kg). Add the driver, fuel and anything else on board to the kerb weight.
  • Quarter-mile time (ET): from the start line to the end of the quarter mile, normally in seconds.
  • Trap speed at the quarter mile: your speed as you cross the quarter-mile line, in mph, km/h or m/s. It is not the average speed over the run.
  • Read the results: the estimated horsepower, the same power in watts, kilowatts and metric horsepower (PS), and the weight the engine moves for each horsepower. The steps show both formulas with your numbers.

The engine horsepower formulas behind it

Both rules are drag-racing rules of thumb, fitted to many real runs rather than derived from first principles. They assume a well-driven car with good traction at roughly sea level.

The two quarter-mile rules
MethodFormulaBest for
Elapsed timehp = weight (lb) ÷ (ET ÷ 5.825)³Clean launches with no wheelspin
Trap speedhp = weight (lb) × (mph ÷ 234)³Most cars; a poor start affects it little
BothAverage of the twoA quick check that the run was clean

The cube in each formula matters. Air drag rises steeply with speed, so a car needs much more power for a small gain in trap speed: going 10% faster at the line takes about a third more horsepower.

Worked examples in pounds and kilograms

  • Sports sedan, US units: 3,200 lb with the driver, an ET of 13.2 seconds and a trap speed of 104 mph. The time rule gives 3,200 ÷ (13.2 ÷ 5.825)³ = 274.99 hp and the trap rule 3,200 × (104 ÷ 234)³ = 280.93 hp. The average is 278 hp, which is 207.3 kW or 282 PS, and the car carries 11.5 lb (5.2 kg) per horsepower.
  • Small family car, metric units: 1,100 kg with the driver (2,425.08 lb), an ET of 17.5 seconds and 129 km/h at the line. The calculator turns 129 km/h into 80.157 mph and gives 89 hp from the time and 97 hp from the trap speed, an average of 93 hp (69.7 kW, 95 PS).
  • Heavier car, trap speed only: 1.6 tonnes and 180 km/h at the quarter-mile line. That is 3,527.4 lb and 111.847 mph, so the estimate is 385 hp, 287.2 kW or 391 PS, with 9.2 lb (4.2 kg) per horsepower.

When the two methods disagree a lot, the launch is usually to blame. The same 3,200 lb car with a slow 15.0 second ET but the same 104 mph trap speed gives 187 hp from the time and 281 hp from the speed; the calculator warns that the gap is over 15% and that the trap figure is the better guide.

Horsepower units: hp, bhp, PS and kW

Car makers quote power in several units, and the results show the main ones together.

A metric horsepower is about 1.4% smaller than a mechanical one, so the PS figure for the same engine is a little higher. Brake horsepower is not a different size of unit; the word brake says the power was measured at the engine, before the gearbox and wheels.

Other ways to find engine horsepower

A dynamometer (dyno) is the proper way to measure power. An engine dyno loads the engine on a stand; a chassis dyno spins the car's wheels on rollers and reads power at the wheels, which is lower than engine power because of gearbox, driveshaft and tyre losses. The quarter-mile rules here estimate engine power.

From torque and rpm: power is torque times rotational speed. In US units, hp = torque in lb-ft × rpm ÷ 5,252, and in metric units kW = torque in N·m × rpm ÷ 9,549. That is why the horsepower and lb-ft curves on a dyno chart always cross at 5,252 rpm.

  • From bore, stroke and cc: bore and stroke give the displacement (π ÷ 4 × bore² × stroke × number of cylinders), the cc figure that Pakistani and other car adverts quote. Displacement alone does not give horsepower, because the same cc can make very different power depending on rpm, valves, tuning and whether the engine has a turbocharger.
  • With boost: a turbo or supercharger raises the pressure of the air going in. The pressure ratio is (boost + atmospheric pressure) ÷ atmospheric pressure, with standard sea-level air at 14.696 psi (101.325 kPa). Power rises with that ratio in theory, but heat, fuel and engine limits mean real gains are smaller, so a dyno or a quarter-mile run is the honest test.
  • From the spec sheet: maker figures are given at a stated rpm, such as peak power at a certain engine speed; your engine only makes that power near that rpm.

Tips for a reliable estimate

  • Only use times from a drag strip or a closed course. Timing runs on public roads is dangerous and illegal.
  • Weigh the car if you can. A 5% error in weight gives a 5% error in horsepower, because both formulas scale directly with weight.
  • Use your best clean run, not the average of runs with wheelspin.
  • Expect lower figures at high altitude or in hot weather: thinner air means less engine power and a slower run, and the rules do not correct for it.
  • Treat the answer as an estimate within about 10%, good for comparing changes to the same car on the same strip.

Power-to-weight: why light cars feel fast

The "Weight per horsepower" line divides the weight by the power. Fewer pounds or kilograms per horsepower means faster acceleration, which is why a light car with a modest engine can keep up with a heavy car with a bigger one. In the examples above, the sports sedan carries 11.5 lb per hp and the small family car 25.9 lb per hp.

Cutting weight improves the ratio just as adding power does. Removing 100 lb from the 3,200 lb car would lower its weight per horsepower without touching the engine, and its quarter-mile time would drop a little as a result.

How it works

A quarter-mile run on a drag strip says a lot about an engine: the more power a car of a given weight has, the sooner it covers the 402 metres and the faster it is going at the end. Enter the car's weight with the driver and either the elapsed time (ET) or the trap speed from the time slip. Two well-known drag-racing rules of thumb turn those into engine horsepower; the Both tab shows the two estimates side by side with their average. The trap-speed figure is usually the steadier one, because a poor launch or wheelspin slows the elapsed time much more than the speed at the line.

The formula

  • From the elapsed time: hp = weight (lb) ÷ (ET in seconds ÷ 5.825)³
  • From the trap speed: hp = weight (lb) × (speed in mph ÷ 234)³
  • Watts = hp × 745.69987 (1 hp = 550 foot-pounds per second); metric hp (PS) = watts ÷ 735.49875
  • 1 kg = 2.20462 lb; 1 US ton = 2,000 lb; 1 mile per hour = 1.609344 km/h

A worked example

A 3,500 lb car with its driver runs the quarter mile in 14 seconds: 14 ÷ 5.825 = 2.403, cubed is 13.88, and 3,500 ÷ 13.88 ≈ 252 hp. If it crosses the line at 100 mph, 100 ÷ 234 = 0.4274, cubed is 0.0781, and 3,500 × 0.0781 ≈ 273 hp. The average of the two is about 263 hp, or 196 kW.

Questions

How do I calculate engine horsepower?

With a quarter-mile run: divide the weight in pounds by (ET ÷ 5.825) cubed, or multiply it by (trap speed in mph ÷ 234) cubed. A 3,200 lb car at 104 mph comes to about 281 hp. On a dyno, power is measured directly, and from torque it is lb-ft × rpm ÷ 5,252.

What is the engine horsepower formula?

Power equals work per unit of time; one horsepower is 550 foot-pounds per second. For engines, hp = torque (lb-ft) × rpm ÷ 5,252. For drag-strip estimates, this page uses hp = weight ÷ (ET ÷ 5.825)³ and hp = weight × (mph ÷ 234)³.

What is the engine power formula in kW?

kW = torque in newton-metres × rpm ÷ 9,549, or kW = hp × 0.7457. The calculator converts its horsepower estimate to kilowatts and metric horsepower automatically.

How accurate are these estimates?

They are rules of thumb fitted to many real drag-strip runs, and usually land within about 10% of a dyno figure for a car with good traction. Very light or very heavy cars, poor launches, headwinds and high altitude all push the estimate off.

Is this power at the engine or at the wheels?

At the engine (flywheel). Power at the wheels is lower, often by 10% to 20%, because of losses in the gearbox, driveshaft and tires.

Can I calculate horsepower from cc or displacement?

Not exactly. Displacement in cc tells you the swept volume of the cylinders, but power also depends on rpm, tuning and boost, so two 1,300 cc engines can differ a lot. A quarter-mile run, a dyno test or the maker's figure gives a real number.

What is the difference between hp, bhp and PS?

Hp and bhp are the same unit, 745.7 W; bhp just means it was measured at the engine. PS, or metric horsepower, is 735.5 W, so the PS number is about 1.4% higher for the same engine.

Which weight should I use?

The weight of the car as it ran, with the driver and the fuel on board. A bathroom-scale estimate of the driver plus the car's kerb weight from the handbook is close enough.

Why do the two methods give different numbers?

The elapsed time includes the launch, so wheelspin or a slow start makes it look like less power. The trap speed depends mostly on how hard the car pulls at the top end, so it is usually the better guide. A big gap between the two often means traction was lost.
