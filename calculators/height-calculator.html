What this height calculator predicts

This height calculator estimates how tall a child is likely to be as an adult. With the child's age, sex, height and weight plus both parents' heights it uses the Khamis-Roche method, a formula published in the journal Pediatrics in 1994. With the parents' heights alone it gives the simpler mid-parental target height for a boy and for a girl, which also works before a baby is born.

Height runs strongly in families, but it is also shaped by nutrition, health and the timing of puberty, so any prediction is an estimate. It is a guide for curious parents and a prompt for a conversation with a doctor when something looks unusual, not a medical diagnosis.

How to use the height calculator

  • Choose a tab: "With the child's measurements" for the Khamis-Roche estimate, or "Parents' heights only" for the mid-parental target.
  • Use the switch at the top for Metric (cm and kg) or US (feet, inches and pounds). You can also type a height such as 4ft2in or 128cm straight into a box.
  • Enter the child's age in years (decimals such as 8.5 are fine), Boy or Girl, the child's height and weight, and the mother's and father's heights.
  • Under More options, tick "The parents' heights were measured, not remembered" if you measured them; otherwise they are lowered slightly, because people tend to remember themselves a little taller.
  • For a child under 4 the calculator shows the parents-only target, because the Khamis-Roche tables start at age 4.

The height formulas: Khamis-Roche and mid-parental height

Khamis-Roche: adult height = B0 + b1 × the child's height + b2 × the child's weight + b3 × the parents' average, with heights in inches and weight in pounds. The four numbers B0 to b3 come from tables for each half year of age from 4 to 17.5, separately for boys and girls, and the calculator blends two rows for ages in between. The weight usually has a small negative coefficient: at the same height a heavier child tends to be further along in growth.

Mid-parental target: add the mother's and father's heights, add 5 inches (12.7 cm) for a boy or subtract 5 inches for a girl, and divide by 2. That is the parents' average plus or minus 2.5 inches (6.35 cm), the usual average difference between men and women. With parents of 158 cm and 172 cm the targets are 171.4 cm for a boy and 158.6 cm for a girl, shown rounded as 171 cm and 159 cm.

Doctors can also estimate adult height from bone age, read from an X-ray of the hand and wrist, which shows how much growing the bones have left. That needs a clinic and is not part of this page.

Worked examples: a girl in inches and a family in Pakistan

  • Girl, US units: 7.5 years old, 4 ft 1 in (49 in) and 52 lb, with measured parents of 5 ft 5 in and 5 ft 11 in. She has reached 74.8% of it and has about 16.5 in to grow.

To measure height at home without a stadiometer, stand barefoot with heels, back and head against a wall, place a hardback book flat on the head, mark the wall under it, and measure from the floor to the mark with a tape. Measure in the morning, when most people are slightly taller.

Growth charts, height for age and percentiles

One estimate says less than a child's growth over time. Doctors plot height for age on growth charts: the WHO Child Growth Standards from birth to 5 years and the WHO growth reference from 5 to 19 years, or the CDC charts in the United States. A child following the same percentile curve year after year is usually growing normally, whatever that percentile is.

The WHO calls a young child stunted when height for age is more than two standard deviations below the WHO median, a sign of long-term undernutrition or illness. In Pakistan, the National Nutrition Survey 2018 measured the nutrition of children under five, including their height for age. If you are worried about a child's growth, a health worker can measure the child and plot a proper chart.

What affects how tall a child grows

  • Genes: tall parents tend to have tall children, which is why both formulas lean on the parents' heights. Children of very tall or very short parents usually end up a little closer to the average than their parents.
  • Nutrition: enough energy, protein, calcium, iron and other nutrients in pregnancy and childhood supports growth, and long-term undernutrition limits it.
  • Health: frequent infections, untreated long-term illness and some hormone problems can slow growth.
  • Puberty timing: early developers grow fast and stop early; late bloomers grow later and often keep going for longer. The Khamis-Roche estimate is least certain for both.
  • Sleep and activity: regular sleep and physical activity are part of healthy growth, but no exercise or supplement is known to add height beyond what the body would reach with good nutrition and health.

Accuracy, limits and when to see a doctor

For most healthy children the Khamis-Roche estimate is within about 2 inches (5 cm) of final height, and the calculator shows that range. The method was built from white American children without growth problems, so it is less certain for other groups, for early or late developers, and for children with a medical condition. The parents-only target is wider still: most children end up within about 8 to 10 cm (3 to 4 in) of it.

Ask a doctor if a child is far shorter or taller than the parents' target suggests, grows very little over a year, crosses percentile lines on a growth chart, or starts puberty much earlier or later than friends.

How it works

For a child aged 4 to 17.5, the Khamis-Roche method predicts adult height from four numbers: the child's current height and weight, and the average of the parents' heights. Each age and sex has its own set of weights for these numbers, worked out from children who were measured every six months until they were grown; between two half-year ages the weights are blended. Because most people remember their height a little too tall, the parents' heights are adjusted down slightly first, unless you tick that they were measured. Without the child's measurements (or under age 4), the target height comes from the parents alone: their average plus 2.5 inches (6.35 cm) for a boy, or minus that for a girl.

The formula

  • Adult height (in) = B0 + b1 × height (in) + b2 × weight (lb) + b3 × parents' average (in), with B0, b1, b2, b3 for the child's age and sex (Khamis and Roche 1994, ages 4 to 17.5)
  • Reported parents' heights, adjusted: mother = 2.803 + 0.953 × height (in), father = 2.316 + 0.955 × height (in)
  • Parents only (mid-parental target): boy = (mother + father + 5 in) ÷ 2, girl = (mother + father − 5 in) ÷ 2
  • US results are rounded to 0.1 inch and metric results to a whole centimetre

A worked example

A boy of 5.2 years, 44 in (3 ft 8 in) tall and 40 lb, with parents of 65 in and 70 in. Adjusted parents: 2.803 + 0.953 × 65 = 64.75 and 2.316 + 0.955 × 70 = 69.17, average 66.96 in. The coefficients 40% of the way from the 5.0 to the 5.5 row are −11.075, 1.09396, −0.06197 and 0.53828, so the adult height is −11.075 + 1.09396 × 44 − 0.06197 × 40 + 0.53828 × 66.96 = 70.6 in, or 5 ft 10.6 in.

Questions

How tall will my child be?

Enter your child's age, sex, height and weight and both parents' heights on the first tab. The Khamis-Roche estimate is usually within about 5 cm (2 in) of the final height for healthy children aged 4 to 17.5. A 10-year-old boy of 138 cm and 31 kg with parents of 158 and 172 cm comes out at about 174 cm.

How do I calculate height from the parents' heights?

Add the mother's and father's heights, add 12.7 cm (5 in) for a boy or subtract 12.7 cm for a girl, then divide by 2. Parents of 158 cm and 172 cm give about 171 cm for a son and 159 cm for a daughter. The "Parents' heights only" tab does this in cm or inches.

What is the height formula for a child?

The Khamis-Roche formula is adult height = B0 + b1 × height + b2 × weight + b3 × parents' average, with heights in inches, weight in pounds and the four coefficients taken from tables for the child's age and sex. The simpler mid-parental formula uses only the parents' heights.

How accurate is the prediction?

For most healthy children the Khamis-Roche estimate lands within about 2 inches (5 cm) of the final height. It is less certain for children who start puberty very early or very late, and for groups unlike the American children the method was built from.

How do I convert height from cm to feet?

Divide the centimetres by 2.54 to get inches, then divide by 12 for feet and keep the remainder as inches. For example 165 cm is 64.96 inches, which is 5 ft 5.0 in, and 175 cm is 5 ft 8.9 in. The table on this page lists common heights.

Why does the calculator need the child's weight?

At the same height, a heavier child tends to be further along in growth, so a little less growth is left. Weight changes the estimate only slightly; height and the parents' heights matter most.

What happens for a child under 4?

The Khamis-Roche tables start at age 4, so for younger children the calculator shows the target height from the parents' heights alone. That target does not use the child's own height or weight.

Can a late bloomer grow taller than predicted?

Yes, the estimate is less reliable for late developers. A child who starts puberty late may look short for a while and then keep growing for longer, often ending up near the parents' target. A doctor can judge this from growth over time or from bone age.

Should I use measured or remembered heights for the parents?

Measured heights are best. If the parents' heights are remembered, leave the box unticked: the method then lowers them slightly, because people tend to remember themselves a bit taller than they are.

When should I talk to a doctor?

If a child is much shorter or taller than the parents' target suggests, grows very slowly over a year, or starts puberty very early or late, ask a doctor. They can track growth on a chart over time, which says much more than one estimate.
