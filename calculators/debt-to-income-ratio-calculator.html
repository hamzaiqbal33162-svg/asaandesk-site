What is a debt-to-income ratio?

Your debt-to-income ratio (DTI) is the share of your gross monthly income that goes to debt payments. Gross income is pay before tax and other deductions. A DTI of 34% means 34 cents of every dollar you earn is already promised to a lender or landlord before you buy food or pay a bill.

Lenders use the ratio to judge whether you can take on a new payment, and it is one of the main numbers checked when you apply for a mortgage, a car loan or a personal loan. It is also a useful health check for your own budget, because a high ratio leaves little room for savings or for a bad month.

DTI is not the same as your credit utilization ratio. Utilization compares card balances with card limits and feeds into your credit score; DTI compares payments with income and does not appear on your credit report.

How to use the debt-to-income ratio calculator

  • Enter Salary and wages (before tax) and set the period to a year or a month. The default is 60,000 a year.
  • Open More income to add Pension and Social Security, Investment and savings income and Other income, such as rental income or alimony received.
  • Enter Rent or Mortgage payment. Open More home costs for Property tax, Home insurance and HOA or building fees.
  • Enter Credit card payments (the minimum due, not the balance), Car loan payments and Student loan payments. Open More debts for personal loans, child support or alimony paid.
  • Each box has its own a month or a year switch, so you can type a yearly property tax bill and monthly loan payments without converting anything.
  • Open House estimate to change the Mortgage rate (default 6.5%), Loan term (30 years), Down payment (20% of the price) and Property tax and insurance (2% of the price a year).

Leave out living costs such as groceries, utilities, phone, fuel and subscriptions. They matter for your budget, but lenders do not count them as debt.

Understanding your results

The first headline is your back-end ratio, which is what most people mean by DTI. The second is the front-end ratio, which counts housing costs only. A gauge shows where you sit on the scale, and a table lists every income and debt by month and by year.

Worked examples

The default case. A 60,000 salary is 5,000 a month. Rent of 1,200, card payments of 150 and a 350 car payment total 1,700, so the DTI is 34% (good) and the front-end ratio is 24%. The house estimate allows 1,300 a month, because 36% of 5,000 is 1,800 and 500 of that is already taken by the card and car payments. At 6.5% over 30 years with 20% down, that payment buys a home of about 193,360.

Paying off the car. Set the car payment to 0 and the DTI falls to 27%. The new home payment rises to 1,400 (now the 28% limit is the smaller one) and the price to 208,234.

A homeowner with a mortgage. Income of 4,000 a month in wages plus 12,000 a year of pension is 5,000 a month. A 1,500 mortgage, 3,600 a year of property tax, 1,200 a year of insurance, 300 of card payments and a 400 student loan give 2,600 of debt: a DTI of 52% (high) and a front-end ratio of 38%.

A Pakistan example. The ratio works in any currency, so you can type rupee amounts even though the boxes show a dollar sign. Take-home pay of Rs 180,000 a month with a car loan installment of Rs 45,000, a credit card payment of Rs 10,000 and a personal loan of Rs 15,000 gives 70,000 ÷ 180,000 = 38.9%. Read the house estimate rows only for US-style mortgages.

What DTI do mortgage lenders allow?

Limits depend on the loan program and on the rest of your file: credit score, savings, down payment and the size of the jump in housing cost. The figures below come from the program rules themselves, and they change, so check the latest guide or ask your lender.

The 28% front-end and 36% back-end pair used in the house estimate is a long-standing rule of thumb for conventional loans, not a legal limit. The CFPB simply notes that different loan products and lenders have different DTI limits.

Debt ratio rules in Canada and Pakistan

Canadian lenders use two ratios. The gross debt service ratio (GDS) counts mortgage payments, property tax, heating and half of any condo fees, and the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada says it should normally be no more than 39% of gross household income. The total debt service ratio (TDS) adds every other debt and should normally be no more than 44%.

In Pakistan, the State Bank of Pakistan caps the debt burden ratio for consumer financing such as personal loans, car finance and credit cards. In September 2021 it lowered the maximum from 50% to 40% of the borrower's net disposable income, so banks measure against take-home pay rather than gross pay. For a home loan the limit is higher: the Prudential Regulations for Housing Finance (updated 18 August 2026) allow all monthly installments, the home loan plus any other consumer financing, to reach 65% of net disposable income. Check your bank's current policy, because the State Bank revises these rules.

How to lower your debt-to-income ratio

  • Pay off a small loan completely. Removing a whole monthly payment, like the 350 car loan above, cuts the ratio faster than spreading extra money across every debt.
  • Avoid new credit before you apply for a mortgage. A new car or a store card adds a payment to the top of the ratio.
  • Raise documented income. A raise, overtime or a second job that you can show on pay slips or tax returns counts; a raise from 60,000 to 66,000 takes the default DTI from 34% to 30.9%.
  • Refinance or consolidate high-interest debt at a lower rate or longer term to cut the monthly payment, but check the total interest before you do.
  • Add a co-borrower whose income will also be on the loan, if they will share the debt.
  • Look at a cheaper home or a larger down payment. A 10% down payment instead of 20% cuts the default home price estimate from 193,360 to 176,744.

Common mistakes when you calculate DTI

  • Using take-home pay for a US loan. US lenders use gross income, so net pay makes your ratio look worse than the lender's number.
  • Typing a card balance instead of the minimum payment. Only the monthly amount due counts.
  • Counting utilities, food or insurance on your car. These are living costs, not debts.
  • Forgetting yearly bills. Property tax and home insurance billed once a year still count; set their period to a year.
  • Leaving out the new loan. Lenders judge the ratio with the payment you are applying for, so test the new payment before you apply.

How it works

Type your income before tax and each debt payment you make, choosing for each box whether the amount is per month or per year. Everything is turned into monthly amounts and added up. Your debt-to-income ratio is all your monthly debt payments divided by your monthly income; the front-end ratio counts only housing costs (rent or mortgage, property tax, home insurance and HOA fees). The rating uses the usual lender bands, and the house estimate shows the largest new home payment the common 28% and 36% rules leave room for, and the price it could buy.

The formula

  • Monthly amount = yearly amount ÷ 12 for every box set to "a year"
  • Debt-to-income (back-end) = all monthly debt payments ÷ monthly income × 100
  • Front-end = monthly housing costs ÷ monthly income × 100
  • Rating: 36% or less good, up to 50% mid-range, 50% or more high
  • New home payment = the smaller of 28% of income and 36% of income − your other (non-housing) debt payments
  • Home price P from that payment: (1 − down%) × P × f + tax and insurance% × P ÷ 12 = payment, with f the monthly payment of 1 borrowed over the loan term

A worked example

A $60,000 salary is $5,000 a month. Rent of $1,200, card payments of $150 and a $350 car payment add up to $1,700, so the ratio is 1,700 ÷ 5,000 = 34% (good) and the front-end ratio is 1,200 ÷ 5,000 = 24%. If the rent stopped, a lender could allow the smaller of 28% × 5,000 = $1,400 and 36% × 5,000 − 500 = $1,300 for a new home payment, which at 6.5% over 30 years with 20% down and 2% a year for tax and insurance buys a home of about $193,360.

Questions

How do I calculate my debt-to-income ratio?

Add up every monthly debt payment, including rent or mortgage, minimum card payments and loan payments, and divide by your gross monthly income, then multiply by 100. For example, 1,700 of payments on 5,000 of income is 34%. The calculator does the sums and converts yearly amounts for you.

What is a good debt-to-income ratio?

Most lenders like 36% or less, with no more than about 28% going to housing, and this calculator rates 36% or less as good. Some loan programs go higher, such as up to 50% through Fannie Mae's automated underwriting. Above 50% most lenders will say no.

What is the debt-to-income ratio for a mortgage approval?

It depends on the program. Fannie Mae's base limit for manually underwritten loans is 36%, rising to 45% with strong credit and reserves, FHA manual underwriting uses 31% front-end and 43% back-end, and VA uses a 41% guideline. Your lender will tell you which applies.

What is the debt-to-income ratio for an FHA loan?

For manually underwritten FHA loans the standard limits are 31% for housing and 43% for all debts. With a credit score of 580 or more and compensating factors such as cash reserves, they can go up to 37/47, 40/40 or 40/50. Loans approved through FHA's automated system can be judged differently.

How do I calculate debt-to-income ratio with credit cards?

Use the minimum payment shown on each statement, not the balance or the amount you choose to pay. Add the minimums together and enter the total in Credit card payments. A card with no balance has no payment and adds nothing.

Should I use income before or after tax?

For US loans, before tax: lenders work from gross monthly income. In Pakistan, the State Bank's debt burden ratio is measured against net disposable income, so use take-home pay there.

Is rent included in the debt-to-income ratio?

Yes, in this calculator and in most lenders' view of your current budget, rent counts as a housing cost. When you apply for a mortgage, the new home payment replaces the rent, which is what the house estimate rows assume.

What is the difference between front-end and back-end DTI?

Front-end counts only housing costs: rent or mortgage, property tax, home insurance and HOA fees. Back-end counts every debt payment, housing included, and is the number usually meant by your DTI.

How is the debt-to-income ratio calculated in Canada?

Canadian lenders use GDS (housing costs, including heating and half of condo fees) and TDS (housing plus all other debts), both against gross household income. The usual guidelines are 39% for GDS and 44% for TDS. The back-end ratio here is close to TDS, but it does not add heating costs.

How is the home price estimate worked out?

It assumes your current rent or mortgage stops, takes the largest payment the 28% and 36% rules allow, and finds the price whose loan payment plus property tax and insurance fits it. Change the rate, term, down payment and tax and insurance share under House estimate.
