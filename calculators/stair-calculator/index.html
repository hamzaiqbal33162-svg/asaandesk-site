What the stair calculator works out

This stair calculator plans a straight stair from the height it has to climb. It finds the number of steps, the rise and run of each step, the total run, the angle, and the length and height of the stringers, the sloped boards that carry the treads. Every option is checked against the stair limits of the International Residential Code (IRC 2021).

Two tabs cover the two common jobs. Step options lists every sensible number of risers for your rise and points out the best one. Full design works out one stair in detail, with treads on the stringers, the way the stringers meet the upper floor and a headroom check under the floor above. It works in feet and inches or in metric.

How to use the stair calculator

  • Measure the total rise from the finished lower floor to the finished upper floor, including any floor covering still to come.
  • I know: choose the run of one step (the tread depth you want) or the total run (the floor space the stair may take).
  • Step options: read the suggested stair and the table of every option with a rise of 5 to 9.5 in.
  • Full design: set the number of risers, or the height of each step, and choose a Standard or Flush stringer mount.
  • Under Treads and headroom, tick "Treads sit on the stringers" and give the tread thickness, and tick the headroom check to enter the floor opening, the floor thickness and the headroom you need.
  • Every box has its own unit menu, so you can mix feet, inches, meters and centimeters.

How to calculate stair rise and run

Divide the total rise by a trial number of risers to get the rise of each step, then adjust until the rise is comfortable and within the code. 110 in ÷ 16 = 6.875 in. All risers should be the same; the IRC allows no more than ⅜ inch difference between the largest and smallest in a flight.

The number of treads is one less than the risers with a standard mount (15 treads for 16 risers), or the same with a flush mount. The total run is treads × run (15 × 10.5 in = 157.5 in, or 13 ft 1.5 in), and the angle is arctan(rise ÷ run).

The stringer is the long side of a right triangle: stringer length = √(total run² + stringer height²). The calculator uses the stringer height to the top tread seat, which accounts for the mount and the tread thickness.

Stair formulas: 2R + T and the metric 2R + G rule

A stair feels natural when the step fits a normal stride. The rule of thumb in inches is 2 × rise + run of about 24 to 25½ in (61 to 65 cm), and the Full design tab shows this comfort check. A deck stair with a 42 in rise, 6 risers of 7 in and 11 in treads scores 2 × 7 + 11 = 25 in.

In England, Approved Document K (2013 edition) states the same idea in millimeters: twice the rise plus the going (2R + G) should be between 550 mm and 700 mm. For a private stair in a home it allows a rise of 150 to 220 mm, a going of 220 to 300 mm, a pitch of at most 42° and headroom of at least 2 m. Other countries publish their own figures, so check the rules where you build.

A metric example: a rise of 2.6 m with 14 equal risers gives 18.57 cm per step. With a 25 cm going the stair has 13 treads, a total run of 325 cm, a stringer of 404.86 cm and an angle of 36.61°, and 2R + G is 62.1 cm, inside both the comfort range and the 550 to 700 mm rule.

Full design: first step, stringer mount and headroom

With a fixed step height, the leftover height becomes the first step. A 108 in rise with 7.25 in steps gives 14 risers of 7.25 in and a first step of 6.5 in once a 1 in tread is added. The calculator flags that the two heights differ by more than ⅜ in, so setting the number of risers instead is the better choice.

A flush mount puts the top tread level with the upper floor and adds one tread to the stair; a standard mount hangs the stringers one rise below the floor. The stringer placement line tells you how far below the floor surface the top tread seat sits.

Headroom is measured straight up from the line along the front edges of the treads to the floor or ceiling above. In the 108 in example with a 9 ft floor opening and a 10 in thick floor, the headroom is only 5 ft 1.05 in, 1 ft 6.95 in short of 6 ft 8 in, and the calculator says the opening must be at least 11 ft 2.14 in long.

Stairs with a landing, deck stairs and other tips

  • A flight may rise at most 151 in under the IRC. For a taller rise, or for an L- or U-shaped stair, plan a landing and work out each flight on its own with the rise from floor to landing and from landing to floor.
  • For deck stairs, measure the rise from the deck surface to the ground where the bottom step will land; sloping ground changes the rise.
  • Measure to finished floors. Adding a 3/4 in floor covering at the top later makes the top step taller than the rest.
  • Cut one stringer, test it in place, then use it as the pattern for the others.
  • For stair carpet or flooring, each step needs about the tread depth plus the rise times the stair width, plus the nosing wrap; work it out from the rise and run shown here.

How it works

Measure the total rise from the finished lower floor to the finished upper floor, and either the run (tread depth) you want for each step or the total floor space the stair may take. Step options lists every number of risers that gives a rise of 5 to 9.5 inches, with the run, stringer length, angle and whether it meets the code limits, and suggests the one that meets them with a rise closest to 7 inches. Full design works out one stair: fix the number of risers (all equal) or the height of each step, choose how the stringers meet the upper floor, add the tread thickness and check the headroom under the floor above. Every box has its own unit menu.

The formula

  • Equal risers: rise = total rise ÷ number of risers. Fixed step height: full risers = whole part of total rise ÷ rise; the leftover becomes a shorter first step
  • Treads = risers − 1 with a standard mount (the upper floor is the top step), or = risers with a flush mount (the top tread is level with the floor)
  • Total run = treads × run; angle = arctan(rise ÷ run)
  • Stringer height = total rise − rise − tread (standard) or total rise − tread (flush); stringer length = √(total run² + stringer height²)
  • Headroom at the edge of the floor opening = rise ÷ run × opening (minus one run for a flush mount), at most the total rise, minus the floor thickness
  • Code limits (IRC 2021 R311.7): rise at most 7¾ in, run at least 10 in, risers within ⅜ in of each other, headroom at least 6 ft 8 in, at most 151 in of rise per flight

A worked example

A total rise of 10 ft (120 in) with 16 equal risers gives a rise of 7.5 in. With a standard mount there are 15 treads; at 10 in each the total run is 12 ft 6 in. The stringer rises 120 − 7.5 = 112.5 in, so it is √(150² + 112.5²) = 187.5 in = 15 ft 7.5 in long, at an angle of 36.87°.

Questions

How do I calculate stair steps?

Divide the total rise by the rise you want for each step and round to a whole number of risers, then divide the total rise by that number to make every step equal. A 110 in rise with steps near 7 in gives 16 risers of 6.88 in and, with a standard mount, 15 treads.

How do I calculate stair rise and run?

Rise = total rise ÷ number of risers; total run = number of treads × run of one step. Keep the rise at most 7¾ in and the run at least 10 in under the IRC, and aim for 2 × rise + run of about 24 to 25½ in.

How do I calculate the length of a stair stringer?

Use the Pythagorean theorem: stringer length = √(total run² + stringer height²). For 15 treads of 10.5 in and a stringer height of 103.125 in, that is about 15 ft 8.26 in; buy boards a little longer for the cuts at each end.

What is the stair formula 2R + G?

It is the stride rule: twice the rise plus the going (tread depth). England's Approved Document K asks for 550 to 700 mm, and the common US rule of thumb is about 24 to 25½ in. A rise of 18.57 cm with a 25 cm going gives 62.1 cm.

What are the code limits for home stairs?

Under the International Residential Code (IRC 2021, R311.7) a riser may be at most 7¾ inches and a tread at least 10 inches deep, all risers in a flight must be within ⅜ inch of each other, the headroom must be at least 6 ft 8 in, and a flight may rise at most 151 inches before a landing. Local rules can differ, so check with your building department.

Does the calculator work in metric?

Yes. Switch to Metric, or pick meters and centimeters in each unit menu. The code checks are shown in both units, and the comfort check gives centimeters.

How do I plan stairs with a landing?

Split the stair at the landing and work out each flight separately, using the height from the lower floor to the landing and from the landing to the upper floor. Use the same rise in both flights so every step feels the same.

What is the difference between a riser and a tread?

A riser is the vertical height of one step and a tread is the flat part you stand on. A straight stair with a standard mount has one tread fewer than risers, because the upper floor itself is the last step.

Standard or flush stringer mount?

With a standard mount the stringers hang one riser below the upper floor and the floor's edge is the top step. With a flush mount the stringers reach the top of the floor and the top tread is level with it, which adds one tread and one run to the stair's length.

How is headroom measured?

Straight up from the sloped line that touches the front edge of every tread to the underside of the floor or ceiling above. Enter the length of the opening in the upper floor and its thickness; if the headroom is too low, the calculator tells you how long the opening needs to be.

What is stair nosing?

The nosing is the front edge of a tread that sticks out past the riser below. It gives the foot a little more room on each step; the code sets limits on how far it may project, so check them for your area.
