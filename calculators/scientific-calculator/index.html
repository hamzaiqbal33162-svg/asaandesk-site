What is a scientific calculator?

A scientific calculator goes beyond adding and multiplying: it has keys for trigonometry (sin, cos, tan and their inverses), powers and roots, logarithms, factorials, the constants π and e, and numbers in scientific notation. It is the calculator students use from secondary school through college physics, chemistry and maths, and the one engineers keep on the desk.

This free online scientific calculator runs in your browser on a phone, tablet or PC, so there is nothing to download or install. It follows the normal order of operations, shows the answer live as you type, keeps the last answer as Ans, has a memory, and lists your recent calculations so you can reuse them.

A graphing calculator adds a larger screen that draws graphs of functions. For plotting you need one of those, but for working out numbers, an exercise or a formula in an exam-style question, a scientific calculator is all you need.

How to use this scientific calculator

  • Choose Angles in: Degrees or Radians before using sin, cos, tan or their inverses. A DEG or RAD tag on the display shows which one is on.
  • Tap the keys or type. On a keyboard, digits, + − * / ^ ( ) ! % and the point work as you expect; s, c, t and l type sin, cos, tan and ln; p types π, e types e, and a types Ans.
  • Watch the answer appear as you type. A function key such as sin opens a bracket for you; if you forget to close it, the calculator closes it at the end and says so.
  • Press = or Enter to finish. The result becomes Ans and goes into the history above the display; tap any line there to use it again.
  • Press an operator straight after = to carry on from Ans, or a digit to start a new calculation. ⌫ (Backspace) deletes the last key and AC (Esc) clears everything.
  • Use M+ to add the answer to the memory, M− to take it away, MR to type the memory into the calculation and MC to clear it. An M tag shows when the memory is not zero.

Order of operations (BODMAS and PEMDAS)

The calculator works out a line the way a maths teacher would, often remembered as BODMAS or PEMDAS: brackets first, then powers and roots, then multiplication and division, then addition and subtraction. So 2 + 3 × 4 is 14, not 20, and (2 + 3) × 4 is 20.

A few rules are worth knowing because handheld calculators do not all agree on them. Powers go from right to left, so 2^3^2 means 2^9 = 512. The minus sign in front of a power applies after the power, so −2² = −4; type (−2)² for 4. A number written next to a bracket, π or a function is multiplied, so 2π, 3(4) and 2sin(30) all work.

When you are unsure, add brackets. They never change a correct answer and they make the calculation say exactly what you mean, which matters in long formulas such as (a + b) ÷ (c − d).

Degrees and radians, and common trig values

Angles can be measured in degrees (a full turn is 360°) or radians (a full turn is 2π). To convert, multiply degrees by π ÷ 180, or radians by 180 ÷ π. School geometry and most physics exercises use degrees; calculus and many physics formulas use radians.

The wrong setting is the most common reason for a strange trig answer. In Degrees, sin(30) is 0.5; in Radians the same keys give about −0.988, because 30 radians is almost five full turns. The values below are what the calculator shows in Degrees.

Logarithms and antilogarithms

The log key gives the common logarithm (base 10): log(1000) = 3 because 10³ = 1000. The ln key gives the natural logarithm (base e ≈ 2.71828): ln(e^5) = 5. Logs of 0 or of negative numbers have no real value, and the calculator says so.

There is no separate antilog key because the antilog is just a power. The antilog of a common log is 10ˣ, so the antilog of 2.5 is 10^(2.5) = 316.22776602; the antilog of a natural log is eˣ. For a log in any other base, divide two logs: log₂(64) = ln(64) ÷ ln(2) = 6.

Chemistry uses logs for pH, which is minus the base-10 log of the hydrogen-ion concentration, so each whole pH step is a tenfold change. A solution with pH 3.2 has a hydrogen-ion concentration of 10^(−3.2), about 0.000631 mol/L.

Powers, roots, factorials and scientific notation

Use xʸ for any power, including fractions and negatives: 2^−3 = 0.125 and 8^(1÷3) = 2. A negative number to a fractional power is real only when the fraction has an odd bottom number, so (−8)^(1÷3) = −2 but (−8)^(1÷2) has no real answer.

n! multiplies all whole numbers up to n: 5! = 120 and 0! = 1. Factorials grow fast, and 170! (about 7.26 × 10^306) is the largest the calculator can show; 171! is too large for a standard double-precision number. For numbers that are not whole, such as 3.5!, the calculator uses the gamma function, x! = Γ(x + 1).

To type a number in scientific notation, use EXP: 6.02214076E23 is the Avogadro constant, 6.02214076 × 10^23 per mole, and 3E8 is roughly the speed of light in metres per second (exactly 299,792,458 m/s). Answers of 10^15 or more, or smaller than 10^−6, are shown as a number times a power of ten, which is how to read scientific notation on this calculator.

Worked examples for physics, maths and money

  • Resolving a force: a 50 N pull at 30° above the ground has a horizontal part of 50 × cos(30) = 43.301270189 N and a vertical part of 50 × sin(30) = 25 N. Set Degrees first.
  • Kinetic energy: a 1,200 kg car at 25 m/s has ½mv² = 0.5 × 1200 × 25² = 375,000 J.
  • Combinations: the number of ways to choose 3 people from 10 is 10! ÷ (3! × 7!) = 120.
  • Growth: PKR 500,000 growing at 12% a year for 5 years becomes 500000 × 1.12^5 = PKR 881,170.84. Note that the % key is not used here; 12% growth is the factor 1.12.
  • Right triangle: legs of 6 and 8 give a longest side of √(6² + 8²) = 10, and the angle opposite the 6 is tan⁻¹(6 ÷ 8) = 36.869897646° in Degrees.

These are the kinds of exercises met in first-year college physics and maths. Typing them as one line, with brackets, avoids rounding the middle steps by hand.

Accuracy, error messages and the % key

Each step is worked out with standard double-precision numbers, about 15 to 16 significant digits. The display rounds the answer to 12 characters, which hides tiny binary rounding noise (0.1 + 0.2 shows as 0.3), and a More digits line shows up to 15 digits when they differ.

Instead of a bare Error, the calculator tells you what went wrong: the tangent of 90° is undefined, you cannot divide by zero, square roots and even roots of negative numbers are not real numbers, log and ln need a number greater than 0, and sin⁻¹ and cos⁻¹ need a number from −1 to 1. While a calculation is unfinished, such as 2 + 3 ×, it simply waits.

The % key follows the keypad convention: it divides the number in front of it by 100. That is why 50 + 10% gives 50.1, not 55. To add 10 percent of 50, type 50 + 50 × 10%, or 50 × 1.1; for longer percentage sums, the percentage calculator explains each step.

How it works

Tap the keys or type on your keyboard. The calculation appears on the display and the answer is worked out as you go; press = (or Enter) to keep it. The answer then becomes Ans, so pressing an operator next carries on from it, and the last calculations are listed above the display so you can tap one to use it again. Brackets, powers, multiplication and division are done before adding and subtracting, the same order you learn at school. Choose Degrees or Radians before using sin, cos and tan. M+ and M− add the current answer to the memory or take it away, MR puts the memory into the calculation and MC clears it.

The formula

  • Order of operations: brackets → functions (sin, log, √ …) → x!, %, x², x³ → powers xʸ (right to left) → × and ÷ → + and −
  • Degrees to radians: angle in radians = angle in degrees × π ÷ 180
  • x% = x ÷ 100 (so 50 + 10% = 50.1)
  • n! = 1 × 2 × … × n, 0! = 1; for other numbers x! = Γ(x + 1)
  • ʸ√x = x^(1 ÷ y); 3E8 = 3 × 10^8
  • The display shows 12 characters: 11 minus the number of whole-number digits are kept after the point

A worked example

2 + 3 × 4: multiplication comes first, so 3 × 4 = 12 and then 2 + 12 = 14. In Degrees, sin(30) = 0.5; in Radians, sin(30) ≈ −0.9880316241 because 30 radians is about 1,719 degrees.

Questions

How do I use a scientific calculator online?

Pick Degrees or Radians, then tap the keys or type the calculation as you would write it, for example 2 + 3 × 4 or sin(30). The answer appears as you type; press = or Enter to keep it as Ans. Nothing needs to be downloaded.

Why does 50 + 10% give 50.1 and not 55?

On this keypad % simply divides the number in front of it by 100, so 10% is 0.1 and 50 + 0.1 = 50.1. To add 10 percent of 50, type 50 + 50 × 10% (= 55), or 50 × 1.1.

Should I use Degrees or Radians?

Use Degrees for angles measured in °, as in most school geometry (sin 30° = 0.5). Use Radians in calculus and physics formulas, where a full turn is 2π. The setting changes sin, cos, tan and the inverse functions only.

How do I find the antilog on a scientific calculator?

Use the 10ˣ key for the antilog of a common log and eˣ for the antilog of a natural log. For example, the antilog of 1.5 is 10^(1.5) = 31.622776602. On a keyboard you can also type 10^1.5.

How do I enter scientific notation on the calculator?

Type the number, press EXP (or the letter E on a keyboard), then the power of ten: 4.5E−6 means 4.5 × 10^−6. Very large or very small answers are shown the same way, as a number times 10 to a power.

Why do I get an error for tan 90°?

The tangent of a right angle has no value: it grows without limit as the angle nears 90°. The same happens at 270°, at π/2 in radians, and so on. Square roots and logs of negative numbers have no real answer either.

Does it follow BODMAS?

Yes. Brackets come first, then powers and roots, then multiplication and division, then addition and subtraction, so 10 − 2 × 3 = 4. Powers are worked out from right to left, and −2² is −4.

Can this scientific calculator solve quadratic equations?

It works out formulas, so you can type the quadratic formula yourself, such as (−5 + √(5² − 4 × 2 × 3)) ÷ (2 × 2) for 2x² + 5x + 3 = 0, which gives −1. For both roots and the steps at once, the quadratic formula calculator is quicker.

Is this scientific calculator good enough for physics?

For the numbers in school and first-year college physics, yes: it has trig in degrees or radians, powers, roots, logs, scientific notation and about 15 significant digits. It does not handle units, so keep track of metres, seconds and newtons yourself.

How accurate is it?

Each step is worked out with about 15 to 16 significant digits, and the display rounds the answer to 12 characters, so tiny rounding noise such as 0.1 + 0.2 = 0.30000000000000004 is hidden and shows as 0.3. The More digits line shows up to 15 digits.

What do Ans, M+ and RND do?

Ans is the last answer you got with =. M+ adds the answer on the display to the memory and M− takes it away; MR types the memory into your calculation and MC sets it back to 0. RND types a random number between 0 and 1.
