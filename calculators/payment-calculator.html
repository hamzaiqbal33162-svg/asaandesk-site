What the payment calculator works out

A payment calculator tells you the monthly payment on a loan with interest, or how long a payment you choose will take to pay the loan off. It works for any fixed-rate loan repaid in equal monthly installments: a car loan, a home loan or mortgage, a personal loan, a motorcycle loan, a home equity loan or a business loan.

Each payment is split in two. Part pays the interest that built up during the month, and the rest, the principal, reduces what you owe. Because the balance falls every month, the interest part shrinks and the principal part grows, even though the payment stays the same. This is called amortization, and both tabs show it in a full schedule.

How to use the payment calculator

  • Pick the currency at the top; it changes only the symbol, so dollars, rupees or any other currency work.
  • Find the payment: enter the Loan amount (200,000 by default), the Loan term in years (15; part years such as 2.5 are fine) and the Interest rate a year (6%).
  • Find the time: enter the Loan amount, the Monthly payment you can afford (2,000 by default) and the Interest rate.
  • Enter the amount you actually borrow, after any down payment or trade-in. Fees and insurance that are paid separately are not part of it.
  • Read the result at the top, then open the yearly and monthly schedules to see where each payment goes.

The loan payment formula

The monthly payment formula is P = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), where L is the loan amount, r the monthly rate (the yearly rate ÷ 12, as a decimal) and n the number of monthly payments. For the default loan, r = 0.06 ÷ 12 = 0.005 and n = 15 × 12 = 180, so P = 200,000 × 0.005 ÷ (1 − 1.005^−180) = 1,687.71.

To find how long a payment P takes, the formula turns around: n = −ln(1 − L × r ÷ P) ÷ ln(1 + r). With 2,000 a month, n = −ln(1 − 1,000 ÷ 2,000) ÷ ln(1.005) = 138.98 months. The answer is rarely a whole number, so the last payment is smaller.

There is one condition: the payment must be larger than the first month's interest, L × r. On 200,000 at 6% that is 1,000, so a payment of 1,000 or less never reduces the balance and the calculator tells you so. At 0% interest the formula is simply P = L ÷ n: 10,000 over 2 years is 416.67 a month.

How much of each payment is interest

With a fixed payment, the split between interest and principal changes every month. Early on, most of the payment is interest, because the balance is at its highest. As the balance falls, less interest builds up and more of the same payment goes to the principal, so the loan shrinks faster toward the end.

Halfway through the term, after 90 payments, the balance is still 122,074.52, about 61% of the original loan. That is why an extra payment early in a loan saves far more interest than the same extra payment near the end, and why the balance you owe when you sell a home or trade in a car is often higher than people expect.

This applies to amortizing loans with equal payments. Some loans work differently: a home equity line of credit (HELOC) often lets you pay interest only during the draw period and has a variable rate, and some credit products charge a flat fee instead of interest on the falling balance. For those, use the calculator only for the period when you repay with equal monthly payments.

Payment formula in Excel and Google Sheets

Excel and Google Sheets use the same function: =PMT(rate, nper, pv). Keep the units consistent: for monthly payments, divide the yearly rate by 12 and multiply the years by 12. For the default loan, =PMT(6%/12, 15*12, -200000) returns 1,687.71. The minus sign on the loan amount simply makes the payment show as a positive number.

To find the number of payments, use =NPER(rate, pmt, pv): =NPER(6%/12, -2000, 200000) returns 138.98 months. Both functions give principal and interest only, with no taxes, insurance or fees, just like this calculator.

Worked examples: car, home and Pakistan loans

A car loan. Borrowing $35,000 at 7% for 5 years costs $693.04 a month and $6,582.52 of interest. Stretching it to 6 years lowers the payment to $596.72 but raises the interest to $7,963.50; shortening it to 4 years means $838.12 a month and only $5,229.69 of interest.

A 15-year or a 30-year mortgage. On 200,000 at 6%, 15 years costs 1,687.71 a month and 103,788.46 of interest. Thirty years lowers the payment to 1,199.10, but the interest more than doubles to 231,676.38, more than the loan itself. For a full house payment with taxes and insurance, add those on top or use our mortgage calculator.

A car loan in Pakistan. Suppose a bank quotes 16% a year on Rs 3,000,000 for 3 years. The monthly installment is Rs 105,471.10, and you pay Rs 796,959.57 of interest over the 36 months. Ask whether the rate is fixed for the whole loan or can change, and if a loan is quoted at a flat rate, use our IRR calculator to find its true yearly rate.

Paying a little more. On the default loan, raising the payment from 1,687.71 to 2,000 pays it off in 11 years 7 months instead of 15 years and saves 25,836.90 of interest. At 2,200 a month it is gone in about 10 years 2 months, with total interest of 67,367.32.

Interest rate, APR and choosing a term

Enter the interest rate, not the APR, to get the payment. The interest rate is the yearly cost of borrowing the money itself. The annual percentage rate (APR) is a broader measure that also includes points, broker fees and other charges paid to get the loan, so it is usually higher. The APR is the better figure for comparing offers from different lenders; the interest rate is the one that sets your monthly payment.

The term is a trade-off between the size of each payment and the total cost. A longer term lowers the payment and makes a bigger loan fit your budget, but you pay interest for more years, so the total interest rises sharply. A shorter term costs more each month and saves a lot overall. Choose the shortest term whose payment you can keep up comfortably, and keep some savings aside for emergencies.

Fixed and variable rates, and payment shock

This calculator assumes a fixed rate for the whole loan. With a variable or adjustable rate, the rate is an index plus a fixed margin set by the lender, so the rate and the payment move when the index moves, usually within caps written into the contract. Variable rates often start lower but carry the risk that payments rise.

A sudden rise in the monthly payment when a rate resets is often called payment shock. To test it, run the calculator twice. On the default loan, after 5 years the balance is 152,018.20 (see the yearly schedule). If the rate then reset from 6% to 8% for the remaining 10 years, enter 152,018.20 over 10 years at 8%: the payment would rise from 1,687.71 to 1,844.40, about 157 more each month.

Tips and related tools

  • Round your payment up. Even a small extra amount each month goes straight to the principal and shortens the loan.
  • Check whether your loan has a prepayment penalty before paying extra.
  • Compare loans on total interest and APR, not only the monthly payment.
  • Remember the costs this payment leaves out: insurance, taxes, maintenance and fees.

How it works

Choose a tab. Find the payment takes the loan amount, the term in years and the yearly rate, and gives the one monthly payment that clears the loan exactly at the end of the term. Find the time works the other way: enter the payment you can afford and it shows how many months it takes to pay the loan off. The payment must be more than one month's interest, or the balance would never go down. Both tabs list every payment, split into interest and principal.

The formula

  • Monthly rate r = yearly rate ÷ 100 ÷ 12; months n = years × 12
  • Monthly payment P = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n); at 0% interest P = L ÷ n
  • Months to pay off with payment P: n = −ln(1 − L × r ÷ P) ÷ ln(1 + r); at 0% n = L ÷ P
  • The payoff only works when P is more than L × r, the first month's interest
  • When n is not a whole number, the last payment is smaller and only clears what is left

A worked example

Borrow 200,000 at 6% a year. Over 15 years, r = 0.005 and n = 180, so P = 200,000 × 0.005 ÷ (1 − 1.005^−180) = 1,687.71 a month, 303,788.46 in all. Paying 2,000 a month instead: n = −ln(1 − 200,000 × 0.005 ÷ 2,000) ÷ ln(1.005) = 138.98 months, about 11 years 7 months, with a smaller last payment of 1,951.56.

Questions

How do I calculate payments on a loan?

Use P = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), with L the loan amount, r the monthly rate (yearly rate ÷ 12) and n the number of months. 200,000 at 6% for 15 years gives 1,687.71 a month. Enter your figures in the Find the payment tab to get it instantly.

How do I calculate the payment on a car loan?

Enter the amount you borrow after the down payment and trade-in, the rate and the term in years. $35,000 at 7% for 5 years is $693.04 a month. Sales tax, registration and insurance are extra unless you add them to the loan.

How long will it take to pay off my loan?

Use the Find the time tab: enter the balance, the rate and the monthly payment you make. 200,000 at 6% paid at 2,000 a month takes 139 payments, about 11 years 7 months, with a smaller final payment of 1,951.56. Raise the payment to see how much sooner you would finish and how much interest you would save.

What is the payment formula in Excel?

=PMT(rate/12, years*12, -loan) gives the monthly payment, for example =PMT(6%/12, 180, -200000) = 1,687.71. Google Sheets uses the same PMT function, and =NPER(rate/12, -payment, loan) gives the number of months.

How do I calculate a loan payment in Pakistan?

The formula is the same; choose PKR as the currency and enter the rate your bank quotes. Rs 3,000,000 at 16% for 3 years is Rs 105,471.10 a month. If the bank quotes a flat rate, the true yearly rate is much higher; check it with an IRR calculation.

Why does my payment have to be more than the interest?

Each month the loan grows by its interest, balance × monthly rate. If the payment is not larger than that, nothing is left over to lower the balance, so the loan never ends. The calculator tells you the smallest payment that works.

Why is the last payment smaller?

A payment you choose rarely divides the loan into whole months. The calculator keeps your payment the same every month and makes the final one just big enough to clear what is left, so you never overpay.

Should I enter the interest rate or the APR?

Enter the interest rate to get the payment, because the APR also includes fees and points that are not charged as monthly interest. Use the APR to compare offers between lenders.

What is payment shock?

It is a sharp rise in the monthly payment, usually when an adjustable rate resets higher or a low introductory rate ends. On a 152,018 balance with 10 years left, a reset from 6% to 8% lifts the payment from about 1,688 to 1,844.

Does paying a little more each month save much?

Usually yes. Every extra amount lowers the balance straight away, so less interest builds up in all the months that follow. Try a higher payment on the Find the time tab and compare the total interest.

Does it work for car, personal and home loans?

Yes, for any loan with a fixed rate and equal monthly payments. Enter the amount you borrow after any down payment. Fees, insurance or a changing rate are not included.
