What this basic calculator does

A basic calculator handles the four operations of everyday arithmetic: addition, subtraction, multiplication and division. This one runs free in your browser with nothing to download, has big keys for a phone, and also takes typing from a computer keyboard.

Unlike many cheap pocket calculators, it follows the order of operations used in school maths, spreadsheets and phones, so a whole line such as 2 + 3 × 4 gives the right answer, 14. It also keeps your last answer (Ans), a memory for running totals and a history of recent sums.

How to use the calculator, step by step

  • Type the calculation with the keys 0 to 9, the point and + − × ÷. On a keyboard, * multiplies and / divides.
  • Watch the live answer under the line as you type. Press = or Enter to keep it; it becomes Ans.
  • To carry on from that answer, press + − × or ÷ straight away. Pressing a digit instead starts a new calculation.
  • ± changes the sign of the number you are typing, so 5 ± gives −5.
  • EXP types a power of ten: 2.5 EXP 4 means 2.5 × 10⁴ = 25,000.
  • ⌫ (or Backspace) removes the last key; AC (or Esc) clears everything.
  • Brackets can be typed from the keyboard, for example (2 + 3) × 4 = 20. A missing closing bracket is added at the end for you.
  • RND types a random number from 0 up to (but not including) 1.

Order of operations (BODMAS and PEMDAS) on a basic calculator

BODMAS (Brackets, Orders, Division and Multiplication, Addition and Subtraction) and PEMDAS are two names for the same rule. Brackets come first, then × and ÷ from left to right, then + and − from left to right. Multiplication does not beat division; whichever comes first in the line is done first.

Everyday examples: shopping, bills and percentages

Running total with memory. At a shop you buy 3 kg of rice at PKR 320 per kg and 2 dozen eggs at PKR 360 per dozen (example prices). Type 3 × 320 = and press M+ (960 is stored), then 2 × 360 = and M+ again. MR now gives 1,680, the bill. M− would take an item off if you return it.

Splitting a bill. A dinner costs PKR 7,850 for 5 people: 7,850 ÷ 5 = 1,570 each.

Percentages without a % key. To find 15% of 2,400, multiply by 0.15: 2,400 × 0.15 = 360. The price after a 15% discount is 2,400 × 0.85 = 2,040, and adding 18% to 2,400 is 2,400 × 1.18 = 2,832. If you type % from the keyboard it means divide by 100, so 50 + 10% gives 50.1, not 55.

Very large or small numbers. EXP keeps zeros out of the way: typing 6.022 EXP 23 × 2 and pressing = shows 1.2044 × 10^24.

Common mistakes and limits

  • Typing 25,000 with a comma: the comma is read as a point on the keyboard, so type 25000.
  • Expecting left-to-right answers: use brackets when you want the addition done first.
  • Dividing by zero: there is no answer, so you get an error; clear it with AC or ⌫.
  • Rounding: 1 ÷ 3 shows 0.3333333333 because the display is 12 characters wide. 0.1 + 0.2 shows 0.3, without the tiny binary error some computer programs print.
  • Numbers longer than about 15 digits lose their last digits in any ordinary calculator; for exact long numbers use the Big Number Calculator.

How it works

Tap the keys or type with your keyboard (digits, + - * /, Enter for =, Backspace and Esc). The answer is worked out as you type and kept when you press =; it then becomes Ans, and pressing + − × or ÷ next carries on from it. Multiplication and division are done before addition and subtraction, so 2 + 3 × 4 is 14. ± changes the sign of the number you are typing, EXP types a power of ten (3 EXP 6 = 3,000,000), and the memory keys keep a number for later.

The formula

  • Order: × and ÷ first (left to right), then + and − (left to right)
  • EXP: a E n = a × 10^n
  • M+ adds the answer to the memory, M− takes it away, MR recalls it, MC clears it
  • The display shows 12 characters: 11 minus the number of whole-number digits are kept after the point

A worked example

12.5 − 20 = −7.5. 1 ÷ 3 shows 0.3333333333 (ten 3s fill the display). 2 + 3 × 4: first 3 × 4 = 12, then 2 + 12 = 14.

Questions

Is this basic calculator online free to use?

Yes. It runs in your browser on a phone or computer, with no download and no sign-up. Your answers, memory and history stay on the page while you use it.

Why is 2 + 3 × 4 equal to 14 and not 20?

Multiplication and division come before addition and subtraction, so 3 × 4 = 12 is worked out first and then 2 is added. A cheap pocket calculator that works strictly left to right would show 20; this one follows the rule used in maths, spreadsheets and phones.

How do I calculate a percentage on a basic calculator?

Multiply by the percent written as a decimal: 12% of 850 is 850 × 0.12 = 102. To add or remove a percent, multiply by 1.12 or by 0.88. For percent change and reverse percentages with the working, use the Percentage Calculator.

How do I keep a running total with the memory keys?

Work out each line and press M+ to add it to the memory, or M− to take it away. MR types the total into your next calculation, and MC sets the memory back to 0. A small M on the display shows that something is stored.

Can I type with my keyboard?

Yes. Use the digits, the point, + - * / and brackets, Enter or = for the answer, Backspace to remove the last character and Esc to clear. You can also click into the display and edit the calculation in the middle.

What does EXP mean on a calculator?

EXP means times ten to the power of the number you type next. 3 EXP 6 is 3 × 10⁶ = 3,000,000, and 5 EXP −3 (press ± after the 3) is 0.005. It saves typing long rows of zeros.

Why does 6 ÷ 2 × 3 give 9?

Division and multiplication have equal rank, so they are done from left to right: 6 ÷ 2 = 3, then 3 × 3 = 9. If you mean 6 ÷ (2 × 3), type the brackets and you get 1.

What happens when I divide by zero?

You get an error that says you cannot divide by zero, because no number times 0 gives anything other than 0. Clear it with AC or Backspace and carry on.

What do Ans and the memory keys do?

Ans is the last answer you got with =. M+ adds the answer on the display to the memory, M− takes it away, MR types the memory into your calculation and MC sets it back to 0. A small M on the display shows that something is stored.

Can I use a basic calculator in the SAT or other exams?

The SAT allows four-function, scientific and graphing calculators without CAS features in its Math section, and the digital test also has a built-in calculator; none is allowed in Reading and Writing. Other boards and universities set their own lists, so check the rules for your exam before the day.
