Cash back or low interest: which car deal is better?

Car makers often run two incentives at once and let you pick one: a cash back rebate that lowers the price, or a low promotional interest rate, sometimes 0%, on the loan. The cash back or low interest calculator works out both deals in full and tells you which one costs less over the whole loan.

The answer is not obvious, because the two offers save money in different ways. Cash back cuts the amount you borrow on day one, but you pay the normal rate on the rest. The low rate leaves the full price in the loan but charges far less interest on it. Which one wins depends on the size of the rebate, the gap between the two rates, the loan length and how much you put down.

How to compare the two offers, step by step

  • Cash back offer: the rebate you get if you take the normal rate.
  • Loan rate with the cash back: the rate you would pay with the rebate, from the dealer or from your own bank or credit union if you finance elsewhere.
  • Low rate without cash back: the promotional rate, 0% or more.
  • Car price: the agreed price before the rebate.
  • Loan length (1 to 120 months) and Down payment.
  • Trade-in, tax and fees (optional): Trade-in value, State, Sales tax rate (your state plus local rate), Title, registration and other fees, and whether to add the tax and fees to the loan.
  • Amounts are in US dollars, because the sales tax rules are those of US states; the comparison works the same way for any car loan if you leave the state as Not chosen.

Choosing a state sets two rules, whether a trade-in lowers the taxed price and whether the cash back is taxed, but not the rate, so always type your own sales tax rate.

A worked example: $3,000 cash back or 2.9%

You agree a price of $42,000, put $6,000 down, and want a 60-month loan. Sales tax is 6.5% and fees are $1,200, both paid up front. The dealer offers $3,000 cash back with financing at 7.2%, or 2.9% with no cash back. With no state chosen, the rebate is still taxed, so the sales tax is $2,730.00 either way.

When the cash back wins and when the low rate wins

The low rate saves interest every month the loan runs, so its advantage grows with the loan length and the amount borrowed. Cash back is a fixed amount, so it wins on short loans, big down payments and small rate gaps. The table keeps the example above and changes only the loan length.

A bigger down payment pushes towards the cash back. On the 60-month loan, putting $15,000 down makes the cash back better by $387.54, and $25,000 down makes it better by $1,570.39.

Your own bank can change the answer too. You can usually take the dealer's cash back and finance it elsewhere, so enter your bank's rate as the loan rate with the cash back. If your credit union offers 5.5% instead of 7.2%, the cash back wins the 60-month example by $896.16.

Even 0% can lose. Against $4,000 cash back at 6.5%, a 0% offer saves $1,567.00 over 60 months, but over 36 months the cash back is better by $692.35.

Sales tax, trade-ins and state rules

States tax car purchases in different ways, and two rules can change the comparison. First, in most states a trade-in lowers the price that is taxed; the calculator taxes the full price, trade-in or not, in California, Hawaii, Kentucky, Maryland, Michigan, Montana, Virginia and Washington, DC.

Second, in most states the tax is charged on the price before a maker's cash rebate, so taking the cash back does not cut the tax. The calculator lets the rebate lower the taxed price in 21 states: in the South, Texas, Oklahoma, Louisiana, Kentucky and Delaware; in the West, Arizona, Utah, Oregon, Montana, Wyoming and Alaska; in the Midwest, Minnesota, Iowa, Missouri, Kansas and Nebraska; and in the Northeast, Pennsylvania, Massachusetts, Vermont, New Hampshire and Rhode Island. There the cash back offer also saves sales tax, which helps it.

For example, with an $8,000 trade-in, $2,000 down and the deal above at 6.5% tax, the low rate wins by $203.88 when the rebate is taxed, as in Florida or New York. In Texas, where the rebate is not taxed, the tax is $2,015.00 with the cash back instead of $2,210.00, and the low rate wins by only $8.88. These are the rules the calculator applies as researched in November 2026; check your state's revenue department before a large purchase.

Cash back or low interest on credit cards and bank accounts

The same question comes up with credit cards: a card that pays cash back on purchases, or one with a lower interest rate? The rule of thumb follows from how card interest works. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains that if you pay the full balance by the due date you keep the grace period and avoid interest on new purchases; if you carry a balance, you pay interest on it and new purchases start earning interest straight away.

So if you always pay in full, the interest rate hardly matters and cash back is free money. If you carry a balance, the rate matters more. Paying off a $3,000 balance over 12 months costs $404.15 of interest at 24% but $249.30 at 15%, according to our Credit Card Calculator: a $154.85 difference, which a 1.5% cash back card would only match on about $10,323 of yearly spending.

Is cash back interest? A dealer's or card's cash back is a discount on what you buy, not interest. A cash bonus from a bank for opening or keeping a deposit account is different: the IRS tells banks to report amounts of $10 or more in box 1 of Form 1099-INT whether or not they are called interest, so such a bonus can be taxed like interest.

Tips before you choose, and related calculators

  • Negotiate the price first, then talk about incentives. A rebate or low rate is worth little if the price has been raised to pay for it.
  • Get preapproved by a bank or credit union before you visit the dealer. The CFPB notes that a dealer's rate can include a markup on top of the lender's buy rate, and that going straight to a bank or credit union tends to be cheaper.
  • Read the fine print. The CFPB warns that rebates and special financing may not be available to everyone; low rates often need excellent credit or a shorter term.
  • Do not stretch the loan just to make the low rate win: a longer term raises the total interest even when the payment falls.

How it works

Dealers often let you choose between cash back off the price and a special low loan rate, but not both. Type the cash back, the normal rate you would pay with it, the low rate, the car price, the loan length and your down payment; trade-in, state, sales tax and fees are in the section below them. The calculator works out each loan in full: the taxed price, the loan amount, what you pay up front, the monthly payment and the interest. The better offer is the one with the lower total cost.

The formula

  • Taxed price = price − trade-in (if your state gives trade-in credit) − cash back (if your state does not tax rebates); sales tax = taxed price × rate
  • Loan = price − cash back − down payment − trade-in (+ tax and fees when added to the loan)
  • Monthly payment M = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), with r = rate ÷ 100 ÷ 12 and n months (L ÷ n at 0%)
  • Interest = M × n − L
  • Total cost = price − cash back + sales tax + fees + interest; the lower total wins

A worked example

A $42,000 car, $6,000 down, 60 months, 6.5% sales tax and $1,200 of fees paid up front. With $3,000 cash back at 7.2%, the loan is $33,000, the payment $656.56 and the interest $6,393.48, a total cost of $49,323.48. At 2.9% without the cash back, the loan is $36,000, the payment $645.27 and the interest $2,716.46, a total of $48,646.46. The low rate saves $3,677.02 of interest, more than the $3,000 cash back, so it is $677.02 cheaper overall.

Questions

Is cash back or low interest better on a car?

It depends on the numbers, which is why the calculator works out both loans. The low rate wins when the interest it saves is more than the cash back, which is more likely on long loans with a big rate gap; the cash back wins on short loans, with large down payments or when you can borrow cheaply elsewhere.

When is the cash back the better deal?

When the cash back is worth more than the interest the low rate saves. That is more likely with a big rebate, a short loan, a large down payment, or a low rate that is only a little below the normal one.

Is 0% financing better than cash back?

Not always. A 0% rate removes the interest, but a large enough rebate can still beat it, especially on a short loan. In our example, 0% beat $4,000 cash back at 6.5% over 60 months by $1,567.00, but lost by $692.35 over 36 months.

Why does my state matter?

States tax car sales differently. In some, a trade-in lowers the price that is taxed and in others it does not; some tax the price before the cash back and some after it. Those rules change the tax, so pick your state in the trade-in, tax and fees section.

Should I add the tax and fees to the loan?

It means less cash up front, but you pay interest on them. Both offers are compared the same way, so the choice seldom changes which offer wins.

What if I could get a better rate from my bank?

Then enter that rate as the loan rate with the cash back: you can usually take the dealer's cash back and finance elsewhere. A cheaper outside loan makes the cash back offer much more likely to win.

Cash back vs low interest credit card: which should I pick?

If you pay your card in full every month, a cash back card is usually better, because you pay no interest on purchases during the grace period. If you carry a balance, a lower rate usually saves more than the cash back earns.

Is cash back counted as interest?

A rebate on a car or a purchase is a price reduction, not interest. A cash bonus paid by a bank on a deposit account is treated differently: banks report amounts of $10 or more on Form 1099-INT whether or not they are called interest.

Does the calculator include the trade-in and down payment?

Yes. Both lower the loan in each offer by the same amount, and the trade-in can also lower the sales tax if your state allows it. A bigger down payment makes the cash back relatively more attractive, because there is less loan for the low rate to save interest on.
