How it works

Dealers often let you choose between cash back off the price and a special low loan rate, but not both. Type the cash back, the normal rate you would pay with it, the low rate, the car price, the loan length and your down payment; trade-in, state, sales tax and fees are in the section below them. The calculator works out each loan in full: the taxed price, the loan amount, what you pay up front, the monthly payment and the interest. The better offer is the one with the lower total cost.

The formula

  • Taxed price = price − trade-in (if your state gives trade-in credit) − cash back (if your state does not tax rebates); sales tax = taxed price × rate
  • Loan = price − cash back − down payment − trade-in (+ tax and fees when added to the loan)
  • Monthly payment M = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), with r = rate ÷ 100 ÷ 12 and n months (L ÷ n at 0%)
  • Interest = M × n − L
  • Total cost = price − cash back + sales tax + fees + interest; the lower total wins

A worked example

A $50,000 car, $10,000 down, 60 months, 7% tax and $2,000 of fees. With $1,000 cash back at 5%, the loan is $39,000, the payment $735.98 and the interest $5,158.69, a total cost of $59,658.69. At 2% without cash back, the loan is $40,000, the payment $701.11 and the interest $2,066.62, a total of $57,566.62. The low rate saves $3,092 of interest, more than the $1,000 cash back, so it is about $2,092 cheaper overall.

Questions

When is the cash back the better deal?

When the cash back is worth more than the interest the low rate saves. That is more likely with a big rebate, a short loan, a large down payment, or a low rate that is only a little below the normal one.

Why does my state matter?

States tax car sales differently. In some, a trade-in lowers the price that is taxed and in others it does not; some tax the price before the cash back and some after it. Those rules change the tax, so pick your state in the trade-in, tax and fees section.

Should I add the tax and fees to the loan?

It means less cash up front, but you pay interest on them. Both offers are compared the same way, so the choice seldom changes which offer wins.

What if I could get a better rate from my bank?

Then enter that rate as the loan rate with the cash back: you can usually take the dealer's cash back and finance elsewhere.

Is a 0% offer always best?

No. A 0% rate removes the interest, but a large enough cash back can still beat it. The calculator handles 0% offers.
