What the resistor calculator does

This resistor calculator decodes resistor colour codes with 3, 4, 5 or 6 bands and shows the value, its tolerance range, the printed letter code and whether it is a standard value. A drawing shows the bands in their real colours, so you can hold the part next to the screen and compare.

Four more tabs cover the other jobs around a resistor: Value to colours finds the bands for any value and the nearest standard values, Series and Parallel add up groups of resistors, and Wire resistance works out the resistance of a length of round wire from its diameter and metal.

How to read a resistor colour code

  • Turn the resistor so the bands bunched near one end are on the left. The tolerance band, usually gold or silver on common parts, then sits on the right, often with a small gap before it.
  • Count the bands and pick 3, 4, 5 or 6 bands in the calculator.
  • Read the digit bands from the left: two on 3 and 4 band resistors, three on 5 and 6 band resistors.
  • The next band is the multiplier: how many zeros to add, or for gold, silver and pink, divide by 10, 100 or 1,000.
  • Then comes the tolerance band (none on a 3 band part, which means ±20%), and on a 6 band part a last band for the temperature coefficient.
  • Pick each colour from its menu. The value appears with a band-by-band table and the working.

A 5 band brown, black, black, brown, brown resistor reads 100 × 10 = 1 kΩ at ±1% (F), between 990 Ω and 1.01 kΩ, with the printed code 1K00. A 6 band red, red, black, black, brown, red resistor is 220 Ω ±1% with 50 ppm/K (R): its value can drift by up to 0.011 Ω for each °C of temperature change.

Finding the colour bands for a value

On the Value to colours tab, type the resistance with its unit (Ω, kΩ or MΩ), pick the number of bands and the tolerance, and the calculator gives the bands. For 5 kΩ with four bands at ±5% it shows green, black, red, gold.

It also checks whether the value is one that is actually made. Resistors come in fixed preferred values per decade, the E-series of IEC 60063: E12 has 12 values (1.0, 1.2, 1.5, 1.8, 2.2, 2.7, 3.3, 3.9, 4.7, 5.6, 6.8, 8.2), E24 has 24 for ±5% parts and E96 has 96 for ±1% parts. 5 kΩ is not in any of them, so the calculator suggests the nearest: 4.7 kΩ in E12, 5.1 kΩ in E24 and 4.99 kΩ in E96.

Resistors in series and in parallel

In series, one after another, the resistances simply add: R = R₁ + R₂ + R₃. On the Series tab, 1,000, 2,200 and 4,700 Ω give 7,900 Ω, or 7.9 kΩ. The total is always larger than the largest resistor.

In parallel, side by side, the conductances (1 ÷ R) add: 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃. On the Parallel tab, 330, 470 and 1,000 Ω give a conductance of 0.006158 S and a total of 162.39 Ω, which is always below the smallest resistor. Two equal resistors in parallel give half of one, so two 1 kΩ parts make 500 Ω.

Type the values separated by commas or spaces and choose whether they are in Ω, kΩ or MΩ. For a mixed network, work out each parallel group first, then add the groups that are in series.

How to calculate a resistor for an LED or a voltage divider

An LED needs a series resistor to limit its current. The formula is R = (supply voltage − LED forward voltage) ÷ LED current, with the forward voltage and current taken from the LED's data sheet. For a 5 V supply and an LED rated at 2 V and 20 mA, the resistor drops 3 V, and the Ohm's law calculator gives 3 V ÷ 0.02 A = 150 Ω and a power of 0.06 W. 150 Ω is an exact E12 value, and the Value to colours tab shows its bands as brown, green, brown, gold.

Check the power too: P = I² × R or V × I. Small discrete resistors are typically rated 1/10, 1/8 or 1/4 W, so 0.06 W is well within a 1/4 W part. When the result is not a standard value, choose the next higher one, which keeps the current a little below the maximum.

A voltage divider uses two resistors in series to give a lower voltage: Vout = Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2), where R2 is the resistor between the output and ground. Two equal resistors give half the input voltage. A load connected to the output acts in parallel with R2, so keep it much larger than R2 or include it in the sum.

SMD resistor codes and other markings

Surface-mount (SMD) resistors are too small for colour bands and carry a printed code instead. In the 3-digit code the first two digits are the value and the third is the number of zeros, so 472 is 47 × 10² = 4.7 kΩ. Precision parts use a 4-digit code with three digits and a multiplier, so 1002 is 100 × 10² = 10.0 kΩ. An R marks the decimal point for small values, so 4R7 is 4.7 Ω.

1% parts often use the EIA-96 code: two digits pick one of the 96 E96 values and a letter gives the multiplier, so 01C means 10 kΩ. The calculator's printed-code line uses the related IEC 60062 letter code, where R, K and M stand for ohms, kilo-ohms and mega-ohms and mark the decimal point: 4K7 is 4.7 kΩ and 1M0 is 1 MΩ. A resistor with a single black band is a zero-ohm jumper.

Wire resistance

The Wire resistance tab uses R = L ÷ (σ × A): length divided by conductivity times cross-section area. For 25 m of annealed copper wire 1.5 mm across, the area is π × 0.75² = 1.76715 mm², and with copper's 58,000,000 S/m the wire has 0.2439 Ω, which is 9.76 Ω per km. The closest American wire gauge is 15 AWG.

Conductivity falls as metal warms. The calculator notes that copper loses about 0.4% per °C, so the same wire has about 16% more resistance at 60 °C than at 20 °C. For long cable runs, the voltage drop calculator turns this resistance into the volts lost on the way.

Tips and common mistakes

  • Read in good light. Brown, red and orange, and violet and grey, are easy to mix up; if a reading gives an odd value, try the nearest colours and see which gives a standard value.
  • If you are unsure which end is which, read both ways. Usually only one direction gives a value in the E-series list.
  • Measure a resistor that is out of the circuit. In a board, other parts in parallel make a multimeter read low.
  • A resistor that has overheated may no longer match its bands, so measure any part that looks scorched.
  • Tolerance is not a fault: a ±5% 330 Ω resistor that reads 320 Ω is within its range of 313.5 to 346.5 Ω.

How it works

Resistors are too small to print a number on, so their value is painted as coloured rings. Hold the resistor with the tolerance ring (often gold or silver, a little apart from the rest) on the right and read from the left: the first two rings (three on 5 and 6 band parts) are digits, the next one says how many zeros to add (or, for gold, silver and pink, to divide by 10, 100 or 1,000), then comes the tolerance, and on 6 band parts a last ring for how much the value drifts with temperature. Pick the colours and the value appears with its tolerance range, its printed letter code and whether it is a standard value. The other tabs go the other way (value to colours), add resistors in series or in parallel, and work out the resistance of a length of wire from its size and metal.

The formula

  • Value = (digits as one number) × multiplier; 3 and 4 band resistors have 2 digits, 5 and 6 band resistors have 3
  • Digit colours: black 0, brown 1, red 2, orange 3, yellow 4, green 5, blue 6, violet 7, grey 8, white 9; multiplier = 10 to the digit, gold × 0.1, silver × 0.01, pink × 0.001
  • Tolerance: brown ±1% (F), red ±2% (G), orange ±0.05% (W), yellow ±0.02% (P), green ±0.5% (D), blue ±0.25% (C), violet ±0.1% (B), grey ±0.01% (L), gold ±5% (J), silver ±10% (K), no band ±20% (M)
  • Temperature coefficient: black 250 (U), brown 100 (S), red 50 (R), orange 15 (P), yellow 25 (Q), green 20 (Z), blue 10 (Z), violet 5 (M), grey 1 (K) parts per million per kelvin
  • Series: R = R₁ + R₂ + … ; parallel: 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …
  • Wire: R = L ÷ (σ × A), with A = π × (d ÷ 2)² and σ the conductivity of the metal

A worked example

Yellow, violet, red, gold: the digits are 4 and 7, red multiplies by 100, so the value is 47 × 100 = 4,700 Ω = 4.7 kΩ. Gold means ±5%, so a good part measures between 4.465 kΩ and 4.935 kΩ. Its printed code is 4K7, and 4.7 kΩ is a value in every standard series from E3 to E24.

Questions

How do I calculate a resistor value from the colour code?

Write down the digit bands as one number, then multiply by the multiplier band. Orange, orange, brown, gold is 33 × 10 = 330 Ω, and gold means ±5%. Pick the colours here and the calculator does it with every step shown.

Which end do I start reading from?

Start at the end where the rings are bunched together. The tolerance ring, usually gold or silver on common parts, sits on the right and is often a little wider or set apart. If both ends look the same, try both readings: only one usually gives a standard value.

How do I calculate resistors in parallel?

Add the reciprocals and take the reciprocal of the sum: 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …. For 330, 470 and 1,000 Ω that gives 162.39 Ω. For two resistors there is a shortcut: R = (R₁ × R₂) ÷ (R₁ + R₂).

How do I calculate resistors in series?

Add them up: R = R₁ + R₂ + …. 1,000 + 2,200 + 4,700 Ω is 7,900 Ω. Use the Series tab to add a long list at once.

How do I calculate the resistor needed for an LED?

Take the LED's forward voltage away from the supply voltage and divide by the LED current. A 2 V, 20 mA LED on 5 V needs (5 − 2) ÷ 0.02 = 150 Ω, using 0.06 W, so a 1/4 W 150 Ω resistor (brown, green, brown, gold) is fine.

How do I read SMD resistor codes?

On a 3-digit code the last digit is the number of zeros: 472 is 4,700 Ω. On a 4-digit code it is the same with three digits first: 1002 is 10,000 Ω. R is the decimal point, so 4R7 is 4.7 Ω.

What do the letters such as J or F mean?

They are the IEC 60062 letter codes printed on parts and in parts lists: F is ±1%, G ±2%, J ±5%, K ±10% and M ±20%. The temperature letters work the same way, for example R means 50 ppm per kelvin.

What is resistor tolerance?

It is how far the real value may be from the marked value. A 330 Ω resistor at ±5% can measure anywhere from 313.5 to 346.5 Ω. Precision circuits use ±1% or tighter parts, shown by a brown or other tolerance band.

What is a standard (E-series) value?

Resistors are made in a fixed set of values per decade, chosen so that neighbouring values overlap within their tolerance. E12 has 12 values (1.0, 1.2, 1.5 … 8.2), E24 has 24 for ±5% parts, and E96 has 96 for ±1% parts. If the value you need is not in the list, the calculator shows the nearest ones.

Why does a 6th band sometimes mean something else?

On most 6 band resistors the last ring is the temperature coefficient, as here. Some military parts use a 5th or 6th ring for reliability (failure rate) instead, so check the maker's data sheet for those.

Does the wire resistance change with temperature?

Yes. Metals conduct less well when warm: copper and aluminium gain about 0.4% resistance per °C. The conductivity presets are for 20 °C; type your own conductivity for another temperature or metal.

What is a resistor used for?

A resistor is a passive two-terminal part that resists the flow of current. It is used to limit current (as for an LED), divide a voltage, set the gain or timing of a circuit, and pull signal lines up or down to a known level.
