What is body surface area (BSA)?

Body surface area, or BSA, is the total area of the skin that covers the body, measured in square metres (m²). This body surface area calculator estimates it from weight and height with eight published formulas, shows the Mosteller result first, and gives every answer in m², square feet and square inches.

BSA matters in medicine because many body processes scale better with surface area than with weight alone. It is used to set the dose of some medicines, most famously cancer chemotherapy, which is often prescribed in milligrams per square metre (mg/m²), and to compare measurements such as kidney function between people of different sizes.

Measuring skin area directly is slow and awkward, so the formulas were built from careful measurements of real people and then fitted to weight and height. Treat the answer as an estimate: for a medicine dose, your care team decides which formula and which weight to use.

How to use this BSA calculator

  • Choose Metric or US units at the top. Metric asks for kg and cm; US units ask for lb and feet and inches.

The eight BSA formulas compared

All eight formulas turn weight and height into an area, but they were built from different groups of people over almost a century. That is why they agree within a few per cent for an average adult and spread further apart at the extremes.

How BSA is used for medicine doses

When a medicine is prescribed per square metre, the dose is the amount per m² times the BSA. For example, if a protocol called for 100 mg per m², the woman above (1.66 m²) would get about 166 mg and the man (2.18 m²) about 218 mg. The real figure always comes from the prescriber and the pharmacy, who may round it, cap it or adjust it for kidney or liver function.

Assumptions about average size matter too. A 2010 study of 3,613 adult cancer patients in UK hospitals found an average BSA of 1.91 m² in men, 1.71 m² in women and 1.79 m² overall, higher than the 1.7 m² that drug cost appraisals had assumed for everyone.

Kidney function is another everyday use. Laboratory reports give the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in mL/min per 1.73 m², which scales every result to the same reference body size so that a large and a small person can be compared on one scale.

Total BSA versus percentage of body surface

People also search for a body surface area calculator for burns, psoriasis or eczema. Those use a different idea: the share of the skin that is affected, given as a percentage of total body surface area (% TBSA), not an area in m².

For burns, doctors estimate % TBSA in adults with the rule of nines, which gives each part of the body a set share, and in children with the Lund-Browder chart, because a child's head is larger and legs are smaller in proportion. For small or scattered patches, the size of the person's whole opened hand is about 1% of TBSA (MSD Manual, 2024). Psoriasis is also graded as mild, moderate or severe partly by the share of BSA it covers.

This page gives the total area. If you know a percentage, you can turn it into an area: 1% of the woman's 1.66 m² is about 0.017 m², or roughly 166 cm².

Tips and common mistakes

  • Check the units. Typing pounds into a kg box overstates the weight by more than double, and BSA by about half as much again.
  • Use a current, measured weight and height. Height enters every formula: under Mosteller, a 10 cm error at 160 cm changes the result by about 3%.
  • For babies and children, prefer Haycock, Boyd or Mosteller, and type the weight in grams if that is how it was recorded.
  • Do not mix formulas when tracking a person over time; compare like with like.
  • Never change a medicine dose from this page. Use it to understand or double-check the number on a prescription and ask the pharmacist about any difference.

How it works

Body surface area is the area of the outside of the body. Doctors use it to dose some medicines, chemotherapy in particular, and to compare kidney and heart measurements between people of different sizes. Nobody measures it directly in practice, so it is worked out from weight and height with one of several published formulas. They agree closely for most adults and drift apart for babies and for very large people. Mosteller is shown first because it is the simplest and the one most used in hospitals.

The formula

  • Du Bois (1916): 0.007184 × W^0.425 × H^0.725
  • Mosteller (1987): √(W × H ÷ 3600)
  • Haycock (1978): 0.024265 × W^0.5378 × H^0.3964
  • Gehan and George (1970): 0.0235 × W^0.51456 × H^0.42246
  • Boyd (1935): 0.0003207 × H^0.3 × G^(0.7285 − 0.0188 × log10 G), with G = weight in grams
  • Fujimoto (1968): 0.008883 × W^0.444 × H^0.663; Takahira (1925): 0.007241 × W^0.425 × H^0.725
  • Schlich (2010): women 0.000975482 × W^0.46 × H^1.08; men 0.000579479 × W^0.38 × H^1.24
  • W = weight in kg, H = height in cm, result in m²; 1 m² = 10.764 sq ft = 1,550 sq in

A worked example

A woman of 62 kg and 160 cm: Mosteller gives √(62 × 160 ÷ 3600) = √2.756 ≈ 1.66 m², about 17.87 sq ft. Du Bois gives 0.007184 × 62^0.425 × 160^0.725 ≈ 1.64 m², and the eight formulas together range from 1.56 to 1.69 m².

Questions

How do you calculate body surface area with height and weight?

The quickest way is the Mosteller formula: multiply weight in kg by height in cm, divide by 3,600 and take the square root. For 62 kg and 160 cm that is √(9,920 ÷ 3,600) ≈ 1.66 m². The calculator does this and seven other formulas at once.

What is body surface area measured in?

Usually square metres (m²). One square metre is about 10.764 square feet or 1,550 square inches, and the calculator shows all three. Medicine doses based on BSA are written per m², for example mg/m².

What is a normal body surface area?

There is no single normal value because BSA follows body size. In a 2010 study of 3,613 UK adults with cancer, the average was 1.91 m² for men and 1.71 m² for women. Kidney results are scaled to 1.73 m² as a reference size, and a newborn-sized baby of 4.2 kg and 54 cm comes out at about 0.25 m² here.

Which formula should I use?

Use the one your doctor, hospital or protocol names. Mosteller is the most common for medicine doses, Du Bois is the oldest and still used for adults, and Haycock or Boyd are often preferred for babies and children.

How do you calculate body surface area for a child?

Enter the child's weight (in grams if you like) and height, and read Haycock, Boyd or Mosteller in the table, which were built or checked with children included. A child of 25 kg and 128 cm gets about 0.94 m² from all three. Ignore Schlich for children; it was fitted to adults and reads far too low.

How is body surface area used for chemotherapy?

Many cancer medicines are prescribed per square metre, so the dose is the amount per m² times the person's BSA. The oncology team works it out with a set formula and may round or cap it. Use this page only to understand the number, never to change a dose.

Is body surface area the same as the percentage used for burns or psoriasis?

No. Burns, psoriasis and eczema are described by the share of the skin affected, as a percentage of total body surface area, estimated with the rule of nines, a child's chart or the hand method (a whole opened hand is about 1%). This calculator gives the total area in m².

Why does Fujimoto give a lower result?

It was fitted to Japanese people in the 1960s, and for the same weight and height it gives a few percent less than Du Bois or Mosteller. This page uses its coefficient 0.008883 as published; a slightly smaller coefficient seen in some tools makes it about 0.6% lower still.

Is there a body surface area formula using weight only?

All eight formulas here need height as well as weight, because two people of the same weight can have quite different shapes. If the height is unknown, measure it rather than guess; under Mosteller, a 10 cm error changes the result by about 3%.

Can I enter the weight in grams?

Yes. Pick g in the weight unit menu, which is handy for babies. The Boyd formula works in grams anyway, and the others are converted to kilograms first.

Can I use this for a dog or a cat?

No. These formulas were fitted to human bodies, and animals have very different shapes. Medicine doses for pets should come from a vet.
