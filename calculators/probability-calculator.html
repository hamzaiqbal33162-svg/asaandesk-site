What probability is

Probability measures how likely something is, on a scale from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). It can be written as a fraction, a decimal or a percentage: a fair coin landing heads has probability 1/2 = 0.5 = 50%. When all outcomes are equally likely, the probability of an event is the number of outcomes that give it divided by the total number of outcomes, so rolling an even number on a die is 3/6 = 0.5.

This probability calculator has four tools. Two events works out every combination of two independent events A and B: both, at least one, neither, exactly one and each on its own. Solve from two values runs that backwards from any two known probabilities. Repeated events finds the chance of something happening every time, never or at least once over many tries. Normal distribution finds the share of values between two bounds on a bell curve. Each tool shows its working, so you can use it to check homework as well as to get an answer.

The key idea behind the first three tools is independence: two events are independent when one happening does not change the chance of the other, as with two separate coin tosses or a coin and a die. Then the chance of both is simply the product of their chances. Events that do affect each other, such as drawing two cards without putting the first one back, need conditional probability, which is explained below.

Probability formulas

These are the rules the calculator uses, plus the two you need for events that are not independent. P(A) means the probability of A, A′ or "not A" is its complement, A ∩ B ("A and B") is the intersection and A ∪ B ("A or B") is the union.

Independent and mutually exclusive are different ideas. Mutually exclusive events cannot happen together (rolling a 2 and a 5 on one die), so P(A and B) = 0. Independent events can happen together, and knowing one happened tells you nothing about the other. Two events with non-zero chances cannot be both.

Worked examples with steps

Two events. A coin shows heads (A, 0.5) and a die shows a six (B, 1/6). The steps read: P(A and B) = 0.5 × 1/6 = 0.0833 (1 in 12); P(A or B) = 0.5 + 0.1667 − 0.0833 = 0.5833; P(neither) = 1 − 0.5833 = 0.4167; P(exactly one) = 0.5.

Percentages. If there is a 70% chance your bus is on time and a 60% chance your friend is on time, and the two are independent, both are on time with chance 0.7 × 0.6 = 0.42 (42%), at least one with 88%, and neither with only 12%.

Cards. In a 52-card deck, drawing an ace (A, 1/13) and drawing a heart (B, 1/4) are independent, because exactly one of the 13 hearts is an ace. P(ace and heart) = 1/52 ≈ 0.0192, and P(ace or heart) = 1/13 + 1/4 − 1/52 = 16/52 ≈ 0.3077.

Solving backwards. Fill in P(A and B) = 0.12 and P(A or B) = 0.58. Then P(A) + P(B) = 0.58 + 0.12 = 0.7 and P(A) × P(B) = 0.12, so P(A) and P(B) are the solutions of t² − 0.7t + 0.12 = 0: 0.4 and 0.3. From P(not B) = 0.25 and P(neither) = 0.1, the solver gets P(B) = 0.75, P(A or B) = 0.9 and then P(A) = 0.6.

A Pakistan example. A captain calls the toss correctly with chance 0.5 each time. Over a five-match series, winning all five tosses has chance 0.5⁵ = 0.03125, about 3.1%, and losing all five has the same chance. Winning at least one toss is 1 − 0.03125 = 0.96875, or 96.9%.

When cards are not put back, the events are dependent. Two aces in a row without replacement is 4/52 × 3/51 = 12/2,652 = 1/221 ≈ 0.0045, because after one ace only 3 aces remain among 51 cards. With replacement it is (1/13)² ≈ 0.0059. For dependent events like these, multiply the first chance by the conditional chance of the second, P(B | A), rather than using the calculator's independent formulas directly.

Probability of multiple events and repeated attempts

For three or more independent events, the rules extend directly. All of them happen with the product of their chances: for events of 0.5, 0.4 and 0.3, that is 0.5 × 0.4 × 0.3 = 0.06. None of them happens with the product of the complements, 0.5 × 0.6 × 0.7 = 0.21, so at least one happens with 1 − 0.21 = 0.79. To use the Two events tool for three events, combine two of them first: the both-happen answer for A and B can be typed as the new A.

For one event tried many times, use the Repeated events tab. The chance of something happening at least once is always 1 minus the chance it never happens, which avoids adding chances (a common mistake: four tries at 1/6 do not give 4/6). Rolling at least one six in 4 rolls of a die has chance 1 − (5/6)⁴ = 0.5177, just better than even, while at least one double six in 24 rolls of two dice is 1 − (35/36)²⁴ = 0.4914, just worse. The two look alike but are not equal, which is why it pays to calculate rather than guess.

The same calculation answers everyday questions. If a component fails with chance 2% in a year, the chance that at least one of 10 independent components fails in a year is 1 − 0.98¹⁰, about 18.3%. The more attempts, the closer "at least once" gets to 1, even for small chances.

The normal distribution and z-scores

Many measurements, from test scores to manufacturing sizes, cluster around an average in a symmetric bell shape called the normal distribution. It is fixed by two numbers: the mean μ, at the centre, and the standard deviation σ, which sets the spread. A value x is turned into a z-score, z = (x − μ) ÷ σ, the number of standard deviations it lies from the mean, and the area under the curve between two z-scores is the probability of a value falling between them.

The calculator finds that area exactly. On the standard curve (mean 0, standard deviation 1), the area between −1 and 1 is 0.68269, between −2 and 2 is 0.9545 and between −3 and 3 is 0.9973, the 68-95-99.7 rule. With a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15, the share below 130 is 0.97725 (z = 2), so about 2.3% lie above it. If exam marks had a mean of 60 and a standard deviation of 10, a mark above 75 (z = 1.5) would be reached by about 6.7% of students, and 68.3% would score between 50 and 70.

Probability at school, in Excel, and as odds

Probability is section 8 of the Cambridge O Level Mathematics syllabus (4024, exams in 2025 to 2027): the 0 to 1 scale, P(A′) = 1 − P(A), relative and expected frequency, and combined events using sample space diagrams, Venn diagrams and tree diagrams, with and without replacement. The Two events tool's Venn diagram and the dice table above match that style of question.

In Excel, =NORM.DIST(130, 100, 15, TRUE) gives the cumulative normal probability below 130 (0.97725), and =NORM.S.DIST(1.96, TRUE) the standard normal value. For simple events, divide a COUNTIF of the favourable rows by the total number of rows.

Odds are another way to write a chance. Odds of 3 to 1 against mean 1 favourable outcome for every 3 unfavourable ones, so the probability is 1 ÷ (3 + 1) = 0.25. Going the other way, a probability of 0.2 is odds of 4 to 1 against.

Common mistakes and related calculators

  • Adding probabilities of events that can happen together: P(A or B) needs − P(A and B).
  • Multiplying probabilities of events that are not independent: use P(A) × P(B | A).
  • Adding chances for repeated tries: use 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ for "at least once".
  • Treating a percentage as a whole number: 30% is 0.3, not 30. The calculator accepts 30% as typed.
  • Reading a normal area as the chance of one exact value: for a continuous measurement the chance of exactly 130.000 is 0; ask for a range.

How it works

Choose a tool. Two events: type the chance of A and of B (as 0.5, 50% or 1/2) and you get all eight ways the pair can turn out, from both happening to neither, with a Venn picture of the four parts. Solve from two values: fill in any two boxes, for example P(A) and P(A and B), and the other values are worked out. Repeated events: give each event's chance and how many times it is tried to see the chance it always, never or at least once happens. Normal distribution: give the mean, the standard deviation and a range to get the share of values inside it, drawn on a bell curve. Every tool treats A and B as independent.

The formula

  • P(not A) = 1 − P(A)
  • P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) for independent events
  • P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B); P(neither) = 1 − P(A or B)
  • P(exactly one) = P(A) + P(B) − 2 × P(A and B); P(A but not B) = P(A) × (1 − P(B))
  • Repeated n times: P(every time) = pⁿ, P(never) = (1 − p)ⁿ, P(at least once) = 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ
  • Normal: P(a < X < b) = Φ((b − μ) ÷ σ) − Φ((a − μ) ÷ σ), where Φ is the standard normal cumulative probability

A worked example

A coin lands heads with chance 0.5 (A) and a die shows a six with chance 1/6 (B). Both happen with chance 0.5 × 1/6 = 1/12, about 0.0833. At least one happens with chance 0.5 + 0.1667 − 0.0833 = 0.5833, and neither with 1 − 0.5833 = 0.4167.

Questions

How do I calculate probability?

When every outcome is equally likely, divide the number of outcomes you want by the total number of outcomes. Drawing a red card from a deck is 26/52 = 0.5. For combinations of events, use the rules: multiply for "and" with independent events, and add then subtract the overlap for "or".

What is the probability formula for A and B?

For independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B), so a 0.5 and a 0.4 chance give 0.2. If B depends on A, use P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B | A), the chance of A times the chance of B once A has happened.

How do I calculate the probability of multiple events?

For independent events, multiply the chances of all of them for "all happen", multiply the complements for "none happen", and take 1 minus that for "at least one happens". Three events of 0.5, 0.4 and 0.3 all happen with chance 0.06 and at least one happens with chance 0.79.

How do I calculate probability with percentages?

Change each percentage to a decimal by dividing by 100, use the rules, and multiply the answer by 100. A 70% and a 60% chance both happen with 0.7 × 0.6 = 0.42, which is 42%. This calculator accepts percentages as typed, such as 70%.

Why is “at least once” worked out from “never”?

Because it is the only outcome left over. If a 1 in 6 chance is tried 4 times, the chance it never happens is (5/6)⁴ ≈ 0.482, so the chance it happens at least once is 1 − 0.482 ≈ 0.518. Adding the single chances (4 × 1/6) would overcount.

What is the probability of rolling a 7 with two dice?

6 of the 36 equally likely results add up to 7 (1 + 6, 2 + 5, 3 + 4 and the reverse of each), so the probability is 6/36 = 1/6, about 16.7%. 7 is the most likely total; 2 and 12 are the least likely, at 1/36 each.

What does independent mean here?

Two events are independent when one happening does not change the chance of the other, like two coin tosses. All the formulas on this page assume that. For events that affect each other, such as drawing two cards without putting the first back, P(A and B) is not simply P(A) × P(B).

Which pairs can the solver use?

Any two of the eight boxes that describe different things. A box and its opposite, such as P(A) and P(not A), or P(A or B) and P(neither), carry the same information, so they cannot pin down both events. Some pairs, like P(A and B) with P(A or B), fit two answers that only swap A and B; the larger one is then shown as A.

How do I find the chance of a value above or below one number on a normal curve?

Leave the other bound empty (or type -inf or inf). For example mean 100, standard deviation 15, no left bound and a right bound of 130 gives the share of values below 130, about 0.977.

What is the table of ranges in the normal tool?

It lists the middle range that holds a given share of all values, from 68.27% (one standard deviation either side of the mean) up to 99.999%. For a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1, 95% of values lie between −1.96 and 1.96.

How do I calculate a normal probability in Excel?

Use =NORM.DIST(x, mean, standard_dev, TRUE) for the chance of a value at or below x, and subtract two of them for a range: =NORM.DIST(70,60,10,TRUE) − NORM.DIST(50,60,10,TRUE) gives about 0.6827. The Normal distribution tab here gives the same number with a shaded curve.

How do I turn odds into a probability?

For odds of a to b against, the probability is b ÷ (a + b); for odds of a to b in favour, it is a ÷ (a + b). Odds of 3 to 1 against give 1 ÷ 4 = 0.25, and odds of 2 to 1 in favour give 2 ÷ 3 ≈ 0.667.
