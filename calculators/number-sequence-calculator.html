What a number sequence is

A number sequence is a list of numbers in a set order that follows a rule, such as 3, 7, 11, 15 … (add 4 each time). Each number is a term: a₁ is the first term, a₂ the second and aₙ the n-th. Adding up the terms gives a series. This number sequence calculator handles the three sequences that school and everyday maths use most: arithmetic, geometric and Fibonacci.

For each one it finds any term you ask for, the sum of the terms up to it, the rule for the n-th term and, for a shrinking geometric sequence, the sum of the whole infinite series. The steps show the formula with your numbers in it, so the page works as a number sequence solver with a full solution.

How to use the number sequence calculator

  • Pick a tab: Arithmetic, Geometric or Fibonacci.
  • Arithmetic: type the First term a₁ (3 to start), the Common difference d (4), which can be negative or a decimal, and Which term n (10). n is also how many terms are added up, and can be as large as 1,000,000,000.
  • Geometric: type the First term a₁ (3), the Common ratio r (2), which can be a decimal such as 0.5 or a negative number, and Which term n (10, up to 1,000,000).
  • Fibonacci: type Which term n (15 to start, up to 10,000). The sequence starts 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8.
  • First terms, differences and ratios can be any number between −10¹⁵ and 10¹⁵. Answers update as you type.

Understanding the results

  • The headline is the term you asked for, such as 10th term = 39 for the opening arithmetic example.
  • The sequence lists the first nine terms so you can check the pattern.
  • Sum of the first n terms adds up a₁ to aₙ.
  • Rule for any term gives the n-th term formula in its simplest form: aₙ = 4n − 1 for 3, 7, 11 …, or aₙ = 3 × 2ⁿ⁻¹ for 3, 6, 12 ….
  • Sum of the infinite series appears when a geometric ratio is between −1 and 1.
  • Fibonacci adds Fₙ ÷ Fₙ₋₁, which gets closer and closer to the golden ratio, and every digit of large terms (up to 400 digits).
  • A table lists each term with the sum so far (all of them up to 100 terms; it is collapsed when there are more than 10), and a bar chart shows the first 20 terms. Very large or small numbers use the × 10ⁿ form.

The arithmetic sum is the average of the first and last terms times the number of terms. The classic exercise of adding up 1 to 100 uses it: 100 × (1 + 100) ÷ 2 = 5,050, which the calculator confirms with a₁ = 1, d = 1 and n = 100.

How to work out a number sequence from its terms

Given a few terms and asked for the next one or the n-th term, test the simple patterns in this order:

  • Differences: subtract each term from the next. If the gap is always the same, the sequence is arithmetic. 5, 8, 11, 14 has gaps of 3, so d = 3, a₁ = 5, and the calculator gives the rule aₙ = 3n + 2, the 10th term 32 and the sum of the first 10 terms, 185.
  • Ratios: divide each term by the one before. If the ratio is always the same, it is geometric. 2, 6, 18, 54 has ratio 3, so the 6th term is 2 × 3⁵ = 486 and the first six terms add up to 728.
  • Second differences: if the gaps themselves go up by a constant amount, the sequence is quadratic, with an n² term. For 2, 6, 12, 20, 30 the gaps are 4, 6, 8, 10 and the second difference is 2; half of that is the n² coefficient, so the rule is aₙ = n² + n. Check: 1² + 1 = 2 and 3² + 3 = 12. Cubic sequences have a constant third difference.
  • Sums of the two before: 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 is Fibonacci; sequences like 2, 1, 3, 4, 7 (the Lucas numbers) follow the same rule from different starting values.
  • Other patterns: squares (1, 4, 9, 16), cubes (1, 8, 27, 64), primes (2, 3, 5, 7, 11) and triangular numbers (1, 3, 6, 10, 15) are worth recognising on sight.

This calculator covers the arithmetic, geometric and Fibonacci cases directly. For a quadratic or other pattern, find the rule by hand as above, then use it to get any term.

Worked examples

A falling arithmetic sequence: 100, 93, 86, 79 … has d = −7. Its 15th term is 100 + 14 × (−7) = 2, the first 15 terms add up to 15 × (100 + 2) ÷ 2 = 765, and the rule is aₙ = −7n + 107.

A geometric sequence with a negative ratio: 1, −2, 4, −8 … has r = −2, so the signs alternate. The 8th term is 1 × (−2)⁷ = −128 and the first 8 terms add up to −85.

An infinite series: 0.9 + 0.09 + 0.009 + … has a₁ = 0.9 and r = 0.1. The first five terms add to 0.99999, and the infinite sum is 0.9 ÷ (1 − 0.1) = 1, which is why 0.999… recurring equals exactly 1.

Doubling: start with 1 and double 63 times (a₁ = 1, r = 2, n = 64) and the 64th term is about 9.2233720368548 × 10¹⁸, with a total of about 1.844674407371 × 10¹⁹. That is the old puzzle of grains of rice doubling on each square of a chessboard.

Fibonacci: the 50th term is 12,586,269,025, and the 100th has 21 digits, 354,224,848,179,261,915,075, which the calculator shows in full.

Sequences and money: salary increments in PKR

Sequences describe pay and savings plans directly. Say a job in Karachi starts at Rs 600,000 a year with a fixed increment of Rs 36,000 every year. That is arithmetic with a₁ = 600,000 and d = 36,000: in year 10 the salary is Rs 924,000, and the ten years together pay Rs 7,620,000.

A raise of a fixed percentage is geometric instead. Rs 100,000 growing by 10% a year has r = 1.1: after nine raises, in year 10, it is about Rs 235,794.77, and the ten yearly amounts add up to about Rs 1,593,742.46. Over long periods a percentage rise always overtakes a fixed one, because each geometric step is bigger than the last. The Compound Interest Calculator applies the same idea to savings with interest.

Fibonacci numbers and the golden ratio

Divide each Fibonacci number by the one before and the answers swing above and below a fixed value, getting closer each time. That value is the golden ratio φ = (1 + √5) ÷ 2 ≈ 1.6180339887499.

For school, in Excel, and common mistakes

Sequences are part of the Cambridge O Level Mathematics syllabus (4024, exams in 2025 to 2027): continuing a sequence, the term-to-term rule, and finding and using the n-th term of linear, quadratic, cubic and exponential sequences. The arithmetic and geometric tabs check linear and exponential answers; the second-difference method above handles the quadratic ones.

In Excel 2021, 2024 and Microsoft 365, =SEQUENCE(10, 1, 3, 4) fills ten rows with 3, 7, 11 … 39, the opening arithmetic example. For a geometric list, type the first term in A1 and =A1*2 in A2, then fill down.

  • Using n instead of n − 1: the n-th term adds the difference n − 1 times, because the first term already counts as one.
  • Mixing up the term and the sum: the 10th term of 3, 7, 11 … is 39, while the first 10 terms add up to 210.
  • Expecting an infinite sum when |r| ≥ 1: with r = 2 or r = −1 the total never settles.
  • Calling a sequence geometric because it grows fast: check that the ratio really stays the same.

How it works

Choose the kind of sequence. In an arithmetic sequence you add the same number (the common difference) each time: 3, 7, 11, 15, … adds 4. In a geometric sequence you multiply by the same number (the common ratio) each time: 3, 6, 12, 24, … multiplies by 2. In the Fibonacci sequence each term is the sum of the two before it: 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, … Type the first term, the step and which term you want; the result gives that term, the sum of all the terms up to it, the rule for any term, a table of the terms and a chart. Decimals are kept exact, and Fibonacci numbers show every digit.

The formula

  • Arithmetic: aₙ = a₁ + (n − 1) × d, and the sum Sₙ = n × (a₁ + aₙ) ÷ 2
  • Geometric: aₙ = a₁ × rⁿ⁻¹, and Sₙ = a₁ × (1 − rⁿ) ÷ (1 − r) (or n × a₁ when r = 1)
  • Infinite geometric series, when −1 < r < 1: S∞ = a₁ ÷ (1 − r)
  • Fibonacci: F₁ = F₂ = 1 and Fₙ = Fₙ₋₁ + Fₙ₋₂
  • Sum of the first n Fibonacci numbers: Fₙ₊₂ − 1

A worked example

Arithmetic, first term 2 and difference 5: the 20th term is 2 + 19 × 5 = 97, and the first 20 terms add up to 20 × (2 + 97) ÷ 2 = 990. Geometric, first term 3 and ratio 0.5: the 6th term is 3 × 0.5⁵ = 0.09375, and since the ratio is below 1 the whole infinite series adds up to 3 ÷ (1 − 0.5) = 6.

Questions

How do I work out a number sequence?

Look at the gaps between terms first: a constant gap means an arithmetic sequence, a constant ratio means a geometric one, and gaps that grow by a constant amount mean a quadratic sequence. Once you know the type, use its n-th term formula, or type the first term and the step into the matching tab here.

What is the formula for the n-th term of a sequence?

For an arithmetic sequence, aₙ = a₁ + (n − 1)d; for a geometric one, aₙ = a₁ × rⁿ⁻¹. For 3, 7, 11 … that gives aₙ = 3 + 4(n − 1) = 4n − 1, so the 20th term is 79.

How do I know if a sequence is arithmetic or geometric?

Subtract each term from the next: if the difference is always the same, it is arithmetic. Divide each term by the one before: if the ratio is always the same, it is geometric. 2, 4, 6, 8 is arithmetic (add 2); 2, 4, 8, 16 is geometric (times 2).

How do I find the n-th term of a quadratic sequence?

Work out the second differences; half of that constant is the coefficient of n². Subtract that n² part from each term, and what is left is a linear sequence whose rule you find as usual. For 3, 8, 15, 24 the second difference is 2, so start with n²; the remainders 2, 4, 6, 8 give 2n, so the rule is n² + 2n.

How do I calculate the sum of a number series?

For an arithmetic series, multiply the number of terms by the average of the first and last: Sₙ = n(a₁ + aₙ) ÷ 2. For a geometric series, Sₙ = a₁(1 − rⁿ) ÷ (1 − r). The Sum of the first n terms line gives this for any n.

When does an infinite geometric series have a sum?

Only when the ratio is between −1 and 1. Then each term is smaller than the one before, the terms get closer to 0, and the total settles at a₁ ÷ (1 − r). With a ratio of 1 or more (or −1 or less) the total never settles.

Can the difference or the ratio be negative or a decimal?

Yes. A negative difference makes the terms go down, a negative ratio makes their signs switch back and forth, and a ratio such as 0.5 halves each term. Decimals you type are kept exact, so no rounding builds up along the sequence.

How do I make a number sequence in Excel?

In current versions of Excel, =SEQUENCE(rows, columns, start, step) creates an arithmetic sequence in one formula, so =SEQUENCE(5, 1, 10, 5) gives 10, 15, 20, 25, 30. In older versions, type the first two terms, select both and drag the fill handle down.

Why does the Fibonacci sequence start with 1, 1?

This calculator counts F₁ = 1 and F₂ = 1, so the 10th term is 55. Some books start from F₀ = 0; their n-th term is the same number one place later.

How large can n be?

Up to a billion for arithmetic sequences, a million for geometric ones and 10,000 for Fibonacci numbers. Very big results use the × 10ⁿ form with 14 significant digits; Fibonacci results also list every digit up to 400 digits.
