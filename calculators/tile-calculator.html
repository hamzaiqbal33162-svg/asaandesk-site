What a tile calculator works out

A tile calculator tells you how many tiles you need to cover a floor, a wall, a shower or a kitchen backsplash, and how many boxes to buy. It divides the area of the surface by the area one tile covers, including its share of the grout joint, rounds up to whole tiles, and adds extra for cuts and breakage.

The count depends on three things: the size of the surface, the size of the tile and the width of the joint between tiles. Larger tiles mean fewer pieces, and wider joints mean slightly fewer tiles because each one, with its joint, covers a little more. The page also prices the job per tile, per box or per square foot or square meter, which is how tiles are sold in most shops, including in Pakistan.

How to use the tile calculator, step by step

  • Tile length and Tile width: the size of one tile. Each box has its own unit menu (in, cm, mm, ft, m, yd), so a 60 × 60 cm tile can go on a floor measured in feet.
  • Grout gap: the width of the joint between tiles, such as 1/8 in or 3 mm. Type 0 for tiles laid edge to edge.
  • Surface to cover: choose Length and width for one rectangle, or Total area if you have already added up several walls or a floor of odd shape (in ft², m², yd², in² or cm²).
  • Tiles per box: from the box label, if you want the number of boxes. It starts at 12.
  • Under Extra and price: Extra to buy (10% unless you change it), and an optional Price per tile, per box, per ft², per m², per yd², per in² or per cm².

Results update as you type. Pick the currency in the currency menu if you add a price.

Understanding the results

Tiles to cover the area is the bare count, rounded up. To buy with 10% extra (or your own percentage) is the number to order. Boxes divides the count by the tiles per box and rounds up, and the Area line repeats the surface in ft² and m².

The rows With 5% to 10% extra and Boxes with 5% to 10% extra show the usual range, so you can see how much one more box adds. With a price, Cost and Cost with 5% to 10% extra appear; a price per area is applied to the surface itself, with the extra percentage added in the note under it. A note also gives how much one tile covers with and without its joint.

Worked examples: bathroom, shower walls, backsplash and kitchen

Bathroom floor. 8 ft × 6 ft is 48 ft². With 12 × 24 in tiles and a 1/8 in joint, the bare count is 24 tiles, 3 boxes of 8. With 10% extra buy 27 tiles, which takes 4 boxes.

Shower walls. A back wall of 5 ft × 8 ft and two side walls of 3 ft × 8 ft add up to 40 + 24 + 24 = 88 ft². Choose Total area, type 88 ft², and use 4 × 12 in tiles with a 1/16 in joint: 259 tiles. Set the extra to 15% for the many cuts around the niche, the valve and the corners, and buy 298 tiles, 8 boxes of 40.

Kitchen backsplash. A strip 10 ft long and 18 in high is 15 ft². With 3 × 6 in tiles and a 1/8 in joint it needs 113 tiles, or 125 with 10% extra.

Metric kitchen floor. A floor of 4 m × 3.5 m = 14 m² with 60 × 60 cm tiles and a 3 mm joint needs 39 tiles; with 10% extra buy 43, which is 11 boxes of 4.

Tiles in square feet in Pakistan

In Pakistan tile prices are often quoted per square foot and tiles sold by the box, while sizes are given in inches (such as 24 × 24) or centimeters (60 × 60). Mixed units are no problem here: type the tile in inches or centimeters, the room in feet and the joint in millimeters.

A room 14 ft × 12 ft is 168 sq ft. With 24 × 24 in tiles and a 3 mm joint it needs 42 tiles, and 47 with 10% extra, which is 12 boxes of 4. At an example rate of Rs 250 per square foot, choose per ft² and the area costs Rs 42,000 (the note adds 10% for the extra tiles). Ask whether the quoted rate covers the tiles only or also the fixing, the bond (adhesive) and the grout.

Tile adhesive is usually sold in bags with a coverage printed on them per square foot or square meter at a given trowel size, so work out the bags from the same area and the maker's figure.

How the tile formula works

Each tile covers its own area plus one joint along two edges, because every joint is shared between two neighbours. So the area per tile is (tile length + gap) × (tile width + gap). For a 12 in tile with a 1/4 in joint that is 12.25 × 12.25 = 150.06 in², against 144 in² for the tile alone.

The number of tiles is the surface area divided by that figure, rounded up, because you cannot buy part of a tile. A 14 ft × 12 ft floor (168 ft²) takes 168 tiles of 12 × 12 in laid with no joint, but 162 with a 1/4 in joint. The calculator removes tiny computer rounding errors before rounding up, so an area that needs exactly 200 tiles shows 200, not 201.

Tips for layout, waste and spares

  • Use about 10% extra for a straight layout and 15% for diagonal, herringbone or other patterns, or rooms with many corners, pipes and doorways.
  • Keep a few spare tiles from the same batch for repairs; shades can vary between production runs.
  • Measure each wall or floor area separately and add them, and subtract large openings such as windows or a bath, but keep the extra on the full area because the edges around openings need many cut pieces.
  • Large tiles waste more per cut, so in small rooms they can need a bigger allowance than the area alone suggests.
  • Check that tiles are rated for the job: floor and shower tiles need to be suitable for wet areas and foot traffic.

How it works

Type the size of one tile and the grout gap, then the length and width of the floor or wall (or its total area) and, if you know it, how many tiles come in a box. The number of tiles appears as you type, with the boxes, the usual 5% to 10% extra for cuts and breakage, and the amount to buy with your own allowance (10% unless you change it under Extra and price). Add a price per tile, per box or per area to see the cost. Every box has its own unit menu, so the tile can be in centimeters while the room is in feet.

The formula

  • Area covered by one tile = (tile length + gap) × (tile width + gap)
  • Tiles = area ÷ area per tile, rounded up
  • Boxes = tiles ÷ tiles per box, rounded up
  • Extra: tiles × 1.05 to tiles × 1.10 (each rounded up); to buy = tiles × (1 + your extra %), rounded up
  • Cost = tiles × price per tile, boxes × price per box, or area × price per area

A worked example

A bathroom floor 8 ft × 6 ft = 48 ft² with 12 × 24 in tiles and a 1/8 in joint: each tile covers 24.125 × 12.125 = 292.52 in², so 48 × 144 ÷ 292.52 = 23.6, rounded up to 24 tiles, or 3 boxes of 8. With 10% extra, buy 27 tiles (4 boxes).

Questions

How do I calculate how many tiles I need?

Multiply the length and width of the surface to get its area, divide by the area of one tile plus its joint, and round up. Add 10% for cuts. A 48 ft² floor needs 24 tiles of 12 × 24 in with a 1/8 in joint, or 27 with the extra.

How many tiles per square meter?

It depends on the tile size: 60 × 60 cm tiles with a 3 mm joint come to about 2.75 per m² (28 for 10 m²), and 30 × 30 cm tiles to about 10.9 per m² (109 for 10 m²).

How do I calculate tiles in square feet?

Work out the area in square feet (length × width in feet), then divide by the tile's area in square feet: a 24 × 24 in tile is 4 sq ft, so 168 sq ft needs about 42 tiles once the joints are counted.

How do I calculate tiles for a bathroom?

Do the floor and each wall separately, subtract the door, window and bath or shower tray, and add the totals. Use the Total area option for the walls and add 10% to 15% extra for all the cuts.

How much extra tile should I buy?

About 10 percent for a simple straight layout, 15 percent for a diagonal or herringbone pattern or a room with many corners. Keep a few spare tiles: the same batch can be hard to find later if one cracks.

Does the grout gap change the number of tiles?

Yes, a little. Each tile covers its own size plus one joint each way, so wider joints mean fewer tiles. With 12 in tiles a 1/4 in joint saves about 4 percent; with small mosaic tiles the effect is much bigger.

How do I tile a wall with a window or door?

Work out the whole wall, then the window or door the same way, and subtract the second count from the first. Keep the extra percentage on the full wall, because the edges around openings need many cut tiles.

Why is the count rounded up?

You cannot buy part of a tile or part of a box, so the calculator always rounds up. It also removes tiny computer rounding errors first, so an area that needs exactly 200 tiles shows 200, not 201.

Can I use a price per square foot?

Yes. Under Extra and price, type the price and choose per ft² (or per m²); the cost of the surface appears, with the extra percentage added in the note.
