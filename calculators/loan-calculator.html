What a loan calculator (EMI calculator) does

A loan calculator shows what a loan really costs: the installment you pay each period, the total of all the payments and how much of that total is interest. In Pakistan and India the equal monthly installment is called the EMI, short for equated monthly installment; elsewhere it is simply the monthly payment. The maths is the same either way.

This calculator has three tabs for the three ways a loan can be repaid. Regular payments is the everyday loan, such as a car, bike, personal, home or business loan, repaid in equal installments. Pay all at the end is a deferred loan, where nothing is paid until the term ends. Bond (face value) starts from the amount due at the end and tells you what it is worth today.

Results update as you type, in the currency you choose at the top, and everything is worked out on your own device.

How to use the loan calculator

  • Loan amount: what you borrow, after any down payment (Regular payments and Pay all at the end tabs).
  • Amount due at the end (face value): on the Bond tab, the fixed amount repaid when the term ends.
  • Term in years and months: the loan tenure. Enter years, months or both, for example 2 years and 6 months, up to 100 years in all.
  • Interest rate: the yearly rate the lender quotes, in percent (6 by default).
  • How often you pay: every day, week, 2 weeks, twice a month, month, quarter, 6 months or year (Regular payments tab).
  • Interest compounds: how often interest is added. On Regular payments it sits under Compounding and is monthly by default, the usual way loan rates are quoted; on the other two tabs it is shown next to the rate and is yearly by default.

Enter the money you actually receive as the loan amount. If the bank takes a processing fee out of the loan, the installment is still worked out on the full amount, so treat the fee as an extra cost when you compare offers.

Understanding the results

On the Regular payments tab the headline is your installment, labelled for the frequency you chose. Below it are the total of all payments, the total interest with its share of everything you pay, and the effective yearly rate, which is what the quoted rate grows to once compounding is counted: 6% compounded monthly is 6.168% a year. If the term is not a whole number of payment periods, a smaller last payment is shown too.

The schedule tables split every payment into interest and principal and show the balance left, and a yearly table adds up each year. Two charts show the split between principal and interest and how the balance falls over time. This full list of payments is the loan's amortization schedule.

The term has a big effect on both the installment and the total interest. Here is a loan of 100,000 at 6% a year, paid monthly:

Payment frequency and compounding

When the yearly rate and the compounding stay the same, paying more often changes the cost only a little, because each payment simply covers a shorter period. What matters is how long the money stays borrowed. Yearly payments cost the most here because each year's principal stays borrowed for the whole year before any of it is repaid.

Compounding works the other way: the more often interest is added, the more you pay. With monthly payments, the same 6% costs 32,268.83 in interest if it compounds yearly, 33,224.60 if it compounds monthly and 33,312.18 if it compounds daily. Leave the monthly default unless your loan agreement says otherwise.

Paying every 2 weeks here is not the mortgage trick of paying half the monthly amount every two weeks. That trick adds the equivalent of one extra monthly payment each year and ends the loan early; this tab works out the true two-week installment for the same term.

Worked examples: EMI, car loan, deferred loan and bond

Monthly EMI: 100,000 for 10 years at 6% gives 1,110.21 a month. The first month's interest is 100,000 × 0.5% = 500.00, so 610.21 of the first payment reduces the loan; by the last payment nearly all of it is principal.

Car loan in Pakistan: Rs 2,000,000 for 3 years at 18% a year gives an EMI of Rs 72,304.79. Over 36 months you repay Rs 2,602,972.48, so Rs 602,972.48 is markup, and the effective yearly rate is 19.562%. The rate is only an example; use the one in your bank's offer.

Personal loan: Rs 1,000,000 for 5 years at 22% costs Rs 27,618.91 a month and Rs 657,134.72 in interest, almost 40% of everything you pay. At rates this high, a shorter term saves a great deal.

Deferred loan: borrow 100,000 for 10 years at 6% compounded yearly with nothing paid until the end, and you owe 100,000 × 1.06^10 = 179,084.77. Bond: a bond that pays 100,000 in 10 years, priced to yield 6% compounded yearly, is worth 100,000 ÷ 1.06^10 = 55,839.48 today.

Watch out for flat rates. With a flat rate, interest is charged on the original amount for the whole term, even as you repay it. A 12% flat rate on Rs 1,000,000 for 3 years means Rs 360,000 of interest and installments of Rs 37,777.78; on a reducing balance, which is what this calculator uses, that installment equals a rate of about 21.2% a year. Always ask for the reducing-balance rate.

Loans in Pakistan: KIBOR, Islamic financing and how much you can borrow

Bank loans in Pakistan are often priced as KIBOR (the Karachi Interbank Offered Rate) plus a margin, with the rate reset at set intervals. For example, HBL's Islamic home finance (checked in November 2026) uses the 1-year KIBOR plus 3% to 4.2%, with the monthly amount fixed for each 12 months. For context, the State Bank of Pakistan raised its policy rate to 12% from 27 October 2026. When your rate resets, enter the remaining balance and the remaining term to get the new installment.

Islamic banks charge profit rather than interest, through structures such as Diminishing Musharakah, for which the State Bank has adopted a Shariah standard. The monthly amounts still fall on a declining balance, so entering the profit rate as the interest rate gives a useful estimate; the bank's own schedule is the final word.

How much can you borrow on your salary? Work backwards from the installment you can afford: loan = installment × (1 − (1 + r)^−n) ÷ r. If you can pay Rs 50,000 a month for 5 years at 22%, that installment repays a loan of about Rs 1,810,354; at 18% it repays about Rs 1,969,013. Banks also apply their own limits; for housing finance the State Bank caps all monthly installments together at 65% of net disposable income.

For an interest-free loan, set the rate to 0. The installment is then simply the amount divided by the number of payments: Rs 1,200,000 over 5 years is Rs 20,000 a month.

Secured and unsecured loans, loan-to-value and tips

A secured loan is backed by something you own, such as a house or a car, which the lender can take if you do not repay. An unsecured loan, such as most personal loans and credit cards, has no collateral; the CFPB notes that lenders see these as riskier and may charge a higher rate for them. With either kind, missed payments can lead to collection and a damaged credit record.

The loan-to-value ratio (LTV) compares the loan with the value of what it buys: a Rs 7,000,000 loan on a Rs 10,000,000 house is 70% LTV. Lenders cap it to keep a cushion if prices fall; for housing finance in Pakistan the State Bank's cap is 90%.

  • Compare loans by total cost, not just by the installment; a longer term always looks cheaper per month.
  • Ask whether the rate is fixed or floating, what it is linked to and how often it resets.
  • Check fees, insurance and prepayment charges, which are not part of the installment shown here.
  • Pay extra when you can: every extra payment cuts the balance that interest is charged on.
  • In a spreadsheet, =PMT(6%/12, 120, -100000) gives the same 1,110.21; keep the rate and the number of periods in the same unit.

How it works

Pick the kind of loan on the tabs. Regular payments is the usual loan with equal installments (EMI): enter the amount, the term and the yearly rate, and choose how often you pay. The calculator turns the yearly rate into a rate for one payment period, finds the one payment that clears the loan by the end of the term, and lists every payment split into interest and principal. Pay all at the end is a deferred loan: nothing is paid during the term, interest is added to the balance and compounds, and one amount is due at the end. Bond works the other way round: you know the amount due at the end (the face value) and get what it is worth today.

The formula

  • Rate per payment r = (1 + i ÷ m)^(m ÷ p) − 1, where i is the yearly rate, m the times interest compounds a year and p the payments a year; continuous compounding: r = e^(i ÷ p) − 1
  • Payments n = term in years × p (a year is 52 weeks or 365.25 days)
  • Installment (EMI) P = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n); at 0% interest P = L ÷ n
  • Each payment: interest = balance × r; principal = P − interest; new balance = balance − principal
  • Pay all at the end: amount due = L × (1 + i ÷ m)^(m × T), or L × e^(i × T) when compounding continuously
  • Bond: value today = face value ÷ (1 + i ÷ m)^(m × T)

A worked example

A loan of 100,000 for 10 years at 6% a year, paid monthly with monthly compounding: r = 0.06 ÷ 12 = 0.005 and n = 120, so P = 100,000 × 0.005 ÷ (1 − 1.005^−120) = 1,110.21 a month. The 120 payments add up to 133,224.60, so 33,224.60 is interest. Left unpaid for 10 years at 6% compounded yearly, the same 100,000 grows to 100,000 × 1.06^10 = 179,084.77.

Questions

What is an EMI and how is it worked out?

EMI means equated monthly installment: the same payment every month until the loan is repaid. It is P = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), where L is the loan, r the monthly rate (yearly rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100 with monthly compounding) and n the number of months. Early payments are mostly interest; later ones mostly repay the loan.

How do I calculate a loan installment in Pakistan?

Choose PKR, enter the amount, the tenure and the yearly rate your bank quotes (often KIBOR plus a margin), and keep monthly payments. The calculator applies EMI = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n) with r = rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100. Rs 2,000,000 for 3 years at 18% comes to Rs 72,304.79 a month.

Can I use it for a home, car or bike loan in rupees?

Yes, for any loan with a fixed rate and equal installments. Pick your currency at the top, enter the amount you borrow after any down payment, the rate the bank quotes and the tenure. Banks may add fees or insurance and many rates float, so the bank's installment can differ a little.

How do I calculate loan interest?

Total interest is the total of all payments minus the amount borrowed. For 100,000 at 6% over 10 years, 120 payments of 1,110.21 add up to 133,224.60, so the interest is 33,224.60. For a single month, the interest is the balance times the monthly rate.

What is loan tenure or loan term?

It is the time you have to repay, in years and months. A longer tenure lowers each installment but raises the total interest: at 6%, a loan of 100,000 costs 15,996.81 in interest over 5 years and 71,943.45 over 20 years.

How do I calculate the loan amount on my salary?

Decide the installment you can afford after your other costs, then work backwards: loan = installment × (1 − (1 + r)^−n) ÷ r. Rs 50,000 a month for 5 years at 22% supports a loan of about Rs 1,810,354. Banks add their own limits, such as the State Bank's 65% cap on all installments for housing finance.

What does the compounding choice change?

It sets how the yearly rate is turned into a rate for each payment. Most loans quote a rate compounded monthly (APR), which is the default. If your lender compounds yearly or daily, choose that under Compounding and the payment updates.

What is the difference between a deferred loan and a bond here?

In both, nothing is paid until the end. For a deferred loan you know what you borrow and get the amount due at the end. For a bond you know the amount due at the end (the face value) and get what you receive today, the bond's price.

How do I calculate a loan payment in Excel?

Use =PMT(rate/12, months, -amount); for example =PMT(6%/12, 120, -100000) returns 1,110.21. Keep the rate and the number of periods in the same unit, a monthly rate with a number of months.

Why is my last payment different?

When the term is not a whole number of payment periods, for example 10 years of daily payments (3,652.5 days), the last payment only clears what is left, so it is smaller. The totals shown are what you really pay.
