What the big number calculator is for

A big number calculator does exact arithmetic on numbers that are too long for a phone or a pocket calculator. It keeps every digit, so multiplying two 20-digit numbers gives all 40 digits of the answer instead of a rounded figure in scientific notation.

It is useful for checking homework on factorials and powers, number theory and cryptography exercises, long decimal expansions such as √2 or 1 ÷ 7, and any sum where the last digits matter, like a long account number, a serial or a very precise measurement. It runs free online in your browser.

How to use it, step by step

  • Type X. It can be a whole number, a decimal or E-notation (3.5e19 means 3.5 × 10¹⁹, 2.5e-8 means 0.000000025). Commas and spaces are ignored, so 1,000,000 or a pasted 1,00,000 both work.
  • Pick the Operation: X + Y, X − Y, X × Y, X ÷ Y, X to the power Y, √X, X², X!, X mod Y, GCF of X and Y, or LCM of X and Y.
  • Type Y when the operation needs it (it is hidden for √X, X² and X!).
  • For division, square roots and negative powers, open More options to set the Digits after the decimal point (20 by default, up to 1,000) and the Last digit: Cut off (exact digits) or Rounded.
  • Use the Copy button to copy the whole result without commas, ready to paste into a spreadsheet or code.

Why ordinary calculators lose digits

Most phone apps, spreadsheets and programming languages store numbers in the IEEE 754 double format, which has room for about 15 to 17 significant decimal digits. Whole numbers are exact only up to 2⁵³ = 9,007,199,254,740,992; one more, 9,007,199,254,740,993, cannot be stored and is silently replaced by its neighbour.

Handheld scientific calculators have similar limits. Casio's manual for the fx-570ES PLUS and fx-991ES PLUS, models used in many schools, gives a calculation range up to ±9.999999999 × 10⁹⁹ with 15 digits used internally, so 70! (about 1.2 × 10¹⁰⁰) already overflows. This page has no such ceiling until an answer reaches about 100,000 digits.

Worked examples

  • Power: 2 to the power 100 has 31 digits, 1,267,650,600,228,229,401,496,703,205,376. Doubling 100 times grows a number by a factor of about 1.27 × 10³⁰.
  • Factorial: 30! = 265,252,859,812,191,058,636,308,480,000,000 (33 digits). 52!, the number of ways to shuffle a deck of cards, has 68 digits, and 1,000! has 2,568.
  • Long division: 1 ÷ 7 to 30 places is 0.142857142857142857142857142857; the six digits 142857 repeat for ever.
  • Square root: √2 to 30 places, cut off, is 1.414213562373095048801688724209.
  • Last digits of a huge power: work out 7 to the power 222 (188 digits), copy it, then find X mod 1000 with Y = 1000. The remainder, 49, gives the last three digits, 049.
  • Exact decimals: 0.1 + 0.2 is exactly 0.3 here, while many programs print 0.30000000000000004.

Names of big numbers: million, billion, lakh and crore

English names go up in steps of a thousand (the short scale used in Pakistan, the United States and the United Kingdom today). In Pakistan and India, lakh and crore are also common, and digits are then grouped as 1,00,000 rather than 100,000.

There is no biggest number: adding 1 to any number makes a bigger one. The name googol for 10¹⁰⁰ came from Milton Sirotta, the young nephew of the American mathematician Edward Kasner, who put it in print in 1938; a googolplex is 10 to the power googol, far too long to write out. Real science also needs big numbers: the Avogadro constant is exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ per mole under the SI definition of 2019.

How to calculate big numbers without a calculator

  • Estimate first with powers of ten: 4,800 × 21,000 ≈ 5 × 10³ × 2 × 10⁴ = 10⁸, so the exact answer, 100,800,000, should have 9 digits.
  • Split one number into easy parts: 498 × 25 = 500 × 25 − 2 × 25 = 12,500 − 50 = 12,450.
  • Check the last digit: the last digit of a product depends only on the last digits of the factors (…7 × …8 ends in 6).
  • For many digits, use long multiplication or long division; the Long Division Calculator shows each step.

How it works

Type X (and Y when the operation needs it) and pick an operation. Numbers can be as long as you like: whole numbers, decimals, or E-notation such as 23E18 for 23 × 10^18. Every result is worked out digit for digit with no rounding inside. When a result never ends, such as 1 ÷ 7 or √2, it is given to the number of digits after the point you choose under More options; by default the extra digits are simply cut off, so every digit shown is correct.

The formula

  • Whole numbers and decimals are stored as exact integers with a decimal scale (1.25 = 125 ÷ 10²)
  • X ÷ Y and X^(−n) are long-divided to the chosen number of decimal places
  • √X = the integer square root of X × 10^(2d), found by Newton's method, then d digits after the point
  • X! = 1 × 2 × … × X; X mod Y = X − Y × floor(X ÷ Y); GCF by Euclid's algorithm, LCM = |X × Y| ÷ GCF

A worked example

2 to the power 100 = 1,267,650,600,228,229,401,496,703,205,376, a 31-digit number that an ordinary calculator would show only as about 1.27 × 10^30. And 0.1 + 0.2 gives exactly 0.3.

Questions

Why are the last digits cut off and not rounded?

Cutting off means every digit you see is a true digit of the answer, which is what you want when checking long expansions such as √2. If you prefer the usual rounding, switch Last digit to Rounded under More options.

How do I type a number like 3.5 × 10^19?

Use E-notation: 3.5e19 or 3.5E19. Negative exponents work too: 2.5e-8 is 0.000000025.

How large can the result be?

Up to about 100,000 digits, which covers 25,000! and 2 to the power 300,000. The Copy button copies the full result without commas.

How does mod work with negative numbers?

The remainder takes the sign of Y: −7 mod 3 = 2, because −7 = 3 × (−3) + 2. This is the floor rule used in most maths texts.

What is the largest number a normal calculator can show?

It depends on the model. Many school scientific calculators stop just under 10¹⁰⁰ (Casio's fx-991ES PLUS manual gives ±9.999999999 × 10⁹⁹), and phone apps lose exact digits after about 15 to 17. This page keeps every digit up to about 100,000 digits.

Can I calculate a factorial of a big number here?

Yes. Choose X! and type a whole number up to 25,000. 100! has 158 digits and 1,000! has 2,568; the result card tells you the digit count and the scientific notation.

Can I type big numbers with commas?

Yes. Commas and spaces are removed before the number is read, so 12,345,678 and 1,00,00,000 both work. The answer is shown with commas, and Copy gives it without them.

How do I convert a big number to lakh or crore?

One lakh is 100,000 and one crore is 10,000,000. Divide by 10,000,000 to get crore: 2,500,000,000 is 250 crore, or 2.5 billion. The table above lists the main steps.

What is the biggest number in the world?

There is none, because any number plus 1 is bigger. Famous large named numbers include the googol (10¹⁰⁰) and the googolplex (10 to the power googol).
