What bandwidth means, and what this bandwidth calculator does

A bandwidth calculator answers practical questions about moving data: how long a download will take, how many megabytes are in a gigabit, and how much bandwidth a website or a set of cameras will use in a month. In networking and on the internet, bandwidth is the most data a connection can carry per second, measured in bits per second (bit/s, often written bps) and its multiples kbit/s, Mbit/s and Gbit/s.

Bandwidth is the size of the pipe, not how full it is. The rate you actually get at a given moment is called throughput, and it is usually lower because of protocol overhead, Wi-Fi interference and other devices sharing the line. Latency (ping) is something else again: the delay before data starts to arrive, measured in milliseconds. A connection can have plenty of bandwidth and still feel slow in games or calls if its latency is high.

The word has other meanings too. In web hosting, bandwidth usually means the data transfer allowed per month, counted in GB or TB rather than in bits per second. In Wi-Fi router settings, channel bandwidth (shown in MHz) is a width of radio frequencies. This page handles the first two; the last section below explains the radio and circuit meaning.

How to use the calculator, tab by tab

  • Download time: type the File size and pick its unit (bytes up to TB, or the binary KiB, MiB, GiB and TiB), then the Connection speed and its unit (bit/s to Tbit/s, or bytes per second if your app shows MB/s). Under More options, Speed lost to overhead takes off a percentage (0% by default, up to 90%) for protocols and other traffic. The same steps work for upload time: use your upload speed instead.
  • Data units: type an Amount of data and pick any of 14 units. You get the amount in all of them at once, from bits to tebibytes.
  • Website bandwidth: enter Page views and choose the period (per second, minute, hour, day, week, month or year), the Average page size with its unit, and a Safety factor for peaks between 1 and 100 (2 by default).
  • Monthly data and speed: under I know, choose The data per month (for a hosting plan's transfer allowance) or The speed (for a connection that runs all the time, such as camera streams or a server uplink), then type the value and its unit.

Results update as you type. Each tab also shows the working, so you can see how the bits, bytes and seconds were multiplied and divided.

Worked examples: downloads, a website and CCTV cameras

Big download. A 6 GB game over a 40 Mbit/s connection is 48,000,000,000 bits ÷ 40,000,000 bit/s = 1,200 seconds, so exactly 20 minutes at full speed. Set 10% overhead and the line delivers 4.5 MB/s instead of 5 MB/s, and the time grows to 22 minutes 13 seconds. The speed table shows that 100 Mbit/s would cut it to 8 minutes and 1 Gbit/s to 48 seconds.

Home line in Pakistan. Sending a 1.2 GB video from Lahore over a 15 Mbit/s connection takes 10 minutes 40 seconds in theory, and 11 minutes 51 seconds with 10% overhead. Many home plans upload much more slowly than they download, so for an upload type your upload speed, not the headline speed of the package.

Website. A blog with 20,000 page views a day and pages of 1.5 MB on average moves 30 GB a day: an average of 2.78 Mbit/s and 913.13 GB a month. With a safety factor of 3 for busy hours and traffic spikes, plan for 8.33 Mbit/s and about 2,739 GB (2.74 TB) a month. Search engine crawlers and other bots also load pages, so count them in your page views if your analytics leave them out.

CCTV. Four cameras that each stream 4 Mbit/s to a recorder or the cloud make a steady 16 Mbit/s. On the Monthly data and speed tab (I know: The speed), that is 172.8 GB per day and 5,259.6 GB (5.26 TB) per month. The per-day figure also tells you roughly how much disk space a week or a month of recording needs at that bitrate; check the real bitrate in your recorder's settings first.

How much bandwidth do you need at home?

Add up what runs at the same time, not everything you own. Streaming services publish their own targets, so they are a sensible starting point. Then add room for phones syncing, game updates and video calls, and remember that Wi-Fi rarely delivers the full speed of the plan.

Bandwidth in hertz: signals, Wi-Fi channels and RLC circuits

In electronics, radio and physics classes, bandwidth is a range of frequencies measured in hertz: the gap between the highest and lowest frequency a signal occupies or a circuit passes, Δf = f₂ − f₁. For a filter or amplifier, f₁ and f₂ are usually the half-power points (3 dB below the peak).

For a series RLC circuit the width of the resonance curve is Δω = R ÷ L in radians per second, which is Δf = R ÷ (2πL) in hertz, and the quality factor is Q = ω₀ ÷ Δω, with ω₀ = 1 ÷ √(LC). A higher Q means a sharper, narrower peak. These formulas describe frequency, not data, so this calculator does not use them; wider radio channels do let a link carry more bits per second, which is how the two meanings connect.

Tips and common mistakes

  • Mixing bits and bytes. Mbps (megabits) is not MB/s (megabytes). Divide a plan's speed by 8 to get the most megabytes per second it can deliver: 100 Mbit/s is 12.5 MB/s.
  • Forgetting overhead. Use 5% to 10% under More options for a realistic estimate on a wired line; Wi-Fi and busy evenings can cost more.
  • Confusing GB and GiB. A file manager that says 4.5 GB may mean 4.5 GiB, which is about 4.83 GB. Pick the unit that matches where you read the size.
  • Planning for the average. Website and office traffic comes in bursts, so use a safety factor of 2 or more and check your hosting plan's limit against the planned figure, not the average.
  • Reading hosting bandwidth as speed. A plan with 1 TB of bandwidth a month limits the total data moved, not the speed of each visit.

How it works

Data sizes are counted in bytes (a file of 2 GB), but connection speeds in bits per second (a 20 Mbit/s plan), and one byte is 8 bits. That factor of 8 is why a 20 Mbit/s line downloads only 2.5 megabytes each second. The first tab turns a file size and a speed into a time, and shows the same file at other common speeds. The second shows one amount of data in every unit. The third works out the bandwidth and the monthly data a website needs from its page views and average page size, with a safety factor for busy hours. The last turns a hosting plan's monthly data into the steady speed it equals, or the other way round.

The formula

  • 1 byte = 8 bits; decimal prefixes: 1 kB = 1,000 B, 1 MB = 1,000,000 B, 1 GB = 10⁹ B, 1 TB = 10¹² B (the same for kb, Mb, Gb, Tb in bits)
  • Binary prefixes: 1 KiB = 1,024 B, 1 MiB = 1,024² B, 1 GiB = 1,024³ B, 1 TiB = 1,024⁴ B
  • Transfer time = data in bits ÷ speed in bits per second (the speed is first reduced by the overhead percentage, if you set one)
  • Website bandwidth (bit/s) = page views per second × page size in bytes × 8; with the safety factor, multiply by it
  • Data per month = bytes per second × 2,629,800 seconds (one month = 365.25 ÷ 12 = 30.4375 days)

A worked example

A 6 GB game download over a 40 Mbit/s line: 6 GB × 8 = 48,000 Mb, and 48,000 Mb ÷ 40 Mbit/s = 1,200 seconds, which is exactly 20 minutes. If 10% of the speed goes to overhead, the line delivers 36 Mbit/s (4.5 MB/s) and the same download takes 22 minutes 13 seconds.

Questions

How do I calculate bandwidth for a download?

Convert the file size to bits (multiply bytes by 8) and divide by the speed in bits per second. A 6 GB file is 48,000 megabits, so at 40 Mbit/s it takes 1,200 seconds, or 20 minutes. The Download time tab does this for you and adds overhead if you set it.

Why is my download slower than my plan's speed?

Plans are sold in megabits per second and file sizes are in megabytes, so divide the speed by 8 first: 100 Mbit/s moves at most 12.5 MB a second. Network protocols, Wi-Fi and other devices on the line also take a share, usually 5% to 10% or more; set it under More options.

What is bandwidth in networking?

It is the most data a link can carry each second, given in bits per second. The speed you actually measure is the throughput, which is usually a little lower, and the delay before data arrives is the latency.

What is bandwidth in hosting?

Hosting companies use bandwidth for the total data their servers may send to your visitors in a month, such as 500 GB or 1 TB. On the Monthly data and speed tab, 500 GB a month equals a steady 1.52 Mbit/s, but real traffic peaks well above the average.

How do I calculate bandwidth usage for a website?

Multiply page views by the average page size to get the data moved, then divide by the time. 20,000 views a day of 1.5 MB is 30 GB a day, or 2.78 Mbit/s on average. Multiply by a safety factor of 2 to 3 for peak hours.

How much bandwidth does a CCTV camera use?

It depends on the resolution, frame rate and compression set in the camera or recorder, so read the bitrate there. Then multiply by the number of cameras: four cameras at 4 Mbit/s each are 16 Mbit/s, which is 172.8 GB a day.

What is the difference between GB and GiB?

A gigabyte (GB) is 1,000,000,000 bytes; a gibibyte (GiB) is 1,073,741,824 bytes (1,024³), about 7.4% more. Drive makers and networks use GB, while many computers show sizes in GiB but label them GB, which is why a new 1 TB drive shows about 931 GB.

How many megabytes are in a megabit?

One megabit is 0.125 megabytes, because a byte has 8 bits. Going the other way, 1 MB is 8 Mb.

How do I calculate bandwidth requirements per user?

Find the heaviest thing each person does at the same time, such as a 5 Mbit/s Full HD stream, multiply by the number of people doing it at once, and add some room. Three Full HD streams need about 15 Mbit/s before any other traffic.

What safety factor should a website use?

Traffic comes in bursts, so plan for more than the average. A factor of 2 is a common starting point; sites with sudden peaks (sales, news, launches) often use 3 to 5.

How is a month counted?

As an average month of 30.4375 days (365.25 days ÷ 12), or 2,629,800 seconds. A 30-day month would give figures about 1.4% lower.
