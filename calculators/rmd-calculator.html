RMDs and balance by year

How it works

Enter your birth year, the year you are taking the distribution for and the account balance on December 31 of the year before. Your age is the age you reach during that year. If that is below your start age (73 if you were born 1951 to 1959, 75 if born in 1960 or later), no RMD is due yet and the page shows the first year one is. Otherwise the balance is divided by the distribution period for your age from the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table. When your spouse is your only beneficiary and more than 10 years younger, the period comes from the Joint and Last Survivor Table instead, which is longer and so gives a smaller RMD. The table then repeats this every year to age 120, taking the RMD at the end of each year and growing the rest at the return you enter.

The formula

  • Age = distribution year − birth year
  • Start age: 73 (born 1951 to 1959), 75 (born 1960 or later)
  • RMD = balance on Dec 31 of the year before ÷ distribution period for your age
  • Distribution period: Uniform Lifetime Table, or the Joint and Last Survivor Table when the sole beneficiary is a spouse more than 10 years younger
  • Projection: next balance = balance × (1 + return) − RMD

A worked example

Born in 1951, you turn 75 in 2026. The Uniform Lifetime Table gives 24.6 for age 75, so a balance of $300,000 on December 31, 2025 means an RMD of 300,000 ÷ 24.6 = $12,195.12 for 2026. If the account grows 5%, it ends 2026 at 300,000 × 1.05 − 12,195.12 = $302,804.88, and the 2027 RMD is 302,804.88 ÷ 23.7 = $12,776.58.

Questions

At what age do RMDs start?

Under the SECURE 2.0 Act, RMDs start in the year you turn 73 if you were born from 1951 to 1959, and in the year you turn 75 if you were born in 1960 or later. People born in 1950 or earlier have already started under the older rules.

When do I have to take the RMD?

By December 31 of each year. The very first one may wait until April 1 of the next year, but then you take two RMDs in that year, which can push you into a higher tax bracket.

When is the joint table used?

Only when your spouse is your sole beneficiary for the whole year and is more than 10 years younger than you. The joint table gives a longer distribution period, so the required amount is smaller. In every other case the Uniform Lifetime Table applies.

What happens if I take less than the RMD?

The shortfall can be charged a 25% excise tax, cut to 10% if you take the missing amount and correct it within about two years. You may always withdraw more than the RMD; the extra does not count toward later years.

Does this work for a Roth IRA or an inherited IRA?

Roth IRAs have no RMDs while the owner is alive. Inherited accounts follow different rules (often a 10-year payout), so this calculator is for your own traditional IRA, SEP, SIMPLE or workplace plan.
