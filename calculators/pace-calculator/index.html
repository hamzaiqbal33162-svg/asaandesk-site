What pace is and what this pace calculator does

In running and walking, pace is the time it takes to cover one unit of distance, usually one kilometre or one mile. A pace of 5:00 per km means each kilometre takes five minutes. Speed is the same information turned around: 5:00 per km is 12 km/h. Runners talk in pace because it maps straight onto race plans and watch splits; treadmills, cyclists and drivers talk in speed.

This pace calculator does four jobs. It works out your pace, your time or your distance from the other two, with race times and kilometre and mile splits. It finds the pace of each part of a run from checkpoint times. It converts between eight pace and speed units. And it predicts a finish time from how far you have come and how long it took. It works for running, walking, hiking, swimming, rowing and cycling, as long as you enter distance and time.

How to use the pace calculator, step by step

  • Pace, time or distance tab: under Work out choose Pace, Time or Distance, then fill in the other two boxes.
  • For a race, pick it from Race or distance (5K, 10K, half marathon, marathon, 1K, 1 mile, 5 miles, 10 miles, 800 m or 1500 m), or choose Type a distance and enter it in km, miles, metres or yards.
  • Enter times in the hours, minutes and seconds boxes; leave hours at 0 for anything under an hour.
  • Choose the Pace unit: per mile, per km, mph, km/h, metres per minute or second, or yards per minute or second.
  • Checkpoint splits tab: enter the total distance and total time from the start at each point, the way a watch shows them. Points 6 to 12 are under a collapsed section.
  • Pace converter tab: pick the From unit, type the pace or speed, and pick the To unit.
  • Finish time tab: enter the distance and time so far and the race or full distance.

The pace formula and pace conversions

The pace formula is simply time ÷ distance. Turn the time into seconds, divide by the distance, then turn the seconds back into minutes and seconds. For 10 km in 52:30: 3,150 seconds ÷ 10 = 315 seconds, which is 5:15 per km.

To go from pace to speed, divide 60 by the pace in minutes: 5:00 per km is 60 ÷ 5 = 12 km/h. To go from km to miles, multiply the pace per km by 1.609344, because a mile is 1.609344 km exactly: 5:00 per km × 1.609344 = 8:02.8 per mile.

Worked examples

Finding pace. You run 5 km in 25:00. 1,500 seconds ÷ 5 = 300 seconds, so your pace is 5:00 per km (8:03 per mile) and your speed 12 km/h. At that pace a 10K takes 50:00 and a half marathon 1:45:29.

Finding time. You want to know how long a marathon takes at 5:41 per km: 341 seconds × 42.195 = 14,388 seconds, just under 4 hours (3:59:48).

Finding distance. A walker in Karachi keeps a brisk pace of 10:00 per km for an hour each evening: 3,600 ÷ 600 = 6 km, about 3.7 miles, which also counts towards the WHO target of 150 minutes of moderate activity a week.

Splits. In the default checkpoint run the kilometres took 4:55, 5:03, 5:04, 4:59 and 4:49, an average of 4:58 per km. Finishing faster than you started, a negative split, is a sign of good pacing.

Finish time. At halfway through a race you have done 10 km in 52:30 (5:15 per km). Keeping that pace for the half marathon gives 3,150 × 21.0975 ÷ 10 = 6,646 seconds, or 1:50:46.

Swimming and rowing. Set the distance unit to metres. 1,500 m in 30:00 is 50 m per minute, which is 2:00 per 100 m. A rower's pace is usually given per 500 m: 2:00 per 500 m is 250 m per minute or 4.17 m/s, which you can type into the converter.

Training with pace and heart rate

Pace tells you what you did; effort tells you what it cost. Most training plans mix easy runs, where you can hold a conversation, with a smaller share of harder tempo runs and intervals. The CDC's talk test is a handy guide: during moderate activity you can talk but not sing, and during vigorous activity you cannot say more than a few words without pausing for breath.

Heart rate adds a number to that feeling. The old rule of 220 minus age is still common, but Tanaka, Monahan and Seals (2001) found 208 − 0.7 × age a better estimate of maximum heart rate in healthy adults; at 35 that is 183.5 beats a minute rather than 185. Harder intervals are a strong stimulus: in a study by Helgerud and colleagues (2007), four 4-minute intervals three times a week raised VO₂max by 7.2% in 8 weeks, more than steady running of the same total work.

WHO recommends that adults do 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity a week. For most people, a run at an easy pace counts as vigorous and a brisk walk as moderate.

Tips and common pacing mistakes

  • Starting too fast. Adrenaline makes the first kilometre feel easy; aim to run it no faster than your goal pace.
  • Trusting GPS blindly. Watches can drift in cities, under trees and on tracks; certified race courses are measured, so race splits are more reliable.
  • Ignoring hills, heat and wind. The calculator assumes even conditions, so plan a slower pace on hot, humid days and on hilly routes rather than chasing a flat-road time.
  • Mixing units. Check that your goal pace and the course distance are both in km or both in miles, or use the converter.
  • Reading the finish predictor as a promise. It assumes you hold your pace to the end; most runners slow in the second half of a long race, so treat it as the best case.
  • Forgetting easy days. Running every session at race pace raises injury risk and slows progress.

How it works

Pick what you want to work out, then fill in the other two. Pace is the time you need for one mile or one kilometre; speed is the same thing turned around (distance per hour, minute or second). From your pace the calculator lists the time for common race distances and the time at every whole kilometre and mile. The Checkpoint splits tab takes the total distance and total time at up to 12 points along a run and shows how fast you went between them. The converter turns any pace or speed into the other units, and the Finish time tab assumes you keep your pace so far to the end.

The formula

  • Pace (seconds per unit) = time in seconds ÷ distance
  • Time = distance × pace; distance = time ÷ pace (or speed × time)
  • Speed = distance ÷ time, e.g. miles per hour = miles ÷ hours
  • km/h = 60 ÷ pace in minutes per km; pace per mile = pace per km × 1.609344
  • 1 mile = 1.609344 km, 1 yard = 0.9144 m; marathon = 42.195 km, half marathon = 21.0975 km
  • Pace of one part of a run = (time at this checkpoint − time at the last one) ÷ (distance here − distance at the last one)
  • Finish time = time so far × full distance ÷ distance so far

A worked example

5 miles in 50:25 is 3,025 seconds ÷ 5 = 605 seconds, or 10:05 per mile. One mile is 1.609344 km, so the same pace is 605 ÷ 1.609344 = 375.93 seconds, or 6:15.93 per km, and a 10K at that pace takes 10 × 375.93 = 3,759 seconds = 1:02:39.

Questions

How do I calculate pace?

Divide your time by your distance. Change the time to seconds first: 25 minutes is 1,500 seconds, and 1,500 ÷ 5 km = 300 seconds, or 5:00 per km. This calculator does it for you and shows the pace in eight units.

How do I calculate pace per km from a time in minutes?

Divide the minutes by the kilometres. 52.5 minutes for 10 km is 5.25 minutes per km; the 0.25 of a minute is 15 seconds, so the pace is 5:15 per km.

What is the difference between pace and speed?

Pace is time per distance, like 5:30 per km. Speed is distance per time, like 10.9 km/h. They describe the same effort: a faster pace is a smaller number, a faster speed is a bigger one.

How do I convert km/h to pace?

Divide 60 by the speed to get minutes per km. 12 km/h is 60 ÷ 12 = 5 minutes per km, and 10 km/h is 6:00 per km. The Pace converter tab does any conversion.

What pace do I need for a sub-4-hour marathon?

About 5:41 per km or 9:09 per mile, held for the full 42.195 km. The same pace gives a half marathon just under 2 hours.

What is a good pace for a 5K?

It depends on age, training and the course. As a guide, 6:00 per km gives a 30-minute 5K and 5:00 per km gives 25 minutes; enter your own time to see your pace and what it means for longer races.

Can I use this pace calculator for walking or swimming?

Yes. For walking use km or miles as usual; 10:00 per km is a brisk walk of 6 km/h. For swimming set the distance in metres or yards and read metres per minute, then work out the time per 100 m.

How do I type a time?

Use the hours, minutes and seconds boxes. Leave hours at 0 for anything under an hour. Decimals are fine in the seconds box.

How do I find the pace for each lap or section?

Open Checkpoint splits and enter the total distance and total time at each point, the way a watch shows them. The table shows the pace between points and your average so far.

Is the finish time prediction reliable?

It assumes you hold your current pace to the end. Most people slow down in the second half of a long race, so treat it as the best case.
