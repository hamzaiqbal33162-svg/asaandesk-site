RMDs and balance by year

What is an RMD?

A required minimum distribution (RMD) is the smallest amount the IRS makes you withdraw each year from a tax-deferred retirement account once you reach a certain age. It applies to traditional IRAs, SEP and SIMPLE IRAs, and workplace plans such as 401(k), 403(b) and 457(b) plans. The rule exists because the money went in, or grew, without tax, and the government wants it taxed eventually.

This RMD calculator works out the amount for 2024 to 2027 from your birth year and your account balance, using the IRS life expectancy tables in force since 2022 and the start ages set by the SECURE 2.0 Act. It also projects your RMDs and balance for every later year, so you can see how the required withdrawals grow as a share of the account.

The RMD is a minimum. You may always take more, and the extra is taxed the same way, but it does not reduce what you must take in later years.

How to use the RMD calculator

  • Enter Your birth year (default 1950).
  • Pick the Year of the distribution: 2024, 2025, 2026 (default) or 2027.
  • Enter the Account balance on Dec 31 of the year before, from your year-end statement (default 300,000). For a 2026 RMD, use the balance on December 31, 2025.
  • Tick My spouse is my only beneficiary if that is true for the whole year, and enter the Spouse's birth year (default 1962). The joint table is used only when the spouse is more than 10 years younger.
  • Open Projection to set the Yearly return on the account (default 5%), which only affects the future years in the table.

Work out each account separately: run the calculator once per IRA or plan with that account's balance. The rules for combining them are explained below.

Understanding your RMD result

The headline is Your RMD for the year, shown as the balance divided by the distribution period. The rows give the Distribution period and the table it came from, Your age in that year and the Share of the balance the RMD takes. With the spouse option ticked, your spouse's age is shown too.

If you are below your start age, the RMD is 0 and the rows show the First RMD year and the RMD start age for your birth year. Notes under the result give your deadline, the first-year April 1 option when it applies, and which rules covered earlier years if you were born in 1950 or before.

The table RMDs and balance by year repeats the calculation to age 120. Each year the RMD is taken at the end of the year and the rest grows at your chosen return, and the chart shows the balance and the RMD by age.

Worked examples for 2026 and 2027

The default case. Born in 1950, you are 76 in 2026. With 300,000 in the account on December 31, 2025, the period is 23.7 and the 2026 RMD is 300,000 ÷ 23.7 = 12,658.23, or 4.22% of the balance, due by December 31, 2026.

A first RMD at age 73. Born in 1953, you turn 73 in 2026. The period is 26.5, so the RMD on 300,000 is 11,320.75. Because it is your first RMD year, you may delay it until April 1, 2027, but then you take two RMDs in 2027, which adds both to that year's taxable income.

Looking ahead to 2027. Born in 1954, you turn 73 in 2027. With 500,000 on December 31, 2026, the 2027 RMD is 500,000 ÷ 26.5 = 18,867.92.

A younger spouse as sole beneficiary. Born in 1950 with a spouse born in 1962, who is 12 years younger, the joint table gives a period of 25.2 instead of 23.7, and the 2026 RMD on 300,000 falls to 11,904.76.

Not yet. Born in 1960, you are 66 in 2026. Your start age is 75, so your first RMD year is 2035 and nothing is required until then.

RMD age and deadlines

The age at which RMDs start has been raised twice since 2019. The calculator uses the rule for your birth year.

RMD start age by birth date
BornRMD start ageRule
Before July 1, 194970½The original rule
July 1, 1949 to 195072SECURE Act of 2019
1951 to 195973SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022
1960 or later75SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022

Your first RMD is due by April 1 of the year after you reach your start age; every later RMD is due by December 31. If you still work for the employer that sponsors your 401(k) or similar plan, you can usually delay that plan's RMDs until you retire, unless you own 5% or more of the business. That exception does not apply to IRAs.

Which accounts need RMDs and how to take them

  • Traditional, SEP and SIMPLE IRAs: work out the RMD for each IRA, add them up, and take the total from any one or more of your IRAs.
  • 403(b) accounts: the same, you may add them up and take the total from one or more 403(b) accounts.
  • 401(k) and other employer plans: each plan's RMD must be taken from that plan.
  • Roth IRAs and Roth accounts in workplace plans: no withdrawals are required while the owner is alive; beneficiaries do have RMDs.
  • An RMD cannot be rolled over into another tax-deferred account, and it is taxed as ordinary income at your tax rate, apart from any after-tax basis.
  • You can ask the account provider to withhold income tax from the RMD so you do not face a large bill at tax time.

RMDs for an inherited IRA

This calculator is for your own account. Inherited IRAs follow separate rules. Under the SECURE Act, most beneficiaries of an owner who died after 2019 must empty the whole account within 10 years, and the IRS says this applies whether the owner died before or after their required beginning date.

Eligible designated beneficiaries can stretch withdrawals over their own life expectancy instead: a surviving spouse, a child who has not reached the age of majority, a disabled or chronically ill person, and anyone not more than 10 years younger than the owner. Beneficiaries use the Single Life Expectancy Table in Publication 590-B. Because these rules are complex and depend on the year of death, check with the account provider or a tax adviser.

Missed RMDs and tips

  • A missed or short RMD can be charged a 25% excise tax on the amount not taken, reduced to 10% if you correct it within two years.
  • Use the right balance: December 31 of the previous year, not today's value.
  • Plan the first year. Taking the first RMD by December 31 instead of waiting until April 1 avoids two taxable RMDs in one year.
  • Spreading withdrawals across the year, or taking them early, does not change the amount due.
  • If you do not need the money, you can reinvest it in a taxable account; you just cannot put it back into the IRA or plan.
  • Compare with your provider. Many IRA providers send an RMD figure each year; if theirs differs from this one, check which year-end balance and which table each used.
  • With several IRAs, choose which one to draw the combined total from, for example the account holding the cash or investments you would most like to sell, so you avoid selling elsewhere at a bad time.

How it works

Enter your birth year, the year you are taking the distribution for and the account balance on December 31 of the year before. Your age is the age you reach during that year. If that is below your start age (73 if you were born 1951 to 1959, 75 if born in 1960 or later), no RMD is due yet and the page shows the first year one is. Otherwise the balance is divided by the distribution period for your age from the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table. When your spouse is your only beneficiary and more than 10 years younger, the period comes from the Joint and Last Survivor Table instead, which is longer and so gives a smaller RMD. The table then repeats this every year to age 120, withdrawing each year's RMD on December 31 and growing what remains at the return you enter under Projection.

The formula

  • Age = distribution year − birth year
  • Start age: 73 (born 1951 to 1959), 75 (born 1960 or later)
  • RMD = balance on Dec 31 of the year before ÷ distribution period for your age
  • Distribution period: Uniform Lifetime Table, or the Joint and Last Survivor Table when the sole beneficiary is a spouse more than 10 years younger
  • Projection: next balance = balance × (1 + return) − RMD

A worked example

Born in 1951, you turn 75 in 2026. The Uniform Lifetime Table gives 24.6 for age 75, so a balance of $300,000 on December 31, 2025 means an RMD of 300,000 ÷ 24.6 = $12,195.12 for 2026. If the account grows 5%, it ends 2026 at 300,000 × 1.05 − 12,195.12 = $302,804.88, and the 2027 RMD is 302,804.88 ÷ 23.7 = $12,776.58.

Questions

How do I calculate my RMD?

Divide the account balance on December 31 of last year by the distribution period for the age you reach this year, from the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table. For example, 300,000 at age 76 is 300,000 ÷ 23.7 = 12,658.23. The calculator looks up the period for you.

How do I calculate my RMD at age 73?

Use a distribution period of 26.5. A 200,000 balance gives 200,000 ÷ 26.5 = 7,547.17; a 300,000 balance gives 11,320.75. Because age 73 is usually your first RMD year, you may wait until April 1 of the next year to take it.

What is the RMD percentage at age 73?

About 3.77% of the previous year-end balance, which is 1 ÷ 26.5. The share rises every year: about 4.07% at 75, 4.95% at 80 and 8.20% at 90.

What is the RMD age in 2026?

In 2026 the start age is 73 for anyone born 1951 to 1959, so people born in 1953 take a first RMD for 2026. People born in 1960 or later start at 75, which means 2035 at the earliest.

How do I calculate the RMD for my 401(k)?

The same way as an IRA: the plan balance on December 31 of last year divided by your distribution period. Each 401(k) must pay its own RMD, and if you still work for the employer and own less than 5% of it, you can usually wait until you retire.

When do I have to take the RMD?

By December 31 of each year. The very first one may wait until April 1 of the next year, but then you take two RMDs in that year, which can push you into a higher tax bracket.

When is the joint table used?

Only when your spouse is your sole beneficiary for the whole year and is more than 10 years younger than you. The joint table gives a longer distribution period, so the required amount is smaller. In every other case the Uniform Lifetime Table applies.

What happens if I take less than the RMD?

The shortfall can be charged a 25% excise tax, cut to 10% if you take the missing amount and correct it within two years. You may always withdraw more than the RMD; the extra does not count toward later years.

How do I calculate the RMD for an inherited IRA?

This calculator does not cover inherited accounts. Most beneficiaries of owners who died after 2019 must empty the account within 10 years, while a spouse, a minor child, a disabled or chronically ill person, or someone not more than 10 years younger can use the Single Life Expectancy Table instead.

Are RMDs taxed?

Yes. The IRS taxes an RMD as ordinary income at your tax rate, apart from any after-tax money (basis) in the account. Qualified Roth withdrawals are tax-free, and Roth IRAs have no RMDs while you are alive.

Can I take my RMD from just one IRA?

Yes, for IRAs. Work out the RMD for each IRA, add them up, and withdraw the total from any one or more of them. 403(b) accounts can be combined the same way, but 401(k) and other plans must each pay their own RMD.

Does this work for a Roth IRA?

Roth IRAs have no RMDs while the owner is alive, so there is nothing to calculate. Use this calculator for your own traditional IRA, SEP, SIMPLE or workplace plan.
