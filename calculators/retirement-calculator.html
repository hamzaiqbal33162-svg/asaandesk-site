What a retirement calculator tells you

A retirement calculator answers one question in several forms: will the money you save be enough for the years after you stop working? This one has four planners in tabs. Am I on track? compares what you need on your retirement day with what your saving plan reaches. How much to save finds the saving that hits a target. How much can I spend turns savings into a monthly budget, and How long will it last counts the months a pot of money pays out.

Each planner grows your money at one steady yearly return and, where it matters, lets your spending rise with inflation. All amounts are before tax and no account limits apply, so the result is a planning estimate for any country and any kind of savings: a 401(k), an IRA, a provident or pension fund, bank deposits or a mix.

Results are shown in the money of the year they happen and, next to them, in today's money. Judge every result in today's money: two million dollars in 30 years buys far less than two million dollars buys now.

How to use the retirement calculator, step by step

  • Choose a tab: Am I on track?, How much to save, How much can I spend or How long will it last. Pick your currency, such as US dollar or Pakistani rupee.
  • Enter Your age and Retirement age. On the on-track and spending tabs also enter Plan to age, the age the money must last to. Planning past the average lifespan is the safer choice.
  • On Am I on track?, enter Yearly income before tax, Retirement savings today and You save each year, either as a % of your pay (it then rises with your pay) or as a fixed amount a year.
  • Enter the Yearly investment return you expect after fees.
  • Open Assumptions to set Yearly inflation (3% by default), Income you want in retirement (75% of your pay just before you retire by default, or an amount a year or a month in today's money), Yearly pay rise, and Other income in retirement, per month, such as Social Security or a pension.
  • On How much to save, enter the Amount you want at retirement. On How much can I spend, enter what you will keep adding: Added every month and Added every year. On How long will it last, enter Money you have and Monthly withdrawal.

Understanding the results

The first tab shows two headline figures: You need at your retirement age and Your plan gives at that age, followed by the share of the need your plan covers. If you fall short, it gives four ways to close the gap: save a fixed amount every month, a fixed amount every year, a share of your pay every year, or add one lump sum today. Each of these replaces your current saving plan rather than adding to it.

On How much can I spend, the rising budget keeps its buying power to the end, while the fixed amount starts higher but buys less every year. With the defaults, $3,768 a month that rises with inflation compares with a fixed $4,970, which is worth only $978 in today's money by age 90.

Worked example: are you on track at 35?

Take the defaults: age 35, retiring at 67 and planning to age 90, pay of $70,000 that rises 3% a year, $30,000 saved, 10% of pay saved each year, a 6% return, 3% inflation and an income goal of 75% of final pay.

Your pay at 67 is about $180,258, so the goal is $11,266 a month, or $4,375 in today's money. Paying that from 67 to 90, rising 3% a year, takes $2,253,445 on your retirement day. Saving 10% of pay reaches $1,098,539: 49% of the need, enough for about $5,492 a month ($2,133 in today's money).

To close the gap you would save $1,839 a month or $22,663 a year instead, which is 22.76% of your pay, or add $319,188 once today. Now add Social Security. If your estimate is about $2,000 a month in today's dollars, that is about $5,150 in the money of age 67 at 3% inflation. Entered as other income, it cuts the need to $1,223,332, and the same plan covers 90% of it.

The last tab answers the drawdown question. With $600,000, a 6% return and $4,000 taken at the end of each month, the money lasts 22 years and 5.7 months. Any withdrawal up to $2,921 a month, the monthly return at the start, would never touch the $600,000 itself.

How much do you need to retire? Rules of thumb and the 4% rule

The US Department of Labor notes that experts estimate you will need 70 to 80 percent of your pre-retirement income to keep your standard of living, which is why the calculator starts at 75%. Some costs stop when work and saving stop, but health costs tend to rise, so test a higher figure as well.

The 4% rule comes from a 1994 study by financial planner William Bengen in the Journal of Financial Planning. Using past US market returns, he found that taking about 4% of savings in the first year and raising that amount with inflation each year would have lasted around 30 years, even for someone who retired just before the weakest stretch in his data. Put the other way round, it suggests a nest egg of about 25 times your first year of spending from savings.

The 4% rule's 300,000 for every 1,000 a month sits at a 0% real return over 25 years, or a 2% real return over 35 years. It is cautious on purpose: markets do not deliver a steady return, and a crash in the first years of retirement, while you are already withdrawing, does more harm than the same crash later. The calculator assumes a steady return, so keep some margin above its answer.

Inflation, returns, longevity and other risks

  • Inflation: at 3% a year prices roughly double in 24 years, so an income that keeps its buying power must grow every year. That is why the need on the first tab is much larger than a fixed income would require.
  • Return: one percentage point more or less each year changes a 30-year result a lot. Use a return after fees, and think about a lower one if you will hold more bonds and cash after you retire.
  • Longevity: in the Social Security Administration's period life table (2023 death rates), a man of 65 can expect about 18.1 more years and a woman about 20.7 more years. Those are averages, and many people live well past them, so planning to 90 or 95 is common caution.
  • Sequence of returns: losses early in retirement hurt more than the same losses later, because you sell investments to pay for living costs while prices are low.
  • Taxes: the calculator works before tax. If your withdrawals will be taxed, the amount you can spend is lower, so raise the income goal to cover the tax.
  • Health and care costs tend to rise with age; a separate reserve for them makes any plan sturdier.

Social Security, 401(k) and IRA rules in the US (2026)

For people born in 1960 or later, the full Social Security retirement age is 67, which is why the calculator's default retirement age is 67. You can start benefits at 62, but the monthly amount can be up to 30% lower; waiting past full retirement age raises it, by as much as 8% a year until age 70. The Social Security Calculator compares claiming ages.

Enter your benefit as Other income in retirement, per month, in the money of the year you retire. If your estimate is in today's dollars, multiply it by (1 + inflation)^(years to retirement): $2,000 today becomes about $5,150 after 32 years at 3%.

Contribution limits set how fast you can save in tax-advantaged accounts. Required minimum distributions from most retirement accounts start in the year you turn 73. Limits change every year, so check the IRS before you rely on them.

Retirement planning in Pakistan: retirement age, EOBI and VPS

Under section 13 of the Civil Servants Act 1973, a federal civil servant retires on completing the sixtieth year of age, unless the competent authority retires them earlier after 20 years of qualifying service. Provincial governments and private employers set their own rules, so check yours. To find a retirement date, add the retirement age to your date of birth with the Date Calculator.

Private-sector workers insured with the Employees' Old-Age Benefits Institution (EOBI) qualify for an old-age pension at 60 for men and 55 for women once contributions have been paid for at least 15 years, under the EOBI Act 1976. Enter the pension you expect as Other income in retirement and save on top of it.

The Voluntary Pension System lets you build savings in approved pension funds under rules set by the Securities and Exchange Commission of Pakistan (SECP). Under section 63 of the Income Tax Ordinance 2001, a salaried or business taxpayer gets a tax credit for contributions up to 20% of taxable income, at their average rate of tax. Each Finance Act can change this, so read the current text on the FBR website.

Rupee plans need realistic rates. The Pakistan Bureau of Statistics puts average consumer price inflation at 29.18% in fiscal year 2022-23 and 23.41% in 2023-24, then 4.49% in 2024-25 and 7.05% in 2025-26. The gap between your return and inflation drives the result, so test several pairs.

Example in rupees: a 30-year-old earns Rs 2,400,000 a year, has Rs 500,000 saved, saves 10% of pay, retires at 60, plans to 85 and wants 70% of final pay. With an assumed 12% return, 8% inflation and 8% pay rises, the need at 60 is Rs 268,902,842, which pays Rs 1,408,772 a month, or Rs 140,000 in today's money. The plan reaches Rs 134,363,552, half of it; closing the gap takes Rs 83,198 a month, or 21.27% of pay each year. The huge rupee figures come from 30 years of 8% inflation, which multiplies prices about ten times, so the today's-money figure is the real goal.

Tips and common mistakes

  • Plan to a late age. Running out of money at 85 is far worse than leaving some at 95.
  • Judge results in today's money; future amounts always look bigger than they are.
  • Do not count the same money twice: a pension or Social Security goes in Other income in retirement, not in Retirement savings today.
  • For a couple, enter your combined pay, savings and yearly saving, and plan to the age the longer-lived partner may reach.
  • The suggested saving amounts replace your current plan. A suggestion of $1,839 a month includes nothing from your 10% plan.
  • Be careful with the fixed budget on the spending tab: it looks generous at first but loses buying power every year.
  • Rerun the plan every year, or after a big change in pay, savings or markets.

How it works

Pick the question you want answered. Am I on track takes your pay, savings and yearly saving, grows them to your retirement age, and works out how much you need on that day to pay the income you want every month until the age you plan to, with that income rising with inflation. If your plan falls short, it shows what to save instead: a monthly or yearly amount, a share of your pay or one lump sum now. How much to save finds the saving that reaches a target amount. How much can I spend turns your savings and regular deposits into a monthly budget for retirement, either rising with inflation or fixed. How long will it last counts the months a pot of money covers a monthly withdrawal.

The formula

  • Monthly rate from a yearly return R: r = (1 + R)^(1/12) − 1; years to retirement n, years in retirement Y
  • Saving phase: balance ← balance × (1 + R) + deposit, deposits at the end of each year (a % of pay rises with your pay)
  • Spending that rises with inflation i is valued at the real return x = R − i: F = (1 − (1 + x)^−Y) ÷ ((1 + x)^(1/12) − 1); amount needed = monthly income from savings × F
  • Saving to close a gap S: monthly = S × r ÷ ((1 + R)^n − 1); yearly = S × R ÷ ((1 + R)^n − 1); % of pay rising g = S × (R − g) ÷ ((1 + R)^n − (1 + g)^n) ÷ pay; lump sum now = S ÷ (1 + R)^n
  • Fixed monthly spending = balance × r ÷ (1 − (1 + R)^−Y); months a pot lasts = −ln(1 − balance × r ÷ withdrawal) ÷ ln(1 + r)

A worked example

At 35, earning $70,000 that rises 3% a year, your pay at 67 is about $180,258, and 75% of it is $11,266 a month. With a 6% return and 3% inflation the real return is 3%, so paying that income from 67 to 90 takes 11,266 × 200.0 = $2,253,445. Saving 10% of pay on top of $30,000 reaches $1,098,539, or 49% of it. Saving $1,839 a month instead (or 22.76% of pay) would close the gap.

Questions

How much do I need to retire?

Enough to pay the income you want, minus any pension or Social Security, every month from your retirement age to the age you plan to, rising with inflation. This amount is often called your nest egg or retirement corpus. A common starting point is 70% to 80% of your pay just before retiring; the calculator turns that into one amount for your retirement day.

What is the formula for a retirement calculator?

The amount needed is the monthly income from savings × F, where F = (1 − (1 + x)^−Y) ÷ ((1 + x)^(1/12) − 1), x is the return minus inflation and Y the years in retirement. Your plan is your savings grown each year at the return, plus each year's saving. The gap between the two sets the extra saving.

How do I calculate retirement income from my savings?

Use the How much can I spend tab: it turns your savings into a monthly budget that rises with inflation, or a fixed one, to the age you plan to. For a quick check, 4% of savings a year, divided by 12, is the starting withdrawal the 4% rule suggests.

What is the retirement age in the USA for Social Security?

For anyone born in 1960 or later the full retirement age is 67. You can claim from 62 with a smaller monthly benefit, up to 30% less, or wait until 70 for a larger one.

What is the retirement age in Pakistan?

Federal civil servants retire on completing 60 years of age under the Civil Servants Act 1973, and provinces and private employers set their own rules. For the EOBI old-age pension, the age is 60 for men and 55 for women, with at least 15 years of contributions.

How do I calculate my retirement date from my date of birth?

Add your retirement age to your date of birth: someone born on 14 August 1970 completes 60 years on 14 August 2030. The Date Calculator adds years to any date; check your employer's rules for the exact last working day.

Can I use this retirement calculator for couples?

Yes, as one household, such as a married couple. Enter your combined pay, savings and yearly saving, the income you want together, and plan to the age the younger or longer-lived partner may reach. For different retirement dates, run it once for each person's share.

How do I add a pension and Social Security?

Open Assumptions and enter the monthly amount in Other income in retirement, per month, in the money of the year you retire. The calculator takes it off the income your savings must pay and shows it as its own column in the income table.

Why does inflation matter so much?

Prices roughly double every 24 years at 3% inflation. An income that keeps its buying power has to grow every year, so it costs much more than a fixed amount. That is why the results also show each figure in today's money.

What return should I use?

A long-run return of 5% to 7% a year is often used for a mix of stocks and bonds before inflation; use less if your money is mostly in cash or bonds, and remember fees lower it. Try a few values to see how sensitive your plan is.

How long will my savings last?

Open the last tab, enter the amount you have, the monthly withdrawal and the return. If the withdrawal is no more than what the money earns each month, it lasts forever; otherwise you see the years and months, plus the monthly amounts that would last exactly 5 to 35 years.

Does it include tax or account limits?

No. All amounts are before tax and no account limits apply, so it works for any country and any mix of accounts. For US account rules, see the 401(k), IRA and Social Security calculators.
