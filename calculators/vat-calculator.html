What is VAT, and what does this VAT calculator do?

VAT, value added tax, is a tax on spending that is charged as a percentage of the price at each stage of a sale. Businesses add it to their prices, pay it over to the tax office, and claim back the VAT they paid on their own purchases, so in the end the full tax is carried by the final buyer. Many countries call the same kind of tax GST (goods and services tax), and Pakistan calls it sales tax.

This VAT calculator adds VAT to a price, removes VAT from a price that already includes it, or works out the missing figures from any two of the rate, the net price, the gross price and the VAT amount. It shows the working step by step, has the standard rates of many countries ready to pick, and works in pounds, euros, rupees, dirhams, riyals, dollars and more.

How to use the VAT calculator

  • Add VAT tab: type the Price before VAT (net), then choose a country under VAT rate, or I will type the rate and type it under Rate.
  • Remove VAT tab: type the Price with VAT included (gross) and pick or type the rate. This is the tab for working backwards from a total.
  • Any two values tab: fill in any two of VAT rate, Net price (without VAT), Gross price (with VAT) and VAT amount, and leave the others empty. It finds the other two, including the rate from a receipt.
  • Pick the currency at the top; the sums are the same in every currency.
  • Read the result and the steps underneath, which show each multiplication or division.

If more than two boxes are filled on the Any two values tab, the calculator uses the first matching pair (rate and net price come first) and warns you when the extra box does not agree. Impossible entries, such as a gross price lower than the net price, get a message instead of a negative result.

Understanding the results

The figures you did not type appear at the top, and the ones you did type are repeated below them, so on the Add VAT tab you see the Price with VAT and the VAT first, and on Remove VAT the Price without VAT and the VAT. When you pick a country, its name is shown under the VAT rate.

Every amount is rounded to the cent on its own. Once in a while the rounded net price and VAT add up to one cent more or less than the gross price; a note tells you when that happens. On an invoice, the usual practice is to round the VAT and work out the other figure from it. HMRC's VAT guide (Notice 700) explains the rounding rules for UK invoices.

VAT formulas: inclusive, exclusive and backwards

VAT exclusive means the price before VAT; VAT inclusive means the price with VAT. To go from exclusive to inclusive, multiply by (1 + rate): at 20%, × 1.20. To go back from inclusive to exclusive, divide by the same factor: ÷ 1.20. To find only the VAT inside an inclusive price, multiply it by rate ÷ (100 + rate): at 20% that is 20/120, or one sixth; at 5% it is 5/105, or one twenty-first; at 15% it is 15/115.

In Excel or Google Sheets, with the net price in A2 and the rate in B2 (as 20%), =A2*(1+B2) gives the gross price and =A2*B2 the VAT. With a gross price in A2, =A2/(1+B2) gives the net price and =A2-A2/(1+B2) the VAT. Wrap any of them in =ROUND(…, 2) to round to the cent.

Worked examples in different countries

  • UAE: adding 5% VAT to AED 2,500 gives AED 125 of VAT and a total of AED 2,625. The UAE Federal Tax Authority lists the standard rate as 5%.
  • Pakistan: a price of Rs 59,000 that includes 18% sales tax is Rs 59,000 ÷ 1.18 = Rs 50,000 before tax, with Rs 9,000 of tax.
  • United Kingdom: £49.99 including 20% VAT is £41.66 before VAT and £8.33 of VAT.
  • South Africa: R1,150 including 15% VAT is R1,000 before VAT and R150 of VAT. The currency list has no rand symbol, but the numbers are the same with any symbol.
  • Finding the rate from a receipt: a total of €61.00 with €10.59 of VAT means a net price of €50.41, and €10.59 ÷ €50.41 × 100 = 21.01%, which is Spain's or the Netherlands' 21% standard rate after rounding.

How VAT works along the supply chain

VAT is collected in small pieces at every stage, but only on the value each business adds. Take a 10% rate. A mill sells flour to a bakery for 1,000 plus 100 of VAT and pays the 100 to the tax office. The bakery sells bread to a shop for 2,500 plus 250 of VAT; it claims back the 100 it paid and pays over 150. The shop sells the bread to customers for 4,000 plus 400 of VAT, claims back 250 and pays over 150.

The tax office receives 100 + 150 + 150 = 400 in total, exactly 10% of the final price of 4,000, and that 400 is what the final customers paid. This is the difference from a retail sales tax, which is charged only once, on the final sale.

VAT rates, registration and VAT vs sales tax

Rates change, so treat the list in the calculator as a starting point and check your tax office. In the UK, GOV.UK lists a standard rate of 20% for most goods and services, a reduced rate of 5% for things such as children's car seats and home energy, and a zero rate for most food and children's clothes; some supplies, such as most financial services, are exempt. Businesses must register for UK VAT when their taxable turnover for the last 12 months goes over £90,000.

In the EU, the VAT Directive requires a standard rate of at least 15%, with no maximum, so each member state sets its own; the calculator's list includes rates such as 19% in Germany, 20% in France, 21% in Spain, 22% in Italy and 23% in Ireland and Portugal. In South Africa, SARS charges 15% and raised the compulsory registration threshold from R1 million to R2.3 million from 1 April 2026; a planned rise in the rate to 15.5% and 16% was reversed. Pakistan's standard sales tax rate on goods is 18%, administered by the Federal Board of Revenue.

VAT and US sales tax both add a percentage to the price, so the sums in this calculator work for either. The difference is in how they are collected: VAT at every stage with credits for tax already paid, a US sales tax once at the final sale, at rates set by each state and city. For US prices, the sales tax calculator has state and local rates.

Common VAT mistakes, and related calculators

  • Taking the rate straight off a gross price. Removing 20% from £120 gives £96, not the correct £100.
  • Using the standard rate for goods that are reduced-rate, zero-rated or exempt.
  • Rounding each line differently from the invoice total; follow your tax office's rounding rules.

How it works

VAT is a percent of the price before tax, the net price. Adding it gives the gross price that the buyer pays. To take VAT out of a gross price you cannot simply subtract the rate: you divide by 1 plus the rate, because the VAT was worked out on the smaller net price. The Add VAT and Remove VAT tabs cover the everyday cases, with standard rates for many countries. The Any two values tab works out the rest from any two of the rate, net price, gross price and VAT amount, for example the rate from a receipt that shows the total and the VAT.

The formula

  • Add VAT: VAT = net price × rate ÷ 100; gross price = net price + VAT
  • Remove VAT: net price = gross price ÷ (1 + rate ÷ 100); VAT = gross price − net price
  • From the VAT amount: net price = VAT ÷ (rate ÷ 100)
  • Find the rate: rate = VAT ÷ net price × 100, where VAT = gross price − net price

A worked example

A price of £49.99 includes 20% VAT. The net price is 49.99 ÷ 1.20 = £41.658…, shown as £41.66, and the VAT is 49.99 − 41.658… = £8.33. Taking 20% of £49.99 instead would give £10.00 of VAT, which is wrong because the VAT was worked out on the smaller net price.

Questions

How do I calculate VAT?

Multiply the net price by the rate divided by 100 to get the VAT, then add it to the net price for the total. At 20%, a £50 item has £10 of VAT and costs £60 in total. The Add VAT tab does this and shows the steps.

How do I remove VAT from a price?

Divide the price by 1 plus the rate. With 20% VAT, divide by 1.20; with 5%, divide by 1.05. Subtracting 20% of the gross price takes off too much, because the VAT was added to the smaller net price.

How do I calculate VAT from a total amount?

Multiply the total by rate ÷ (100 + rate). At 20%, the VAT inside £120 is £120 × 20/120 = £20; at 18%, the tax inside Rs 59,000 is Rs 9,000. The Remove VAT tab gives both the VAT and the net price.

What is the VAT formula in Excel?

With the net price in A2 and the rate in B2, =A2*B2 gives the VAT and =A2*(1+B2) the gross price. With a gross price in A2, =A2/(1+B2) gives the net price. Use ROUND(…, 2) to round to the cent.

What is the VAT rate in Pakistan?

Pakistan's standard sales tax rate on goods is 18%, which you can pick from the list. Some goods have other rates set by law, so check the rate on your invoice or with the tax authority.

Which VAT rate should I use?

Most goods and services use the country's standard rate, which you can pick from the list. Many countries also have reduced or zero rates for things like food, children's clothes or books. If you are not sure, check the item on your tax office's website or on the receipt.

Is GST the same as VAT?

They work the same way for this sum: a percent added to the net price. Australia, New Zealand, Singapore and India call it GST, and Pakistan calls it sales tax. US sales tax is also added on top, but it is set by each state and city.

Why do the rounded amounts sometimes not add up?

Each amount is rounded to the cent on its own, so the net price and the VAT can add up to one cent more or less than the gross price. Invoices usually round the VAT first and work out the other figure from it; the result notes when this happens.

Can I use pounds, euros or rupees?

Yes. Pick the currency symbol at the top of the calculator. The sums are the same in every currency.
