What voltage drop is and what this calculator shows

Voltage drop is the voltage a cable uses up because of its own resistance, so the load at the far end gets a little less than the supply. This voltage drop calculator works it out for a cable run: the volts lost, the drop as a share of the supply, the voltage left at the load, and the smallest wire size that keeps the drop within your limit.

It handles DC, single-phase AC and three-phase AC, copper or aluminium (and silver or gold), wire sizes in AWG or mm², and runs in PVC or steel conduit. Too much drop makes lamps dim, heaters weak and motors run hot, so it is worth checking on any long run.

How to use the voltage drop calculator

  • Pick a tab: Wire in conduit (AC effects included), Wire size only (plain DC resistance), or My own resistance (a value from a cable data sheet).
  • Choose US or Metric at the top to list wire sizes in AWG or mm², then pick the wire metal and size, and for conduit whether it is PVC or aluminium conduit, a cable, or steel conduit.
  • Supply voltage and Supply type: for example 120 V or 230 V single-phase, 400 V three-phase, or 12 V DC.
  • Load current in amps, and the one-way length of the run, from the supply to the load.
  • Power factor (AC in conduit): 1 for heaters and lamps, about 0.8 to 0.9 for motors.
  • More options: conductors in parallel, conductor temperature, frequency, reactance and the largest drop you accept (3% by default).

Voltage drop formula for single-phase, three-phase and DC

Voltage drop follows Ohm's law, V = I × R, applied to the wire. For DC and single-phase circuits the current flows out and back, so both wires count: Vdrop = 2 × L × I × R, with L the one-way length, I the current and R the resistance per unit length of one conductor. For a balanced three-phase circuit the factor is √3 instead of 2: Vdrop = √3 × L × I × R.

For AC the resistance is replaced by the effective impedance Z = R cos φ + X sin φ, where X is the cable's reactance and cos φ the power factor. With conductors in parallel, divide by the number of sets. The drop as a percentage is Vdrop ÷ supply voltage × 100.

Worked by hand with the My own resistance tab: a cable with 1.2 Ω/km, 60 m long, carrying 32 A on 230 V single-phase drops 2 × 60 m × 32 A × 0.0012 Ω/m = 4.608 V, which is 2.00% of 230 V and leaves 225.39 V at the load.

The resistance of a wire is its metal's resistivity divided by its cross-section, and it rises with temperature: R = ρ₂₀ × (1 + α × (T − 20 °C)) ÷ A. That is why the calculator asks for the running temperature, 75 °C by default in US mode and 70 °C in metric mode.

Choosing a wire size for voltage drop

The last line of the result is the smallest common wire size that keeps the drop at or under your limit. On a 230 V single-phase circuit carrying 25 A over 40 m, 4 mm² copper drops 9.35 V (4.07%), and the calculator suggests 6 mm², which drops 2.72%.

Voltage drop is only one test. The wire must also carry the current without overheating (its ampacity) and be protected by the right fuse or breaker, so always use the larger of the size for voltage drop and the size the current rating needs. For very large loads, several conductors in parallel per phase cut the drop in proportion: two sets halve it.

Worked examples: three-phase, aluminium and 12 V solar

  • Three-phase feeder: 16 mm² copper, 80 m, 50 A at 400 V and a power factor of 0.85 drops 7.89 V, only 1.97%, leaving 392.11 V. The √3 factor makes three-phase runs much more efficient than single-phase ones for the same current.
  • The same run in aluminium: 16 mm² aluminium has 2.1062 Ω/km instead of 1.2894 Ω/km for copper at 70 °C, so the drop rises to 12.7 V (3.17%), and the calculator suggests 25 mm² aluminium to stay under 3%.
  • 12 V DC solar or battery cable: 6 m of 4 mm² copper carrying 10 A drops 0.52 V, which is 4.31% of 12 V, and the calculator suggests 6 mm² (2.87%). Low-voltage systems are hit hardest, because the same 0.52 V is only 2.16% of a 24 V supply.
  • Use Wire size only for DC runs and quick checks: it uses the plain DC resistance at the temperature you set (20 °C by default).

Voltage drop across a resistor and in series or parallel circuits

The same law gives the voltage drop across any resistor: V = I × R. A 330 Ω resistor carrying 20 mA drops 6.6 V. In a series circuit the current is the same through every part, and by Kirchhoff's voltage law the drops across all the parts add up to the supply voltage, so each resistor's share is its resistance ÷ the total resistance × the supply.

In a parallel circuit every branch has the full voltage across it, so each branch's drop equals the supply voltage and only the currents differ. In a series-parallel circuit, reduce each parallel group to one resistance first, then share the voltage along the series path. The Ohm's law and resistor calculators handle these steps; this calculator focuses on the resistance of the wiring itself.

Acceptable voltage drop and why results differ from code tables

The calculator warns when the drop is over 3% for a branch (final) circuit and over 5% from the supply to the furthest load, which are common design targets. The limit that applies to you is set by the wiring code where you work, such as the NEC in the US or the national rules elsewhere, and some equipment needs tighter limits.

The resistance here comes from first principles, for a solid round conductor at the chosen temperature, with a standard allowance for reactance. Code tables such as NEC Chapter 9 Table 9 are based on stranded wire in conduit with measured values, so they often show a drop a few percent higher. For a design that must meet a code, check against the table that code uses.

Tips and common mistakes

  • Enter the one-way length. The formula already counts the return wire, so typing the round trip doubles the drop.
  • Use the current the circuit really carries, or the breaker rating for a worst case.
  • Do not forget the temperature: copper at 70 °C has about 20% more resistance than at 20 °C.
  • Check both voltage drop and ampacity; a size that passes one can fail the other.
  • For motors, use the running power factor; for heaters and lamps use 1.

How it works

Every wire has a little resistance, so current flowing through it uses up some voltage before it reaches the load. Choose the wire (metal and size in AWG or mm²), the supply (DC, single-phase or three-phase AC and its voltage), the current and the one-way length, and the calculator shows the volts lost, the share of the supply and the voltage left at the far end. The resistance is worked out from first principles: the resistivity of the metal at the wire's running temperature divided by its cross-section. For AC in conduit it also adds the small skin effect and the cable's reactance, weighted by the power factor of the load. A single-phase or DC circuit loses voltage in both the outgoing and the return wire (factor 2); a balanced three-phase circuit uses √3. The last row suggests the smallest common size that keeps the drop within your limit (3% by default).

The formula

  • Resistance per metre: R = ρ(T) ÷ A, with ρ(T) = ρ₂₀ × (1 + α × (T − 20 °C))
  • Copper (annealed standard): ρ₂₀ = 1/58 Ω·mm²/m = 1.7241 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m, α = 0.00393 per °C; aluminium: ρ₂₀ = 2.82 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m, α = 0.0039 per °C
  • AWG size n: diameter d = 0.127 mm × 92^((36 − n) ÷ 39), area A = π d² ÷ 4 (1/0 is n = 0, 4/0 is n = −3); kcmil: A = kcmil × 0.5067 mm²
  • AC in conduit: R(AC) = R × (1 + yₛ), skin effect yₛ = xₛ⁴ ÷ (192 + 0.8 xₛ⁴) with xₛ² = 8πf × 10⁻⁷ ÷ R; reactance X = 0.08 Ω/km × f ÷ 50 Hz (× 1.25 in steel conduit); Z = R cos φ + X sin φ
  • Drop: DC and single-phase V = 2 × L × I × Z ÷ n; three-phase V = √3 × L × I × Z ÷ n (L one-way length, n conductors in parallel per phase)
  • Drop % = drop ÷ supply voltage × 100; voltage at the load = supply − drop

A worked example

A 2.5 mm² copper cable, 30 m long, carrying 16 A on a 230 V single-phase supply: at 70 °C copper's resistivity is 2.063 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m, so the wire has 8.25 Ω per km. With a power factor of 0.85 and 0.08 Ω/km reactance, Z = 8.25 × 0.85 + 0.08 × 0.527 = 7.06 Ω/km. The drop is 2 × 30 m × 16 A × 0.00706 Ω/m = 6.77 V, which is 2.95% of 230 V, leaving 223.2 V at the load.

Questions

How do I calculate voltage drop in a cable?

Multiply 2 × the one-way length × the current × the resistance per unit length for DC or single-phase, or use √3 instead of 2 for three-phase. A 60 m run with 1.2 Ω/km at 32 A drops 2 × 60 × 32 × 0.0012 = 4.608 V. The calculator also adds the AC reactance and the temperature effect.

What is the voltage drop formula for 3 phase?

Vdrop = √3 × L × I × Z, with L the one-way length, I the line current and Z the impedance per unit length of one conductor. For 16 mm² copper, 80 m, 50 A at 400 V and a power factor of 0.85, that is 7.89 V or 1.97%.

How do I calculate voltage drop across a resistor?

Multiply the current through it by its resistance: V = I × R. In a series circuit the drops across all the resistors add up to the supply voltage; in a parallel circuit each branch has the full supply voltage across it.

What wire size do I need for voltage drop?

Enter your run and read the last line, the smallest size that keeps the drop within your limit (3% unless you change it). Then make sure that size is also rated for the current; use whichever of the two is larger.

How do I calculate voltage drop for 12 V DC or solar?

Choose DC as the supply type and enter 12 V, the current and the one-way length. 6 m of 4 mm² copper at 10 A drops 0.52 V, or 4.31%, so 6 mm² is the suggested size. Low voltages need thicker cable for the same current.

Why is my answer a little lower than an electrician's code table?

This calculator works the resistance out from the metal's resistivity and the conductor's area, as a solid round wire. Code tables such as NEC Chapter 9 Table 9 are measured for stranded wire in conduit and include conduit losses, so they often show a drop a few percent higher. For a design that must meet a code, check the result against the table your code uses.

How much voltage drop is acceptable?

Common design targets are no more than 3% on a branch (final) circuit and 5% in total from the supply to the furthest load, and the calculator warns above those. The limit that applies to you is set by your local wiring code, and some equipment needs a tighter one.

Which length do I enter?

The one-way length of the cable, from the supply or panel to the load. The formula already counts the return wire for DC and single-phase circuits.

Is the smallest suggested size safe to use?

Only for voltage drop. A wire must also be rated for the current it carries (its ampacity) and protected by the right breaker or fuse, so pick the larger of the two sizes.

What temperature should I use?

Use the temperature the wire runs at under load. Code tables use the insulation rating, 75 °C for most US building wire and 70 °C for PVC cable. At room temperature (20 °C) copper has about 16% less resistance than at 70 °C.
