What this IRA calculator compares

An IRA calculator shows how much your savings could grow by retirement and which kind of account leaves you the most after tax. An IRA, or individual retirement arrangement, is a US savings account with tax benefits. In a traditional IRA, contributions may be tax-deductible and growth is taxed only when you withdraw. In a Roth IRA, you put in money that has already been taxed, and qualified withdrawals are tax-free. The IRA itself is only a tax wrapper: inside it you choose the investments, such as funds, shares, bonds or bank deposits, and their returns drive the growth this page projects.

This page starts all three choices from the same pre-tax dollars, so the comparison is fair: a traditional IRA, a Roth IRA and an ordinary taxable brokerage or savings account. It grows each one with compound interest year by year, takes the tax off at the right moment, and tells you which ends ahead and by how much. It also checks your yearly amount against the IRS contribution limit for 2026 or 2027.

How to use the IRA calculator, step by step

  • Balance today (before tax): what you already have saved for retirement. For the Roth and taxable account it is treated as if moved over now, so today's tax rate is taken off it first.
  • Yearly contribution (before tax): what you set aside each year from pay before tax. It is added at the end of each year until you retire.
  • Your age and Retirement age: the years in between set how long the money grows.
  • Expected yearly return: the average growth you assume each year (6% by default). Returns are not guaranteed, so try more than one.
  • Your tax rate now and Tax rate in retirement: your top (marginal) income tax rates. The Income Tax Calculator can help you estimate them.
  • Under More options, pick the Account type for the IRS limit check (Traditional or Roth IRA, SIMPLE IRA or SEP IRA) and the Tax year of the limits (2026 or 2027).

How an IRA grows over time: a worked example

With the defaults, the traditional IRA grows from $20,000 by 6% a year, and $7,500 is added at the end of each year. After the first year it holds 20,000 × 1.06 + 7,500 = $28,700. The Roth starts from $20,000 × (1 − 0.22) = $15,600 and receives 7,500 × 0.78 = $5,850 a year. The taxable account receives the same after-tax amounts but only grows at 6% × 0.78 = 4.68% a year, because tax is taken from each year's gains.

Time does most of the work. A saver who starts at 25 with nothing and puts in $7,500 a year at 7% until 65 reaches $1,497,263 in a traditional IRA, more than double the default result, because the money has 40 years to compound instead of 30.

Traditional or Roth IRA: the tax rate rule

With the same tax rate now and in retirement, a traditional and a Roth IRA end with exactly the same after-tax amount. Paying 22% at the start or 22% at the end takes the same share of a pot that grows at the same rate. The choice comes down to whether you expect your tax rate to be higher or lower when you withdraw.

A lower rate later favours the traditional IRA, which is common for people whose income drops in retirement. A higher rate later favours the Roth, which can suit young savers in a low bracket today. Turn the default around, 12% now and 22% later, and the Roth comes out ahead by the same $70,781.

Other things can tip the balance. Roth IRAs have no required minimum distributions while the owner is alive, and regular Roth contributions can be taken back out without tax. Traditional IRA contributions may not be deductible at all if you or your spouse are covered by a workplace plan and your income is high, which removes their main advantage.

Income ranges are modified adjusted gross income (MAGI). Inside a range, the Roth amount you may put in, or the traditional deduction, shrinks step by step to zero at the top. For example, a single filer under 50 with a MAGI of $160,500 in 2026 sits halfway through the $153,000 to $168,000 Roth range, so the Roth limit is cut in half: $7,500 × ($168,000 − $160,500) ÷ $15,000 = $3,750. IRS Publication 590-A rounds the result up to the next $10 and allows at least $200 while you are inside the range. For comparison, the 2025 IRA limit was $7,000, or $8,000 from age 50, according to IRS Publication 590-A.

You also cannot put in more than your taxable pay for the year, and there is no longer an age limit for traditional IRA contributions. Contributions for a year can be made until that year's tax-filing deadline, not counting extensions; for 2025 that was generally 15 April 2026.

SEP IRA, SIMPLE IRA, 401(k) and employer matching

A SEP IRA is funded only by the employer, including a self-employed person paying into their own. The IRS allows up to 25% of each employee's pay, within the dollar cap in the table. For your own SEP as a self-employed person, pay means net self-employment earnings reduced by half your self-employment tax and by the SEP contribution itself, so the real share works out lower than 25%; IRS Publication 560 has the worksheet.

A SIMPLE IRA is for employers with generally 100 or fewer employees. Each year the employer either matches your deferrals up to 3% of pay or puts in 2% of pay for every eligible employee. Traditional and Roth IRAs you open yourself have no employer match.

To include a SIMPLE match here, add it to the yearly contribution; the limit check compares the whole amount with the employee limit, so a warning may appear even though the match itself does not count toward that limit.

A workplace 401(k) has much higher limits than an IRA: $24,500 of employee deferrals in 2026 and $25,500 in 2027, plus a catch-up of $8,000 in 2026 and $8,500 in 2027 from age 50 (IRS Notices 2025-67 and 2026-66). If your employer matches 401(k) contributions, that match is money an IRA cannot give you, so compare both before you decide where each dollar goes. You can have both, though a workplace plan can limit the traditional IRA deduction, as the table above shows.

Rollovers, transfers and Roth conversions

Money often reaches an IRA from an old employer's plan. The cleanest way is a direct rollover or a trustee-to-trustee transfer, where the money moves between institutions without passing through your hands. If a plan pays you instead, the IRS says it must withhold 20%, and you have 60 days to put the full amount into the new account, using your own money to replace what was withheld, or the missing part is treated as a withdrawal.

Between IRAs, you may make only one 60-day rollover in any 12-month period, counting all your IRAs together. Trustee-to-trustee transfers and conversions from a traditional to a Roth IRA do not count toward that limit.

A Roth conversion moves traditional IRA money into a Roth IRA. Under IRS Publication 590-A, the part that would have been taxed if you had withdrawn it is added to your income for the year of the conversion. That is exactly how this calculator treats today's balance in the Roth column: it pays your current tax rate first and then grows tax-free. If the result shows the Roth ahead, a conversion may be worth a closer look, ideally in a year when your income and tax rate are low.

IRA withdrawals, RMDs and early withdrawal penalties

Traditional IRA withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income, and IRS Publication 590-B says a 10% additional tax generally applies if you take money out before age 59½, unless an exception fits. If some of your contributions were not deductible, they form your basis, tracked on Form 8606, and that share of each withdrawal is not taxed again.

A Roth IRA withdrawal is a qualified, tax-free distribution when it comes at least five years after your first Roth contribution and you are 59½ or older, disabled, or using up to $10,000 (a lifetime limit) for a first home; payouts to heirs also qualify. Your regular contributions come out first and are never taxed again.

Required minimum distributions (RMDs) force traditional IRA owners to start withdrawing. Under current IRS guidance the first one is for the year you reach 73, and it can wait until 1 April of the next year. The RMD formula is the balance on 31 December of the previous year divided by your distribution period from the Uniform Lifetime Table: $500,000 at age 75 is 500,000 ÷ 24.6 = $20,325.20. A missed RMD may face a 25% excise tax, cut to 10% if corrected within two years. Roth IRAs need no RMDs while the owner is alive. The RMD Calculator works these out.

Tips, common mistakes and related calculators

  • Use your marginal rate, not your average rate. The tax saved or paid on the last dollars you earn is what matters for this choice.
  • Be honest about retirement income. Social Security, pensions and traditional IRA withdrawals together set your later tax rate.
  • Run more than one return. A 2-point change in the yearly return can move the result by hundreds of thousands of dollars over 30 years.
  • Do not compare a $7,500 Roth contribution with a $7,500 traditional one. The fair match is $7,500 traditional against $5,850 Roth at a 22% rate, which is what the calculator does.
  • Check the income limits before choosing a Roth or counting on a traditional deduction.
  • Remember that the results are estimates in future dollars, not adjusted for inflation, and they leave out fees and state taxes.

How it works

The calculator follows the same pre-tax money three ways. In a traditional IRA the whole amount goes in and grows, and you pay your retirement tax rate when you take it out. In a Roth IRA you pay today's tax rate first, so less goes in, but nothing is taxed later. In a taxable account you also pay today's rate first, and then tax on each year's growth too. Today's balance is treated the same way, as if it were moved into each account now. Deposits go in at the end of each year until you retire. The account with the most money after all tax is the better one for you.

The formula

  • Years n = retirement age − your age; yearly return R; tax now t₁; tax in retirement t₂
  • Traditional: B ← B × (1 + R) + C, starting from the balance S; after tax = B × (1 − t₂)
  • Roth: B ← B × (1 + R) + C × (1 − t₁), starting from S × (1 − t₁)
  • Taxable: B ← B × (1 + R × (1 − t₁)) + C × (1 − t₁), starting from S × (1 − t₁)
  • Closed form for the traditional balance: S × (1 + R)^n + C × ((1 + R)^n − 1) ÷ R
  • With t₁ = t₂ the traditional and Roth results are exactly equal

A worked example

The defaults: $20,000 today plus $7,500 a year from age 35 to 65 at 6%. The traditional IRA reaches $707,806, which is $622,869 after a 12% retirement tax. The Roth starts from $15,600 and adds $5,850 a year after 22% tax, reaching $552,089. The taxable account grows at 6% × 0.78 = 4.68% after tax and ends at $429,494. Here the traditional IRA wins by $70,781 because the tax rate falls from 22% to 12% in retirement.

Questions

How do I calculate IRA growth?

Grow the balance by the yearly return and add each year's contribution: S × (1 + R)^n + C × ((1 + R)^n − 1) ÷ R. With $20,000 today, $7,500 a year and 6% for 30 years that is $707,806 before tax. The calculator also takes off the tax for each account type.

Is a traditional or a Roth IRA better?

It mostly depends on your tax rate now compared with your rate in retirement. If you expect a lower rate later, the traditional IRA usually wins; if you expect a higher rate, the Roth wins. With the same rate both end with the same after-tax amount.

How much can I put in an IRA in 2026?

For 2026 and 2027 the limit is $7,500 a year across all your traditional and Roth IRAs, plus $1,100 more from age 50. A SIMPLE IRA allows $17,000 in 2026 and $17,500 in 2027 (more from 50), and a SEP IRA up to 25% of pay, capped at $72,000 in 2026 and $75,000 in 2027.

Why does the Roth start with less money?

To compare fairly, every account starts from the same pre-tax dollars. Money going into a Roth or a taxable account has already been taxed at your current rate, so only the after-tax part is invested.

Why is the taxable account the lowest?

It is taxed twice: once on the money you put in and again every year on the interest or gains it earns. The IRAs avoid the yearly tax, so their growth compounds on the full amount.

How do I calculate an IRA RMD?

Divide the account balance on 31 December of last year by the distribution period for your age in the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table. At 75 the period is 24.6, so a $500,000 IRA must pay out at least $20,325.20 that year.

Can I withdraw from an IRA before 59½?

You can, but traditional IRA withdrawals are taxed and a 10% additional tax generally applies before 59½ unless an IRS exception fits. Regular Roth contributions can come out at any time without tax; Roth earnings are tax-free only in a qualified distribution.

Does this include required minimum distributions or penalties?

No. It compares the values on the day you retire. Taking money out before 59½ can add a 10% penalty, and traditional IRAs need RMDs from age 73 under current IRS guidance; see the RMD calculator for those.

How do I calculate my IRA deduction?

If neither you nor your spouse has a workplace retirement plan, a traditional IRA contribution is fully deductible. If one of you does, the deduction shrinks across the income ranges in the limits table and disappears at the top; IRS Publication 590-A has the worksheet.

Can a child have an IRA?

Yes, if the child has taxable pay from work. The contribution for the year cannot be more than that pay or the yearly limit, whichever is lower, so a teenager who earns $3,000 can put in up to $3,000.

Do IRAs get an employer match?

Traditional and Roth IRAs you open yourself do not. A SIMPLE IRA does: the employer matches up to 3% of pay or gives 2% to every eligible employee. A 401(k) often has a match too; use the 401(k) calculator for that.

What is the IRA formula in Excel?

Use =FV(rate, years, -yearly contribution, -balance today), for example =FV(6%, 30, -7500, -20000) = $707,806.22 for the traditional IRA before tax. Multiply by (1 − retirement tax rate) for the after-tax value.
