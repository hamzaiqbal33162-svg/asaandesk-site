What this Canadian mortgage calculator works out

A Canadian mortgage calculator gives your regular payment on a home loan in Canada using the rules that apply there, which differ from US-style calculators in two important ways. Fixed rates are compounded twice a year rather than monthly, and a buyer with less than 20% down must pay for mortgage default insurance, usually from CMHC, which is added to the loan.

This page handles both. Enter the price, your down payment, the rate and the amortization, and it shows the payment for six payment frequencies, the insurance premium, the minimum down payment for that price, the total interest, the payoff date, and a full amortization schedule by year and by month. It can also add property tax, home insurance and condo fees so you see the real monthly cost of owning the home.

All amounts are in Canadian dollars. The rules described below were in force in November 2026; mortgage rules and rates change, so check with your lender before you commit.

How to use the Canadian mortgage calculator

  • Home price: the purchase price of the property. The default is C$700,000.
  • Down payment: switch between % and C$ and type your amount. The default is 10%. The result tells you the legal minimum for that price.
  • Amortization: the total years to repay the whole mortgage, 25 by default. With less than 20% down you can choose 30 years only if you tick First-time buyer or a newly built home.
  • Interest rate: the yearly rate your lender quotes, 4.5% by default.
  • Payment frequency: Monthly, Twice a month, Every two weeks, Every two weeks accelerated, Weekly, or Weekly accelerated. Every frequency is also compared in a table below the result.
  • Mortgage insurance and rate type: the CMHC box adds the default insurance premium automatically when you put down less than 20%. Rate type is Fixed (compounded twice a year) or Variable (compounded monthly).
  • Yearly costs: Property tax (as a % of the price or a dollar amount, 0.7% by default), Home insurance (C$1,800 a year), Condo or strata fee and Other costs. Untick the box to see the mortgage alone.
  • Dates: the First payment date sets the months shown in the schedule and the payoff date.

The headline shows the payment for the frequency you chose, and a second figure adds the yearly costs spread over each month. The rows and tables explain where the money goes.

The notes under the result flag anything you should know: the effective monthly rate, provincial sales tax on the premium, the 0.20-point premium surcharge for amortizations over 25 years, and a warning if your down payment is below the legal minimum or your amortization is too long for an insured mortgage.

The mortgage payment is only part of what a home costs each month. Property tax is set by your municipality and varies widely, so enter your city's figure as a percentage of the price or as a dollar amount. Home insurance protects the building and your belongings; it is separate from mortgage default insurance. In a condo or strata building, the monthly fee pays for shared upkeep, the building's insurance and its reserve fund, and it can rise over time.

Lenders count these costs too. When CMHC works out the gross debt service ratio, it includes principal, interest, property tax and heating, plus half of any condo fees. Tick Add property tax, insurance and other yearly costs and fill in your figures, and the Monthly with costs result and the cost table show the total you would actually pay each month and over the whole amortization.

Why Canadian mortgages compound twice a year

Section 6 of the federal Interest Act says a blended-payment mortgage must state its interest rate calculated yearly or half-yearly, not in advance; otherwise the lender cannot charge interest on the principal. Lenders therefore quote fixed rates compounded semi-annually. To get the rate for each payment, you convert the half-yearly rate, not divide the yearly rate by 12.

The formula for a monthly payment is: monthly rate i = (1 + yearly rate ÷ 2)^(1/6) − 1, then payment = mortgage × i ÷ (1 − (1 + i)^−n), where n is the number of payments. At 4.5%, i = 1.0225^(1/6) − 1 = 0.37153% a month, slightly less than 4.5% ÷ 12 = 0.375%. On the default mortgage of C$649,530 that is C$3,594.97 a month instead of C$3,610.30, about C$15 less, or roughly C$4,600 over 25 years.

To calculate a Canadian mortgage payment in Excel, use =PMT((1+4.5%/2)^(1/6)-1, 25*12, -649530), which returns 3,594.97. For bi-weekly payments, replace 1/6 with 2/26 and 25*12 with 25*26.

Variable-rate mortgages usually compound monthly, because the rate moves with the lender's prime rate. Choose Variable under Rate type to see the effect: at the default 4.5%, the monthly payment rises to C$3,610.30.

Minimum down payment and CMHC mortgage insurance

The minimum down payment depends on the price. For a home of C$500,000 or less it is 5%. From C$500,000 up to C$1.5 million it is 5% of the first C$500,000 plus 10% of the part above. From C$1.5 million it is 20% of the whole price, because insured mortgages are not available at that price. The C$1.5 million cap took effect on 15 December 2024; it was C$1 million before.

With less than 20% down, the lender needs mortgage default insurance, which protects the lender, not you, if you stop paying. The premium is a one-time percentage of the loan and is usually added to the mortgage, so you pay interest on it too. In Ontario, Quebec and Saskatchewan, provincial sales tax is charged on the premium; it cannot be added to the loan and is paid in cash at closing.

Loans at 80% or less are not required to be insured, but some lenders insure them anyway; the calculator only adds a premium below 20% down. When the amortization is longer than 25 years, insurers add 0.20 percentage points, so a 30-year first-time buyer mortgage with 5% down carries 4.20%.

CMHC, the Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation, is the federal Crown corporation for housing. Besides insuring mortgages, it guarantees Canada Mortgage Bonds, which lenders use to raise money for new mortgages.

Monthly, bi-weekly or accelerated payments

How often you pay changes very little unless you choose an accelerated option. An accelerated bi-weekly payment is half the monthly payment, made 26 times a year. That is 13 monthly payments a year instead of 12, and the extra money goes straight to the principal.

Worked examples

A 20% down payment with no insurance. An C$800,000 home with C$160,000 down needs a C$640,000 mortgage at 80% loan to value, so no premium is due. At 5% fixed over 25 years the monthly payment is C$3,722.27 and the total interest is C$476,681.57. A US-style calculator that divides 5% by 12 would show C$3,741.38.

A first-time buyer with the minimum down payment. A C$600,000 condo needs at least C$35,000 down (5% of C$500,000 plus 10% of C$100,000). The C$565,000 loan is 94.2% of the price, so the premium is 4.00%, plus 0.20 points for a 30-year amortization: C$23,730, making the mortgage C$588,730. At 4.2% fixed the monthly payment is C$2,866.54 over 30 years. Over 25 years the premium would be C$22,600 and the payment C$3,154.93, about C$288 more a month, but the total interest would fall from C$443,223 to C$358,879.

Passing the stress test. Lenders regulated by the federal government must check that you could afford the payment at a higher qualifying rate: the greater of your contract rate plus 2 points or 5.25%. For the default mortgage at 4.5%, that is 6.5%, which the calculator shows as a monthly payment of C$4,350.71. CMHC caps the gross debt service ratio (housing costs ÷ gross income) at 39%. With C$408.33 of property tax and, say, C$150 of heating a month, housing costs are C$4,909.04, so the household needs a gross income of about C$151,050 a year to qualify, before counting any other debts against the 44% total debt service limit.

Term, renewal, prepayment and rates

The amortization and the term are different things. The amortization is the full time to pay off the debt, often 25 years. The term is the length of your current contract and rate, usually 1 to 5 years. When the term ends you renew, often at a different rate, and the calculator's assumption of one rate for the whole amortization becomes an estimate.

A closed mortgage has a lower rate but limits extra payments and charges a penalty if you pay it off or break it early. For a fixed rate, the penalty is generally the larger of three months' interest or the interest rate differential, which depends on how rates have moved. Most closed mortgages still allow some prepayment each year, such as a lump sum or a higher regular payment, but the privileges differ from lender to lender. An open mortgage lets you repay any amount at any time without penalty, at a higher rate. Many mortgages are also portable, so you can take them to a new home.

Rates follow the market. Banks set their prime rate, which moves with the Bank of Canada's policy rate; the Bank raised that rate to 2.50% on 28 October 2026, and its next scheduled decision is on 9 December 2026. Variable-rate mortgages are priced as prime plus or minus a margin, while fixed rates track bond yields. Compare several quotes and use the calculator to see what each rate costs you in dollars.

Fixed or variable? A fixed rate keeps your payment the same for the whole term, which makes budgeting easy. A variable rate changes whenever prime changes. With some variable mortgages the payment moves with the rate; with others the payment stays the same and the share going to principal shrinks when rates rise, so the mortgage takes longer to pay off. Ask your lender which kind you are offered, and test a rate 1 or 2 points higher in the calculator to see whether you could cope.

Help for first-time buyers, tips and related tools

  • A First Home Savings Account (FHSA) lets you put in up to C$8,000 a year, C$40,000 in total, with a tax deduction; a qualifying withdrawal to buy your first home is tax-free.
  • The RRSP Home Buyers' Plan lets you withdraw up to C$60,000 from your RRSP for a first home and repay it over 15 years. For first withdrawals made from 2026 to 2028, repayments start in the fifth year after the withdrawal.
  • Budget for closing costs on top of the down payment: legal fees, land transfer tax in most provinces, the sales tax on the premium where it applies, inspections and moving.
  • Choose the shortest amortization you can comfortably afford. Five extra years lower the payment but add tens of thousands of dollars of interest.
  • Ask for accelerated bi-weekly payments if your pay schedule allows; it is the easiest way to save interest without a bigger commitment.

How it works

Enter the home price, your down payment, the amortization (the years to pay off the whole mortgage), the yearly rate and how often you pay. Canadian fixed-rate mortgages must state their rate compounded twice a year, so the rate per payment comes from that, not from the yearly rate divided by 12. With under 20% down the mortgage must be insured: the one-time CMHC premium, a share of the loan that depends on your down payment, is added to the mortgage. The payment is then the level amount that clears the mortgage by the end of the amortization. Accelerated payments are the monthly payment split in two (or four), which adds one monthly payment a year and ends the mortgage sooner.

The formula

  • Loan before insurance = price − down payment; loan to value = that ÷ price
  • Insurance premium = loan × rate (0.60% up to 65% loan to value, then 1.70%, 2.40%, 2.80%, 3.10% and 4.00% up to 95%; +0.20 points over 25 years); mortgage = loan + premium
  • Rate per payment i = (1 + yearly rate ÷ 200)^(2 ÷ payments a year) − 1 (fixed); variable: (1 + yearly rate ÷ 1200)^(12 ÷ payments a year) − 1
  • Payment = mortgage × i ÷ (1 − (1 + i)^−n), n = years × payments a year
  • Minimum down payment = 5% of the first $500,000 + 10% of the rest; 20% from $1.5 million

A worked example

An $800,000 home with 20% down needs a $640,000 mortgage, so no insurance is required. At 5% compounded twice a year the monthly rate is 1.025^(1/6) − 1 = 0.41239%, and over 25 years (300 payments) the payment is $3,722.27. Dividing 5% by 12 instead would give $3,741.38, about $19 a month too much.

Questions

How do I calculate mortgage payments in Canada?

Convert the quoted fixed rate to a monthly rate with (1 + rate ÷ 2)^(1/6) − 1, then use the usual loan formula: mortgage × i ÷ (1 − (1 + i)^−n). Add the CMHC premium to the mortgage first if you put down less than 20%. This calculator does all of that and shows six payment frequencies.

Why is a Canadian mortgage payment different from a US one at the same rate?

Canadian fixed-rate mortgages compound twice a year, as the Interest Act requires, while US loans compound monthly. The same 5% therefore costs a little less per month in Canada. Variable-rate mortgages usually compound monthly; pick Variable under the rate type to see that.

What is the Canadian mortgage formula in Excel?

Use =PMT((1+rate/2)^(1/6)-1, years*12, -mortgage). For a C$649,530 mortgage at 4.5% over 25 years, =PMT((1+4.5%/2)^(1/6)-1, 300, -649530) gives C$3,594.97, the same as this calculator.

When do I need CMHC insurance?

When your down payment is under 20% of the price. The premium is a one-time share of the loan, from 2.80% at 15% down to 4.00% at 5% down, and is normally added to the mortgage. Homes priced at C$1.5 million or more cannot be insured, so they need at least 20% down.

What is the minimum down payment in Canada?

5% of the first C$500,000 of the price plus 10% of the part above it, up to C$1.5 million. A C$700,000 home needs at least C$25,000 + C$20,000 = C$45,000. From C$1.5 million the minimum is 20% of the whole price.

What is the Canadian mortgage stress test?

Federally regulated lenders must check that you could still afford the payment at a qualifying rate: the greater of your contract rate plus 2 points or 5.25%. At a 4.5% contract rate you qualify at 6.5%. Enter 6.5% in the calculator to see the payment the lender will test.

Can I get a 30-year mortgage in Canada?

Yes. With 20% or more down, the insured-mortgage limits do not apply and your lender sets the longest amortization it offers. With less than 20% down, 30 years is allowed since 15 December 2024 only for first-time buyers and buyers of newly built homes, and the insurance premium is then 0.20 points higher.

What is the difference between the term and the amortization?

The amortization is how long it takes to pay off the whole mortgage, often 25 years. The term is how long your rate and lender contract last, usually 1 to 5 years, after which you renew, often at a different rate.

Is accelerated bi-weekly worth it?

Accelerated bi-weekly pays half your monthly payment every two weeks. That is 26 half payments a year, one more monthly payment than usual, so a 25-year mortgage ends about three to four years early and saves a lot of interest. Plain bi-weekly payments only spread the same yearly amount and save little.

Can I make extra or lump-sum payments?

Most closed mortgages allow some prepayment each year without penalty, such as a lump sum or a higher regular payment, but the limits differ by lender and are written in your contract. Open mortgages allow any amount. To see how much a lump sum would save, use our mortgage payoff calculator.

What is the Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation?

CMHC is the federal Crown corporation for housing. It sells mortgage default insurance to lenders for buyers with less than 20% down, guarantees Canada Mortgage Bonds and publishes housing research and data.

What is the mortgage prime rate in Canada?

Each bank sets its own prime rate, and it moves with the Bank of Canada's policy rate. The Bank raised the policy rate to 2.50% on 28 October 2026. Variable-rate mortgages are priced as prime plus or minus a margin, so check your lender's current prime.
