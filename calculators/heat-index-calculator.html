What the heat index means

The heat index is the "feels like" temperature on a hot day: what the air feels like to your body once humidity is added to the temperature. This heat index calculator works it out from the air temperature and the relative humidity, or the dew point, with the US National Weather Service (NWS) method, and shows the NWS risk level that goes with it.

Your body cools down when sweat evaporates from your skin. In humid air sweat dries slowly, so you lose heat more slowly and feel hotter than the thermometer says. In very dry air the opposite can happen, and the heat index can even be a little below the air temperature. The figure is for shade and a light breeze; it is an estimate of heat stress, not medical advice.

How to use the heat index calculator

  • Choose "With humidity" if you know the relative humidity (the % on a weather app), or "With dew point" if your forecast gives a dew point instead.
  • Enter the Air temperature and pick its unit: °C, °F or K.
  • Enter the Relative humidity in %, or the Dew point with its own unit. The dew point cannot be higher than the air temperature.
  • Read the heat index in your unit, the same figure in the other two units, the risk level with plain advice, and how much warmer (or cooler) than the air it feels.
  • The banded scale shows where the result sits from Caution to Extreme danger, and the steps show which NWS formula was used and any correction.

Heat index risk levels

The NWS groups heat index values into four levels. The table gives them in both units with the heat illnesses the NWS links to each.

The NWS notes that direct sunshine can raise the heat index by up to 15°F (about 8°C), so in full sun the real risk can be one level higher than shown.

Worked examples: Karachi, Lahore, Doha and the US

  • Humid coastal heat: suppose an afternoon in Karachi reads 34°C with 70% humidity. In °F that is 93.2°F; the NWS regression gives 116.2°F, so the heat index is 47°C (116°F), 12.8°C warmer than the air, in the Danger band.
  • Dry inland heat: suppose a very hot, dry day in Lahore reads 43°C with 20% humidity. The heat index is 44°C (111°F), only 0.9°C above the air, but still in the Danger band because the air itself is so hot.
  • With a dew point: suppose an evening in Doha, Qatar reads 38°C with a dew point of 28°C. The calculator first works out the relative humidity, 57%, then gives a heat index of 53°C (127°F), Extreme danger.
  • In Fahrenheit: 95°F and 55% humidity gives 108.9°F, about 109°F, in the Danger band. At 100°F and only 8% humidity the dry-air correction of −1.1°F brings it to about 93°F, which feels cooler than the air.

The heat index formula, for Excel or Python

The NWS method works in °F. First it tries the simple formula HI = 0.5 × (T + 61 + (T − 68) × 1.2 + 0.094 × RH). If the average of that result and T is under 80°F, that is the answer. Otherwise it uses the Rothfusz regression shown under How it works, then subtracts a correction when the humidity is under 13% between 80 and 112°F, or adds one when it is over 85% between 80 and 87°F.

To use it in Celsius, change °C to °F first (°F = °C × 9/5 + 32), work out the heat index, then change it back. In a spreadsheet, put T in A1 and RH in B1 and type the regression as one formula, with A1^2 for T² and B1^2 for RH²; in Python, write it the same way with ** for powers. Check your formula against this page with a few values from the chart above.

Heat index, wind, sun and work: what it leaves out

  • Wind: the heat index has no wind input. A breeze helps sweat dry, while still air in a trench or a closed room makes it feel worse.
  • Sun: the values are for shade. Full sun adds up to about 15°F (8°C) according to the NWS, and OSHA gives up to 13.5°F (7.5°C).
  • Workload and clothing: hard physical work and heavy protective clothing add body heat. OSHA calls the heat index a screening tool and recommends a wet bulb globe temperature (WBGT) meter at the work site, which includes sun and wind.
  • Low values still matter: OSHA reports outdoor workers dying of heat stroke on days when the highest heat index was only 86°F (30°C).
  • Cold weather: below about 50°F (10°C) humidity matters little and wind matters a lot; use the Wind Chill Calculator instead.

Staying safe when the heat index is high

  • Drink water regularly, before you feel thirsty, and limit sugary or alcoholic drinks.
  • Wear light, loose, light-coloured clothes and a hat; rest in the shade or indoors.
  • Move hard work, sport and errands to the early morning or evening, and take breaks.
  • Check on babies, older people, people with long-term illness and outdoor workers, who are at higher risk.
  • Know the warning signs: heavy sweating, cramps, dizziness, headache or nausea mean rest and cool down. Confusion or hot, dry skin can be heat stroke, which is an emergency.

How it works

Enter the air temperature and either the relative humidity or the dew point. Your body cools itself by sweating, and sweat dries more slowly in humid air, so humid heat feels hotter than dry heat at the same temperature. The heat index puts a number on that feeling, using the method of the US National Weather Service: a simple formula first, and when the result is about 80°F (27°C) or higher, the full NWS regression with its two corrections for very dry and very humid air. The result is matched to the NWS risk levels, from Caution to Extreme danger.

The formula

  • Simple formula (°F): HI = 0.5 × (T + 61 + (T − 68) × 1.2 + 0.094 × RH)
  • If the average of that and T is 80°F or more: HI = −42.379 + 2.04901523T + 10.14333127RH − 0.22475541T·RH − 0.00683783T² − 0.05481717RH² + 0.00122874T²RH + 0.00085282T·RH² − 0.00000199T²RH²
  • RH under 13% and T 80 to 112°F: subtract (13 − RH)/4 × √((17 − |T − 95|)/17)
  • RH over 85% and T 80 to 87°F: add (RH − 85)/10 × (87 − T)/5
  • Risk levels (°F): Caution 80 to 90, Extreme caution 90 to 103, Danger 103 to 125, Extreme danger 125 and above

A worked example

At 85°F (29.4°C) and 70% humidity the simple formula gives 86.5°F; its average with 85°F is above 80°F, so the full regression is used and gives 92.7°F. The heat index is about 93°F (34°C), in the Extreme caution band.

Questions

What does the heat index mean?

It is how hot it feels to a person in the shade with a light breeze, once humidity is taken into account. At 32°C (90°F) and 60% humidity it feels like about 37°C (99°F), because sweat cannot dry and cool you as well.

How do I calculate the heat index using temperature and humidity?

Enter both on the "With humidity" tab. The calculator turns the temperature into °F, applies the NWS simple formula, and switches to the full Rothfusz regression when the result is about 80°F or more. At 34°C and 70% humidity the heat index is 47°C (116°F).

How do I calculate the heat index in Celsius?

Pick °C for the air temperature. The NWS formula itself works in Fahrenheit, so the calculator converts your temperature, computes the heat index and converts it back, showing °C first with °F and K below.

What is the heat index formula?

The NWS uses HI = −42.379 + 2.04901523T + 10.14333127RH − 0.22475541T·RH − 0.00683783T² − 0.05481717RH² + 0.00122874T²RH + 0.00085282T·RH² − 0.00000199T²RH², with T in °F and RH in %. For mild values it uses a simpler formula, and it corrects for very dry or very humid air.

Can I use the dew point instead of humidity?

Yes. Choose "With dew point". The calculator works out the relative humidity from the dew point and the air temperature, then the heat index; 38°C with a 28°C dew point is 57% humidity and a heat index of 53°C.

Does wind change the heat index?

The heat index has no wind input; it assumes a light breeze. Stronger wind usually helps you cool, and still air makes it feel worse. For work sites OSHA recommends a WBGT reading, which includes wind and sun.

What is the difference between heat index and temperature?

The temperature is what a thermometer in the shade reads. The heat index adds the effect of humidity on how your body cools, so on a humid day it is higher than the temperature and on a very dry day it can be slightly lower.

Why is the heat index sometimes lower than the temperature?

In very dry air sweat dries quickly and cools you well, so a hot, dry day can feel slightly cooler than the thermometer says. The NWS formula includes a correction for humidity below 13%.

Does sunshine change the heat index?

Yes. The heat index assumes shade. Standing in full sun can make it feel up to about 8°C (15°F) hotter, so the real risk can be one level higher than shown.

How do I stay safe in high heat index weather?

Drink water often, wear light loose clothes, rest in the shade, and move hard work to the cooler morning or evening. Watch for heavy sweating, dizziness, headache or nausea. Confusion or very hot dry skin can mean heat stroke: call for emergency help.
