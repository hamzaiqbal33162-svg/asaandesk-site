What is BMR (basal metabolic rate)?

Your BMR is the smallest amount of energy your body needs to stay alive for a day: to keep the heart beating, the lungs breathing, the brain working and the body warm. This BMR calculator estimates it from your sex, age, height and weight, or from your lean mass, and gives the answer in kcal or kJ a day and per hour.

In a laboratory, BMR is measured with a person lying down, awake and relaxed, after 10 to 12 hours without food and 8 hours of physical rest, in a room neither warm nor cold enough to make the body work at heating or cooling (FAO, WHO and UNU, 2004). Few people ever have that test, so formulas built from many such measurements are used instead.

According to the same report, BMR makes up about 45 to 70% of an adult's total daily energy use, depending on age and lifestyle, and it depends mainly on age, sex, body size and body composition. That is why the calculator asks for exactly those inputs, and why the activity table underneath matters: your BMR alone is never what you should eat in a day.

How to calculate BMR with this calculator

  • Choose Metric (kg and cm) or US units (lb, feet and inches).
  • Pick Male or Female and enter your age (15 to 80), weight and height.
  • Open Formula and units to change the formula. Mifflin-St Jeor is the default; the revised Harris-Benedict formula is the classic alternative; Katch-McArdle needs your body fat percentage (the Body Fat Calculator can estimate it from a tape measure).
  • Choose whether to show energy in calories (kcal) or kilojoules (kJ); the other unit is always shown underneath.
  • Read your BMR, the same value per hour, and the table that multiplies your BMR by six activity levels to estimate what you burn on a normal day.

The three BMR formulas compared

All three formulas estimate resting energy from easy measurements, but they were built in different ways. The figures in the table come from the original papers.

The Mifflin-St Jeor authors found that the 1919 Harris-Benedict equations overestimated measured resting energy by about 5%. They also found that fat-free mass on its own predicted resting energy better than weight alone, which is the logic behind lean-mass formulas such as Katch-McArdle.

Same person, three formulas: a 28-year-old woman in Lahore, 158 cm and 62 kg, gets 1,307 kcal a day from Mifflin-St Jeor, 1,389 kcal from the revised Harris-Benedict formula, and 1,281 kcal from Katch-McArdle if her body fat is 32%. A spread of about 100 kcal between formulas is normal.

BMR by age for men and women

The formulas subtract a fixed amount for every year of age: 5 kcal a year in Mifflin-St Jeor, and about 5.7 (men) or 4.3 (women) in the revised Harris-Benedict formula. This table shows what that means for a reference man of 175 cm and 75 kg and a reference woman of 160 cm and 60 kg whose size stays the same.

Real ageing is less tidy. A 2021 study in Science that measured daily energy use in thousands of people found it fairly stable from about 20 to 60 once body size and lean mass were accounted for, with a decline after 60. Keeping muscle through strength training is the most practical way to keep your BMR up as you get older.

Worked examples in kg and lb

  • Metric: the Lahore example above, 1,307 kcal a day by Mifflin-St Jeor, is 5,470 kJ, or about 54 kcal an hour. With light exercise (1.375) she burns about 1,796 kcal a day; with daily exercise (1.55), about 2,025 kcal.
  • US units: a 50-year-old man, 5 ft 9 in and 185 lb, has a BMR of about 1,690 kcal a day (70 kcal an hour). Sedentary, his daily need is about 2,027 kcal; exercising 4 to 5 times a week, about 2,475 kcal.
  • The same man in metric, 175 cm and 84 kg, gets 1,689 kcal: 10 × 84 + 6.25 × 175 − 5 × 50 + 5 = 840 + 1,093.75 − 250 + 5 ≈ 1,689.
  • Pakistan reference: the Pakistan Dietary Guidelines for Better Nutrition (2018) use a BMR of 1,515 kcal for a 60 kg adult man and 1,241 kcal for a 55 kg woman when estimating national energy needs.

What raises or lowers your BMR

  • Lean mass: muscle and organs use far more energy at rest than fat tissue, so two people of the same weight can differ if one is more muscular. In the Mifflin-St Jeor study, fat-free mass was the best single predictor of resting energy.
  • Body size: a taller or heavier body has more tissue to maintain, so BMR rises with height and weight.
  • Sex: at the same height and weight, men usually have more muscle, which is why the formulas give them a higher value.
  • Age: the formulas lower BMR a little each year, mostly reflecting the slow loss of muscle in adulthood.
  • Dieting: losing weight lowers BMR because there is less body to run, and studies of very large weight losses have found an extra slowing that can last for years.
  • Health: thyroid problems, fever, illness, some medicines and pregnancy all change energy use, so a doctor should check if your numbers seem far off.

BMR, RMR and how accurate the formulas are

Resting metabolic rate (RMR) or resting energy expenditure (REE) is measured under looser conditions than BMR, for example without the full overnight fast, so it can read slightly higher. The formulas in this calculator were built from resting or basal measurements, and in everyday use the two terms are often treated as the same thing.

In a 2005 systematic review of prediction equations, Mifflin-St Jeor was the most reliable, predicting resting energy within 10% of the measured value in more non-obese and obese adults than any other equation. Roza and Shizgal put the precision of Harris-Benedict for a normal person at about 14%. The review also warned that older adults and US ethnic minorities were underrepresented in the studies, so for South Asian users the result is a well-informed starting point, not a measurement.

Tips: use a recent, accurate weight; choose Katch-McArdle only if your body fat figure is reliable; never eat less than your BMR for long without medical advice; and use the activity table, not the BMR alone, when planning meals. How it works

Your basal metabolic rate (BMR) is the energy your heart, brain, lungs and other organs use when you lie completely still for a whole day. For adults it is about 45 to 70% of everything you burn, depending on age and lifestyle. The calculator offers three well-known formulas: Mifflin-St Jeor (the default, using weight, height, age and sex), the revised Harris-Benedict formula, and Katch-McArdle, which uses only your lean mass and so needs your body fat percentage. The table multiplies your BMR by the usual activity factors to show what you burn on a normal day.

The formula

  • Mifflin-St Jeor: men BMR = 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age + 5; women the same − 161
  • Revised Harris-Benedict: men 13.397W + 4.799H − 5.677A + 88.362; women 9.247W + 3.098H − 4.330A + 447.593
  • Katch-McArdle: BMR = 370 + 21.6 × lean mass (kg), lean mass = weight × (1 − body fat % ÷ 100)
  • Daily need = BMR × activity factor: 1.2, 1.375, 1.465, 1.55, 1.725 or 1.9
  • 1 kcal = 4.1868 kJ

A worked example

A 40-year-old woman, 160 cm and 55 kg, with the revised Harris-Benedict formula: 9.247 × 55 + 3.098 × 160 − 4.330 × 40 + 447.593 ≈ 1,279 kcal a day, which is about 5,353 kJ. If she exercises 1 to 3 times a week she burns about 1,279 × 1.375 ≈ 1,758 kcal a day.

Questions

What is the difference between BMR and daily calories?

BMR is what you would burn lying still all day. Walking, working, exercising and digesting food add to it. Your daily need (often called TDEE) is BMR times an activity factor, shown in the table.

How do I calculate BMR for a woman?

With Mifflin-St Jeor: 10 × weight in kg + 6.25 × height in cm − 5 × age − 161. A 28-year-old woman of 62 kg and 158 cm gets 620 + 987.5 − 140 − 161 ≈ 1,307 kcal a day. The revised Harris-Benedict version is 9.247W + 3.098H − 4.330A + 447.593.

How do I calculate BMR for a man?

With Mifflin-St Jeor: 10 × weight in kg + 6.25 × height in cm − 5 × age + 5. A 50-year-old man of 84 kg and 175 cm gets about 1,689 kcal a day. The revised Harris-Benedict version is 13.397W + 4.799H − 5.677A + 88.362.

Which formula is the most accurate?

In comparison studies Mifflin-St Jeor came closest for most healthy adults, usually within 10% of a lab measurement. Harris-Benedict tends to read a little higher. If you know your body fat percentage, Katch-McArdle can be closer for very lean or very muscular people.

Can I calculate BMR with body fat percentage?

Yes. Choose Katch-McArdle under Formula and units and enter your body fat. The calculator works out your lean mass (weight × (1 − body fat ÷ 100)) and uses 370 + 21.6 × lean mass. It ignores height and age, so it rewards muscle rather than size.

Should I eat only my BMR to lose weight?

No. Your BMR is what you burn at complete rest, so eating only that amount is already a large deficit for anyone who moves. Use your daily need from the activity table, or the Calorie Calculator, and subtract a moderate amount such as 250 to 500 kcal.

Can a 13-year-old use the BMR calculator?

This calculator starts at 15, and the formulas were built from adult data. Children and young teens grow fast and need enough energy for growth, so their needs should be judged by a doctor or dietitian, often with growth charts.

Why does my BMR go down with age?

Most adults slowly lose muscle and their organs use a little less energy as they age, so the formulas subtract a small amount for each year. Strength training helps keep muscle and slows the drop.

Can I raise my BMR?

Building muscle raises it a little, because muscle uses more energy at rest than fat. Very low calorie dieting can lower it. Sleep, illness, thyroid problems and some medicines also change it, so ask a doctor if your numbers seem far off.

Is BMR the same as BMI?

No. BMR is energy (calories you burn at rest), while BMI is a size measure (weight divided by height squared). They use some of the same inputs, but they answer different questions; the BMI Calculator shows your weight class.
