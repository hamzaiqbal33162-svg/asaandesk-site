How it works

The first tab uses the official US consumer price index, CPI-U, published every month by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The index tracks the price of a fixed basket of goods and services, so the ratio of two index values tells you how much prices changed between two dates. Multiply your amount by that ratio to get the amount with the same buying power at the other date. Pick a month, or a whole year to use that year's average. The other two tabs skip the index and use one average rate you choose, which is handy for planning or for countries other than the US.

The formula

  • Value at the second date = amount × CPI at the second date ÷ CPI at the first date
  • Total inflation = (CPI later ÷ CPI earlier − 1) × 100
  • Average inflation a year = (CPI later ÷ CPI earlier)^(1 ÷ years between) − 1, where a whole-year average counts as July of that year
  • Future value at a flat rate r for n years = amount × (1 + r)^n
  • Past value at a flat rate r for n years ago = amount ÷ (1 + r)^n

A worked example

The 2016 average CPI is 240.007 and the October 2026 CPI is 337.511. So $100 in 2016 is worth 100 × 337.511 ÷ 240.007 = $140.63 in October 2026: total inflation of 40.63%. From the middle of 2016 to October 2026 is 10.25 years, so the average is 1.4063^(1 ÷ 10.25) − 1 = 3.38% a year.

Questions

Which inflation figures does this use?

The CPI-U for all items, U.S. city average, not seasonally adjusted, with 1982-84 = 100. It is the headline consumer price index the Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes each month and the one most often used to compare the value of money over time. The page says which month is the latest one in the table.

Why is there no figure for October 2025?

Prices for October 2025 were not collected because of the lapse in US federal funding that autumn, so the Bureau of Labor Statistics published no index for that month. We do not fill in a guess: pick September or November 2025, or the 2025 average, which BLS worked out from the other 11 months.

What is the difference between a month and the whole-year average?

A month uses that month's index. The whole-year average is the mean of the year's monthly figures, which smooths out seasonal ups and downs. It is a fair choice when you only know the year, such as an old salary or a house bought in 1995.

Can I use it for Pakistan, India or another country?

The first tab is for US prices only. For other countries use the Future value or Past value tab with that country's average inflation rate, for example a rate from the national statistics office or the central bank.

Does inflation mean my savings lose value?

Money kept as cash buys less when prices rise. The Future value tab shows what an amount kept as cash will buy later, in today's money. Savings that earn more than the inflation rate keep or grow their buying power.
