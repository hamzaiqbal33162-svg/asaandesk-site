How it works

The half-life is the time it takes for half of a decaying amount to disappear. After one half-life 50% is left, after two 25%, after three 12.5%, and so on. Choose what you want to find and fill in the other three values; use one time unit for everything (the unit menu under More options only labels the answers). The result also gives the mean lifetime τ and the decay constant λ, how many half-lives have passed, a table of the amount after each half-life and a decay curve. The second tab converts between half-life, mean lifetime and decay constant.

The formula

  • Amount left: Nₜ = N₀ × (1/2)^(t ÷ t½)
  • Starting amount: N₀ = Nₜ × 2^(t ÷ t½)
  • Time: t = t½ × log₂(N₀ ÷ Nₜ)
  • Half-life: t½ = t × ln 2 ÷ ln(N₀ ÷ Nₜ)
  • Mean lifetime τ = t½ ÷ ln 2, decay constant λ = ln 2 ÷ t½ = 1 ÷ τ

A worked example

100 g of a substance with a half-life of 5 years: after 10 years, 10 ÷ 5 = 2 half-lives have passed, so 100 × (1/2)² = 25 g is left. Its mean lifetime is 5 ÷ 0.693 = 7.21 years and its decay constant is 0.693 ÷ 5 = 0.139 per year.

Questions

What is the difference between half-life, mean lifetime and decay constant?

They are three ways to describe the same decay. The half-life is the time until half is left. The mean lifetime τ is the average time one atom lasts, about 1.44 times the half-life. The decay constant λ is the share that decays per unit of time, and it equals 1 ÷ τ.

Which units should I use?

Any, as long as they match. If the half-life is in years, type the time in years too, and the answer comes out in years. The amounts can be grams, atoms, percent or anything else, as long as both amounts use the same unit.

Does anything ever decay to exactly zero?

In this model, no: each half-life halves what is left, so the amount gets smaller and smaller without reaching 0. In practice, after about 10 half-lives less than 0.1% is left.

Why must the amount left be smaller than the starting amount?

Decay only removes material, so the amount left can never be larger than the amount you started with. If the two amounts are equal, no time has passed and the half-life cannot be worked out.

What half-life does carbon dating use?

Carbon-14 has a half-life of about 5,730 years. If a sample has a quarter of its original carbon-14 left, two half-lives have passed, so it is about 11,460 years old. Enter 100 and 25 as the amounts and 5730 as the half-life to check.
