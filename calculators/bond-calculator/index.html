How it works

A bond pays a fixed coupon every period and its face value at the end. Its price is all of those payments discounted at the yield to maturity, split into the same periods (a 6% yield paid twice a year is 3% a period). The first tab works on a coupon date: pick the value you want and the calculator finds it from the other four, solving numerically for the yield. The second tab prices a bond on any day between coupon dates: it finds the last and next coupon dates from the maturity date, counts the days with the chosen rule, and discounts every payment from today.

The formula

  • Price = C × (1 − (1 + i)^−n) ÷ i + face × (1 + i)^−n, with C = coupon each period, i = yield ÷ payments a year, n = periods left
  • Years to maturity: n = ln((C − i × face) ÷ (C − i × price)) ÷ ln(1 + i)
  • Accrued interest = C × days since the last coupon ÷ days in the period
  • Dirty price = Σ C ÷ (1 + i)^(w + k) + face ÷ (1 + i)^(w + n − 1), with w = 1 − accrued days ÷ period days
  • Clean price = dirty price − accrued interest

A worked example

A bond with a face value of 100, a 5% coupon paid once a year and 3 years left, at a 6% yield: 5 ÷ 1.06 + 5 ÷ 1.06² + 105 ÷ 1.06³ = 4.7170 + 4.4500 + 88.1600 = 97.3270. It sells below face value because its coupon is below the yield.

Questions

Why does a bond's price fall when yields rise?

The coupons are fixed. When new bonds pay more, an old bond is only worth buying at a lower price, which lifts its yield to the market level. When yields fall, its price rises above face value.

What is the difference between the clean and the dirty price?

The clean price is the quoted price. Between coupon dates the seller has earned part of the next coupon, so the buyer pays the clean price plus that accrued interest: the dirty price.

Which day count should I pick?

Use the one in the bond's terms. US corporate and municipal bonds usually use 30/360, US Treasuries Actual/Actual, and many money-market instruments Actual/360.

What is the current yield?

The yearly coupons divided by the price. It ignores the gain or loss as the price moves to face value at maturity, which the yield to maturity includes.

Why is the face value 100?

Bond prices are usually quoted per 100 of face value, so 97.327 means 97.327% of face. Enter the real face value, such as 1,000, to see amounts for one bond.
