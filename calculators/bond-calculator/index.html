What a bond calculator tells you

A bond calculator puts a fair price on a bond, or works back from a price to the yield you would earn. A bond is a loan you make to a government or a company. In return the issuer pays you interest, called the coupon, on fixed dates, and hands back the face value (also called par value) on the maturity date.

What you should pay today depends on the return you want. If you need 7% a year and the bond pays only a 5% coupon, you will buy it only below face value, and the gain as the price climbs back to par at maturity makes up the difference. This page does that discounting for you. It solves for any one of price, yield to maturity, face value, years to maturity or coupon, and it gives the clean and dirty price when you buy between two coupon dates.

How to use the bond calculator, step by step

There are two tabs. Use the first for a quick answer on a coupon date and the second when you know the exact settlement and maturity dates.

  • Choose your currency. Results are shown to four decimals because bonds trade in small fractions of a unit.
  • Price, yield or coupon tab: pick a value in the Work out the menu (Price, Yield to maturity, Face value, Years to maturity or Coupon). Its box disappears and the other four stay.
  • Fill in the rest. Face (par) value starts at 100, so a price reads as a price per 100 of face value, the way quotes are printed. Yield to maturity is a yearly percent, and Years to maturity can have decimals such as 4.5.
  • Coupon a year is either a percent of the face value or an amount of money a year; switch with the Coupon unit buttons. For a zero-coupon bond, enter 0.
  • Set Coupon paid to Once a year, Twice a year, Every quarter or Every month. The yield is split into the same periods, so a 6% yield paid twice a year is worked as 3% each half year.
  • Price between coupon dates tab: enter the face value, yield, coupon and how often it is paid, then the Maturity date and the Settlement (purchase) date, which starts as today. Pick the Day count rule written in the bond's terms: 30/360, Actual/360, Actual/365 or Actual/Actual.

Understanding the results

The first tab puts the value you asked for at the top. For a price, a note says whether it is below face value (a discount), above it (a premium) or exactly at par. The rows under it fill in the rest.

How to calculate bond price and yield to maturity

The bond price formula is a present value sum. Each coupon is divided by (1 + i) raised to the number of periods until it arrives, and the face value is discounted over the whole term. With C for the coupon each period, i for the yield each period and n for the periods left, Price = C × (1 − (1 + i)^−n) ÷ i + face × (1 + i)^−n.

Take a 10-year bond with a face value of 1,000 and a 5% coupon paid twice a year, when similar bonds yield 7%. Then C = 25, i = 0.035 and n = 20. The 20 coupons are worth 25 × 14.2124 = 355.31 today, the 1,000 repaid in year 10 is worth 1,000 ÷ 1.035^20 = 502.57, and the price is 857.88.

Yield to maturity runs the other way. It is the one rate that makes the discounted payments equal to the price you pay, so it is the return you earn if you hold to the end and every payment arrives. There is no neat formula for it; it is found by trial, and the calculator runs that search for you. Buy the same bond at 950 and the yield is 5.6617% a year, above the 5% coupon because you also gain 50 at maturity. Its current yield, 50 ÷ 950 = 5.2632%, leaves that gain out.

In Excel, PRICE(settlement, maturity, rate, yld, redemption, frequency, basis) returns the price per 100 of face value, and YIELD works back from a price. The basis codes match the day counts here: 0 or blank for US 30/360, 1 for Actual/Actual, 2 for Actual/360 and 3 for Actual/365, with 4 for the European 30/360. PRICE returns the clean price: Microsoft's own example (5.75% coupon, 6.5% yield, paid twice a year, bought 15 February 2008, maturing 15 November 2017, 30/360) gives 94.63, the clean price the dates tab shows for the same bond.

Why bond prices fall when interest rates rise

A fixed-rate bond keeps paying the same coupon whatever happens to market rates. When new bonds start paying more, an older, lower-coupon bond can only compete at a lower price, one that lifts its yield to the market level. When rates drop, the old coupon looks generous and the price rises above par. The US Securities and Exchange Commission calls this interest rate risk and points out that it applies even to US Treasury bonds.

How far a price moves depends mainly on two things. Longer maturities move more, because the fixed coupon is locked in for longer, and lower coupons move more, because more of the value sits in the distant face value. The table shows a 5% bond paying twice a year, per 100 of face value.

Duration puts one number on this sensitivity. FINRA's rule of thumb is that a bond's price moves by roughly its duration, in percent, for each 1 percentage point change in rates, in the opposite direction, and that the estimate gets less accurate for large moves. If you hold to maturity and the issuer pays in full, these swings do not change what you finally receive: the coupons and the face value.

Clean price, dirty price and day-count rules

Coupons are paid on fixed dates, but bonds change hands on any working day. Between two coupon dates the seller has already earned part of the next coupon, and the buyer pays that share back to them. That share is the accrued interest. Prices are quoted clean, without it, while the buyer pays the dirty (full or invoice) price: the clean price plus accrued interest.

Accrued interest = coupon each period × days since the last coupon ÷ days in the period. The day-count rule in the bond's terms decides how the days are counted. Under 30/360 every month counts as 30 days and the year as 360. Actual/360 and Actual/365 count the calendar days since the last coupon but assume a 360- or 365-day year. Actual/Actual counts calendar days both since the last coupon and in the whole coupon period.

Bond prices in Pakistan: Pakistan Investment Bonds

In Pakistan the best-known coupon bonds are Pakistan Investment Bonds (PIBs), issued by the Government of Pakistan through the State Bank of Pakistan (SBP). SBP's investor guide says fixed-rate PIBs pay a fixed coupon on face value every six months, are issued in multiples of PKR 100,000 and are held for you in an Investor Portfolio of Securities (IPS) account at a bank or primary dealer. You can enter SBP auctions through a non-competitive bid or buy from other holders in the secondary market, and you can sell through your bank before maturity.

Because PIB coupons come twice a year, choose Twice a year and enter the coupon as a percent. A PIB with PKR 100,000 face value, a 12% coupon and 5 years left pays Rs 6,000 every half year. If the market yield for that tenor is 11%, it is worth Rs 103,768.81, a premium; at 13% it is worth Rs 96,405.58, a discount. Your coupon is the same either way; only the price of buying it changes.

Coupon rates, auction yields and the tenors on offer change from one auction to the next, so take the yield from SBP's latest auction results or your bank's quote. The same guide says income tax is deducted from the profit under the law in force and Zakat is not deducted at source compulsorily; ask your bank for the current rate that applies to you.

Treasury, corporate, zero-coupon and savings bonds

US Treasury notes are sold with terms of 2, 3, 5, 7 or 10 years and Treasury bonds with terms of 20 or 30 years. Both pay interest every six months, and TreasuryDirect sells them in steps of $100 (rules as of November 2026). To price one, choose Twice a year and, between coupon dates, Actual/Actual.

Corporate and municipal bonds use the same maths, but their yields also carry a reward for the chance that the issuer cannot pay, so two bonds with the same coupon and maturity can trade at very different prices. The calculator takes the yield you enter as given; it does not judge credit quality.

A zero-coupon bond pays nothing until maturity and is sold below face value. Enter a coupon of 0: a 1,000 face value due in 10 years at a 5% yield compounded once a year is worth 1,000 ÷ 1.05^10 = 613.91. The Coupon paid choice then only sets how the yield compounds.

US savings bonds (Series EE and Series I) are a different product. They are held in a TreasuryDirect account rather than traded, can be cashed after one year (giving up three months of interest if cashed before five years), and EE bonds are guaranteed to double in value in 20 years. Their value follows Treasury's own rules, so use TreasuryDirect's Savings Bond Calculator for them.

Tips and common mistakes

  • Match the payment frequency. Entering a semi-annual bond as Once a year changes both the coupon timing and the compounding, and moves the price.
  • Read quotes as a percent of face value. A quote of 98.50 is 985.00 on a 1,000 bond and Rs 98,500 on a PKR 100,000 PIB.
  • Do not confuse the coupon rate with the yield. The coupon is fixed when the bond is issued; the yield moves every day with the price.
  • Compare bonds by yield to maturity, not by coupon, and compare yields on the same basis, such as both semi-annual.
  • Budget for the dirty price when you buy between coupon dates; the accrued interest is part of what leaves your account.
  • A callable bond can be repaid early. The yield to maturity assumes it runs to the final date, so also work out a yield to the call date by entering the years to the call and the call price as the face value.
  • Results are before tax and fees. Broker charges and tax on coupons lower what you keep.

How it works

A bond pays a fixed coupon every period and its face value at the end. Its price is all of those payments discounted at the yield to maturity, split into the same periods (a 6% yield paid twice a year is 3% a period). The first tab works on a coupon date: pick the value you want and the calculator finds it from the other four, solving numerically for the yield. The second tab prices a bond on any day between coupon dates: it finds the last and next coupon dates from the maturity date, counts the days with the chosen rule, and discounts every payment from the settlement date.

The formula

  • Price = C × (1 − (1 + i)^−n) ÷ i + face × (1 + i)^−n, with C = coupon each period, i = yield ÷ payments a year, n = periods left
  • Zero-coupon price = face ÷ (1 + i)^n
  • Years to maturity: n = ln((C − i × face) ÷ (C − i × price)) ÷ ln(1 + i)
  • Current yield = coupons a year ÷ price
  • Accrued interest = C × days since the last coupon ÷ days in the period
  • Dirty price = Σ C ÷ (1 + i)^(w + k) + face ÷ (1 + i)^(w + n − 1), with w = 1 − accrued days ÷ period days
  • Clean price = dirty price − accrued interest

A worked example

A 10-year bond with a face value of 1,000 and a 5% coupon paid twice a year, priced when similar bonds yield 7%: C = 25, i = 0.035 and n = 20. The coupons are worth 25 × (1 − 1.035^−20) ÷ 0.035 = 25 × 14.2124 = 355.31 today and the face value 1,000 ÷ 1.035^20 = 502.57, so the price is 857.88. It sells below face value because its coupon is lower than the yield.

Questions

How do you calculate a bond price?

Divide the yearly yield and coupon by the number of payments a year, discount every coupon and the face value at that rate per period, and add the results. A 5% coupon on 1,000 paid twice a year for 10 years, at a 7% yield, comes to 857.88. Pick Price in the Work out the menu to get it at once.

How do I calculate bond yield to maturity?

Yield to maturity is the rate that makes all the discounted coupons and the face value add up to the price you pay. It has no closed formula and is found by trial. Choose Yield to maturity, enter the price, face value, years left and coupon, and the calculator searches for it.

What is the difference between the coupon rate and the yield?

The coupon rate is fixed when the bond is issued and sets the interest paid on face value. The yield depends on the price you pay: buy below par and your yield is above the coupon; buy above par and it is below.

Why does a bond's price fall when yields rise?

The coupons are fixed. When new bonds pay more, an old bond is only worth buying at a lower price, which lifts its yield to the market level. When yields fall, its price rises above face value.

What is the difference between the clean and the dirty price?

The clean price is the quoted price. Between coupon dates the seller has earned part of the next coupon, so the buyer pays the clean price plus that accrued interest, which is the dirty price.

Which day count should I pick?

Use the one in the bond's terms. US corporate and municipal bonds usually use 30/360, US Treasuries Actual/Actual, and many money-market instruments Actual/360.

What is the current yield of a bond?

The coupons for a year divided by today's price. It ignores the gain or loss as the price moves to face value at maturity, which the yield to maturity includes, so the two differ whenever a bond trades away from par.

Why is the face value 100?

Bond prices are usually quoted per 100 of face value, so 97.29 means 97.29% of face. Enter the real face value, such as 1,000 or PKR 100,000, to see the amounts for one bond.

How is a Pakistan Investment Bond (PIB) price worked out?

The same way as any coupon bond: fixed-rate PIBs pay their coupon every six months, so choose Twice a year, enter the coupon rate, the years left and the current market yield for that tenor. Take the yield from SBP's latest auction results or your bank's quote.

Can I use this as a Treasury bond calculator?

Yes for marketable Treasury notes and bonds, which pay interest every six months: choose Twice a year and, for a date between coupons, the Actual/Actual day count. It does not value US savings bonds.

What is the bond price formula in Excel?

Use =PRICE(settlement, maturity, rate, yld, redemption, frequency, basis), which gives the clean price per 100 of face value; YIELD does the reverse. Basis 0 is 30/360, 1 Actual/Actual, 2 Actual/360 and 3 Actual/365, the same choices as the dates tab here.

Can this calculator value Series EE or Series I savings bonds?

No. Savings bonds are not traded and pay no coupons; their value follows Treasury's own interest rules. TreasuryDirect has a Savings Bond Calculator for them.
