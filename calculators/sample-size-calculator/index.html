How it works

To plan a survey, pick how sure you want to be (the confidence level), how close you need the answer (the margin of error, such as ± 5%), and the share you expect to answer one way (50% if you have no idea). The page gives the smallest number of people to ask. If the whole group is small, such as 800 staff, type the population size and the number drops. The second tool runs the other way: give the number of people who answered and the share you measured to get your margin of error. The z values come from the usual 2-decimal table (1.96 for 95%), and the page says which one it used.

The formula

  • n₀ = z² × p × (1 − p) ÷ e², with p and e as fractions (50% = 0.5)
  • Population of N: n = n₀ ÷ (1 + (n₀ − 1) ÷ N), then round up
  • Margin of error: e = z × √(p × (1 − p) ÷ n), times √((N − n) ÷ (N − 1)) for a population of N
  • z from the 2-decimal table: 80% 1.28, 90% 1.65, 95% 1.96, 98% 2.33, 99% 2.58, 99.9% 3.29

A worked example

For 95% confidence (z = 1.96), a margin of ± 5% and an expected 50%: n₀ = 1.96² × 0.5 × 0.5 ÷ 0.05² = 384.16, so you need 385 answers. In a company of 1,000 people: 384.16 ÷ (1 + 383.16 ÷ 1,000) = 277.8, so 278 answers are enough.

Questions

Why is 50% the safe choice for the proportion?

p × (1 − p) is largest at 50%, so 50% gives the biggest sample size. If the real share turns out to be 20% or 80%, your margin of error is only smaller than planned, never larger.

Why does a bigger population hardly change the answer?

Once the population is much larger than the sample, its size barely matters: about 385 answers give ± 5% at 95% confidence for a city or a whole country alike. The population size only helps when the sample would be a large part of the group.

Which z values does it use?

The 2-decimal values printed in most textbooks and survey tables, such as 1.65 for 90% and 1.96 for 95%, so the answers match those tables. The exact values (1.6449 and 1.9600) can give a slightly smaller sample, for example 1,083 instead of 1,089 for 90% confidence and ± 2.5%.

Does a sample size guarantee a good survey?

No. The formula assumes people are picked at random. A large sample of only volunteers, or with many people not answering, can still be biased. Ask more people than the result if you expect some not to reply.
