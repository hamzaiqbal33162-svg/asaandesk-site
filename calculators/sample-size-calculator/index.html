What sample size means in research and statistics

A sample size calculator tells you how many people, households, products or records you need to measure so that your result is close enough to the truth for the whole group. You rarely can ask everyone, so you ask a random sample and accept a known amount of uncertainty, called the margin of error.

Sample size determination is a trade-off between three things: how precise the answer must be (the margin of error), how confident you want to be that the true value lies inside that margin (the confidence level), and how varied the answers are likely to be (the expected proportion). Tighter margins and higher confidence both cost more people.

This page uses the standard formula for estimating a proportion, such as the share of customers who are satisfied or the share of households with a water filter. It is the formula behind most survey and cross-sectional study plans, with an optional correction when the whole population is small.

How to use the sample size calculator

  • Choose the tab: Find the sample size to plan a survey, or Find the margin of error to judge one you already ran.
  • Confidence level: pick from 70% to 99.999%. 95% is the usual choice and the default.
  • Margin of error (sample size tab): how close the result must be, plus or minus. It starts at 5%.
  • Expected proportion (sample size tab): the share you expect to answer yes. Leave it at 50% if you are not sure; that gives the largest, safest sample.
  • Sample size and Proportion you measured (margin tab): how many people answered and the share that said yes. They start at 100 and 60%.
  • Population size (both tabs): leave it empty for a very large or unknown population, or type the size of a small group, such as 1,000 employees, to apply the finite population correction.

The sample size formula with an example

The formula is n₀ = z² × p × (1 − p) ÷ e², where z is the critical value for your confidence level, p is the expected proportion and e is the margin of error, both written as decimals. It comes from the margin of error of a proportion, e = z × √(p(1 − p) ÷ n), solved for n. This is often called Cochran's formula, after the statistician William Cochran.

Step by step for 95% confidence, ± 5% and p = 50%: z² = 1.96² = 3.8416. p × (1 − p) = 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25. e² = 0.05² = 0.0025. So n₀ = 3.8416 × 0.25 ÷ 0.0025 = 384.16, and rounding up gives 385. You always round up, because 384 would fall just short of the precision you asked for.

For a known, finite population N, shrink it with n = n₀ ÷ (1 + (n₀ − 1) ÷ N). For a company of 1,000: 384.16 ÷ (1 + 383.16 ÷ 1,000) = 384.16 ÷ 1.38316 = 277.74, so 278 answers are enough.

Above about 20,000 the population barely changes the answer. A survey of 385 random people gives the same ± 5% for a university of 25,000 students in Karachi, a city of millions or the whole of Pakistan. What matters is the number of answers, not the share of the population. For small groups the correction saves a lot of work: 80 of 100 staff instead of 385.

Margin of error from a finished survey

The Find the margin of error tab runs the formula backwards: e = z × √(p(1 − p) ÷ n). With 100 answers and 60% saying yes at 95% confidence, e = 1.96 × √(0.6 × 0.4 ÷ 100) = 9.60%, so the true share is likely between 50.40% and 69.60%.

Example in Pakistan: a market research firm surveys 1,200 adults and finds 35% shop online. The margin is ± 2.70%, so the share is likely between 32.30% and 37.70%. With a known population, the correction shrinks the margin: 400 answers at 50% from a population of 2,000 gives ± 4.38% instead of ± 4.90%.

The margin is widest at 50% and narrows toward 0% or 100%. That is why the sample size tab assumes 50% when you have no better guess: a lower or higher real share only makes the result more precise than planned.

If a pilot study or an earlier survey shows the share is near 10%, you can use 10% and need far fewer people. But a ± 5% margin on a 10% share is very loose (5% to 15%), so rare outcomes usually call for a smaller margin, such as ± 2%.

Planning a real study, and the formula in Excel

  • Non-response: divide by the response rate you expect. If only 60% reply, invite 385 ÷ 0.6 = 642 people to end with 385 answers.
  • Cluster sampling: surveys that pick villages, schools or streets first and then people within them are less precise than a simple random sample. Multiply by the design effect, the factor by which clustering inflates the variance. With a design effect of 2, 385 becomes 770; use the value reported by similar studies.
  • Subgroups: if you need ± 5% for men and women separately, each group needs about 385, not the total.
  • Means instead of proportions: for an average, such as mean income, use n = (z × σ ÷ e)², where σ is the expected standard deviation and e the margin in the same units. With σ = 15 and e = 2 at 95%, n = (1.96 × 15 ÷ 2)² = 216.09, so 217.
  • Comparing two groups, clinical trials and A/B tests: these need a power analysis based on the smallest difference worth detecting, the significance level and the power (often 80%). This page does not cover that design.
  • Qualitative research: interviews and focus groups are sized by when new themes stop appearing, not by this formula.

Excel has no sample size function, but the formula fits in one cell. For 95% confidence, ± 5% and p = 50%: =ROUNDUP(NORM.S.INV(0.975)^2*0.5*0.5/0.05^2,0) returns 385. NORM.S.INV(0.975) is the exact z, 1.959964, so for some settings Excel can be one or two lower than the 2-decimal tables this page follows.

For a population in cell B1, wrap the result: =ROUNDUP(n0/(1+(n0-1)/B1),0), with n0 the unrounded value. For the margin of error of a finished survey: =1.96*SQRT(p*(1-p)/n).

Common mistakes

  • Treating the result as invitations rather than completed answers. Plan for non-response on top.
  • Using a convenience sample, such as only people who follow your page, and trusting the margin of error. The formula assumes random selection.
  • Typing the margin of error as 0.05 into a percent box. On this page type 5 for ± 5%.
  • Assuming a bigger population always needs a much bigger sample. Beyond a few tens of thousands it hardly changes.
  • Rounding down. Always round the sample size up.

How it works

To plan a survey, pick how sure you want to be (the confidence level), how close you need the answer (the margin of error, such as ± 5%), and the share you expect to answer one way (50% if you have no idea). The page gives the smallest number of people to ask. If the whole group is small, such as 800 staff, type the population size and the number drops. The second tool runs the other way: give the number of people who answered and the share you measured to get your margin of error. The z values come from the usual 2-decimal table (1.96 for 95%), and the page says which one it used.

The formula

  • n₀ = z² × p × (1 − p) ÷ e², with p and e as fractions (50% = 0.5)
  • Population of N: n = n₀ ÷ (1 + (n₀ − 1) ÷ N), then round up
  • Margin of error: e = z × √(p × (1 − p) ÷ n), times √((N − n) ÷ (N − 1)) for a population of N
  • z from the 2-decimal table: 80% 1.28, 90% 1.65, 95% 1.96, 98% 2.33, 99% 2.58, 99.9% 3.29

A worked example

For 95% confidence (z = 1.96), a margin of ± 5% and an expected 50%: n₀ = 1.96² × 0.5 × 0.5 ÷ 0.05² = 384.16, so you need 385 answers. In a company of 1,000 people: 384.16 ÷ (1 + 383.16 ÷ 1,000) = 277.8, so 278 answers are enough.

Questions

How do you calculate sample size?

Use n = z² × p × (1 − p) ÷ e². For 95% confidence (z = 1.96), p = 0.5 and a margin of 0.05 that is 384.16, so 385 people. For a small population, divide by 1 + (n − 1) ÷ N and round up.

What is the sample size formula for a known population?

First work out n₀ as for a large population, then apply the finite population correction n = n₀ ÷ (1 + (n₀ − 1) ÷ N). For N = 500 at 95% and ± 5% that gives 218 instead of 385.

Why is 385 the magic number in surveys?

It is the sample for 95% confidence, a ± 5% margin and the safest 50% proportion when the population is large. Because the population size stops mattering, the same 385 applies to a town or a country.

Why is 50% the safe choice for the proportion?

p × (1 − p) is largest at 50%, so 50% gives the biggest sample size. If the real share turns out to be 20% or 80%, your margin of error is only smaller than planned, never larger.

Why does a bigger population hardly change the answer?

Once the population is much larger than the sample, its size barely matters: about 385 answers give ± 5% at 95% confidence for a city or a whole country alike. The population size only helps when the sample would be a large part of the group.

How do I calculate sample size for a cross-sectional study?

For estimating a prevalence, use this page with the expected prevalence as the proportion, your margin of error and 95% confidence, then add for non-response and any cluster design effect. A prevalence of 20% with ± 5% needs 246 before those adjustments.

Which z values does it use?

The 2-decimal values printed in most textbooks and survey tables, such as 1.65 for 90% and 1.96 for 95%, so the answers match those tables. The exact values (1.6449 and 1.9600) can give a slightly smaller sample, for example 1,083 instead of 1,089 for 90% confidence and ± 2.5%.

How do I calculate sample size in Excel?

Type =ROUNDUP(NORM.S.INV(0.975)^2*0.5*0.5/0.05^2,0) for 95% confidence and ± 5%, which returns 385. Change 0.975 to 0.95 for 90% or 0.995 for 99%, and 0.05 to your margin.

What sample size do I need for a thesis survey?

Decide the margin of error your supervisor accepts, often ± 5% at 95% confidence, then use the population of your study group if it is small, such as the students of one college. Report the formula, the values used and the response rate in your methods chapter.

Does a sample size guarantee a good survey?

No. The formula assumes people are picked at random. A large sample of only volunteers, or with many people not answering, can still be biased. Ask more people than the result if you expect some not to reply.
