What a p-value is, in simple terms

A p-value calculator tells you how surprising your result would be if nothing special were going on. In a hypothesis test you start from a null hypothesis, such as "the new method makes no difference". The p-value is the probability of getting a test statistic at least as extreme as yours if that null hypothesis were true.

A small p-value, such as 0.01, means data like yours would be rare under the null hypothesis, so you have evidence against it. A large p-value, such as 0.40, means your result is the kind of thing chance alone produces often, so the data do not contradict the null hypothesis. They do not prove it either.

This page works with z-scores, the test statistic of z-tests and of many large-sample tests. It gives every tail area of the standard normal curve at once, and it can run backwards to find the critical z for a chosen p-value.

How to use the p-value calculator

  • Z-score: type your test statistic. The box starts at 2. Negative values are fine.
  • Leave the other boxes empty and read all five areas: Left tail P(x < Z), Right tail P(x > Z), Two tails P(x < −Z or x > Z), Mean to Z and Between −Z and Z.
  • To go backwards, empty the Z-score box and type one p-value into any of the five boxes, for example 0.05 in Two-tailed p-value. The page returns the matching z (the critical value) and the other four areas.
  • Each area is drawn on its own small bell curve with the shaded region, and the steps show how each one follows from the left-tail area.

How to calculate a p-value from a z-score by hand

  • State the null hypothesis and the direction of the test: two-tailed, right-tailed or left-tailed. Decide this before looking at the data.
  • Work out the z statistic. For a mean with known σ: z = (x̄ − μ₀) ÷ (σ ÷ √n). For a proportion: z = (p̂ − p₀) ÷ √(p₀(1 − p₀) ÷ n).
  • Find Φ(z), the area to the left of z, from a z table or this calculator.
  • Left-tailed: p = Φ(z). Right-tailed: p = 1 − Φ(z). Two-tailed: p = 2 × (1 − Φ(|z|)).
  • Compare p with your significance level α, usually 0.05. If p ≤ α, reject the null hypothesis; otherwise do not reject it.

Worked examples

Packet weights (mean and standard deviation): a company says its packets hold 500 g. A sample of 36 packets has a mean of 497.5 g, with a known standard deviation of 6 g. z = (497.5 − 500) ÷ (6 ÷ √36) = −2.5 ÷ 1 = −2.5. Typing −2.5 gives a left-tail p-value of 0.0062097 and a two-tailed p-value of 0.012419. Both are below 0.05, so the data are evidence that the packets are lighter than claimed.

Flour bags in Pakistan: a mill in Faisalabad fills 20 kg bags. An inspector weighs 49 bags and finds a mean of 19.93 kg; past records give σ = 0.21 kg. z = (19.93 − 20) ÷ (0.21 ÷ 7) = −0.07 ÷ 0.03 = −2.333. The left-tail p-value is 0.0098162, under 1%, so short filling is likely and worth checking.

A proportion: a shop believes 40% of customers pay by mobile wallet. In a sample of 400, 46% do. z = (0.46 − 0.40) ÷ √(0.40 × 0.60 ÷ 400) = 0.06 ÷ 0.024495 = 2.4495. The two-tailed p-value is 0.014305, significant at 5% but not at 1%.

Backwards, a critical value: typing 0.01 into the two-tailed box returns z = 2.5758. Any two-tailed test statistic beyond ±2.5758 is significant at the 1% level.

What p < 0.05 does and does not mean

The 0.05 cut-off is a convention, not a law of nature. Some studies use 0.01 or 0.001 instead, and a fuller report gives the exact p-value together with the size of the effect and a confidence interval, rather than a bare "significant".

In 2016 the American Statistical Association published a statement on p-values. Its main points, in short: a p-value shows how incompatible the data are with a stated model; it is not the probability that the hypothesis is true or that the result was due to chance alone; decisions should not rest only on whether p crosses a threshold; analyses should be reported in full; and a p-value says nothing about how big or important an effect is.

So a tiny p-value from a huge sample can come from a difference too small to matter, and a large p-value from a small sample can hide a real effect. Pair the p-value with a Confidence Interval Calculator result to see the size of the effect.

P-values for t, chi-square and other tests

This page uses the normal distribution, which suits z-tests and large samples. A t statistic from a small sample needs the t distribution with its degrees of freedom; for example t = 1.96 with 60 degrees of freedom has a two-tailed p-value of about 0.0546, a little above the 0.05 that z gives. Chi-square and F tests have their own distributions too.

In Excel, =NORM.S.DIST(z, TRUE) returns the left-tail area Φ(z), so =2*(1-NORM.S.DIST(ABS(z),TRUE)) is the two-tailed z p-value. =T.DIST.2T(t, df) gives a two-tailed t p-value. In R, pnorm(z) gives the left tail and 2*pnorm(-abs(z)) the two-tailed value.

Common mistakes

  • Reading p as the chance that the null hypothesis is true. It is the chance of data this extreme if the null hypothesis is true.
  • Choosing one tail after seeing which way the data went.
  • Treating p = 0.049 and p = 0.051 as opposite results. They are almost the same evidence.
  • Saying "accept the null hypothesis" when p is large. The correct wording is "fail to reject".
  • Running many tests and reporting only the significant ones. With 20 tests at the 5% level, one false alarm is expected by chance.

How it works

Type the z-score from your test (for example a z statistic of 2.1) and the page gives the p-value for each kind of test: left-tailed, right-tailed and two-tailed, plus the areas between the mean and Z and between −Z and Z. Each is drawn as a small bell curve with its area shaded. It also works backwards: empty the z-score box, type one p-value, such as 0.05 in the two-tailed box, and you get the z-score that matches it (the critical value) and the other areas. All areas come from the standard normal distribution.

The formula

  • Left tail: p = Φ(z)
  • Right tail: p = 1 − Φ(z)
  • Two tails: p = 2 × (1 − Φ(|z|))
  • From the mean: |Φ(z) − 0.5|; between −z and z: 2Φ(|z|) − 1
  • Backwards: z = Φ⁻¹(p) for a left-tail p, and z = Φ⁻¹(1 − p ÷ 2) for a two-tailed p

A worked example

A test statistic of z = 2 gives a right-tail p-value of 1 − 0.97725 = 0.02275. The two-tailed p-value is twice that, 0.0455, which is below 0.05, so the result is significant at the 5% level in a two-tailed test.

Questions

What is a p-value in simple terms?

It is how often you would see a result at least as extreme as yours if the null hypothesis were true. A p-value of 0.03 means such data would turn up about 3 times in 100 by chance alone, which is unusual enough that many studies call it significant.

How do I calculate a p-value from a z-score?

Find the area to the left of z under the standard normal curve, Φ(z). For a right-tailed test use 1 − Φ(z), for a left-tailed test Φ(z), and for a two-tailed test double the smaller tail. Typing z here does all three at once.

Should I use the one-tailed or the two-tailed p-value?

Use two-tailed when your test asks whether there is any difference (higher or lower). Use one tail when you only test one direction that you chose before seeing the data: the right tail for “greater than”, the left tail for “less than”.

What z-score matches p = 0.05?

For a two-tailed test it is 1.96 (2.5% in each tail). For a one-tailed test it is 1.6449. Type 0.05 into the matching box to see it.

What does a p-value of 0.05 mean in hypothesis testing?

It is the usual significance level: if p is 0.05 or less you reject the null hypothesis at the 5% level. It means the result would happen 5% of the time or less if the null hypothesis were true, not that there is a 95% chance your idea is right.

How do I calculate a p-value in Excel?

For a z statistic in cell A1, =2*(1-NORM.S.DIST(ABS(A1),TRUE)) gives the two-tailed p-value and =1-NORM.S.DIST(A1,TRUE) the right tail. For a t statistic use =T.DIST.2T(ABS(A1), df).

Can I get a p-value from the mean and standard deviation?

Yes, by turning them into a z statistic first: z = (sample mean − claimed mean) ÷ (σ ÷ √n). A sample of 36 with mean 497.5 against a claim of 500 and σ = 6 gives z = −2.5, which this page turns into p-values.

Why is my p-value so small it shows powers of ten?

Far from the mean the areas get tiny: a z-score of 6 has a right-tail p-value of about 9.87 × 10^-10. The page keeps those digits instead of rounding them to 0.

Does this work for t-tests or chi-square tests?

No, it uses the normal (z) distribution only. For a t statistic with large samples (over about 30 values per group) the normal p-value is close; for small samples use a t-distribution table or tool.

Is a smaller p-value a bigger effect?

Not necessarily. The p-value depends on both the size of the effect and the sample size, so a huge sample can make a tiny difference highly significant. Look at the effect size or a confidence interval to judge how large it is.
