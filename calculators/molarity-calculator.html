What molarity is

Molarity is the number of moles of a dissolved substance in one litre of solution. Its unit is moles per litre (mol/L), written M and read "molar". A molarity calculator turns the grams you weigh out into moles and divides by the volume of the solution, or works backwards to tell you how many grams to weigh or how much solution to make.

A mole is a fixed count of particles, 6.02214076 × 10²³ of them by the SI definition, and the molecular weight (molar mass) of a substance tells you how many grams one mole of it weighs. So 58.44 g of table salt (NaCl) is one mole, and dissolving it to make exactly 1 litre of solution gives a 1 M solution. IUPAC calls molarity "amount concentration".

The molarity formula and its rearrangements

Everything comes from two lines: moles n = mass ÷ molecular weight, and molarity c = n ÷ volume in litres. Put together, c = m ÷ (M × V). Solve for the other values and you get the mass to weigh, m = c × V × M, and the volume to make, V = m ÷ (M × c).

Molarity formula with examples

  • Molarity from grams: 4 g of sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 39.997 g/mol from the formula) dissolved to 250 mL. That is 0.1000 mol in 0.25 L, so the solution is 0.4000 M, with a mass concentration of 16 g/L.
  • Grams to weigh out: 500 mL of 0.2 M copper sulfate made from the blue pentahydrate, CuSO4·5H2O (249.677 g/mol). You need 0.2 × 0.5 = 0.1 mol, or 24.9677 g of the crystals. Using the formula without the water (CuSO4) would give a solution that is too weak.
  • Volume to make: 10 g of glucose (C6H12O6, 180.156 g/mol) as a 0.05 M solution fills 1.110 L, about 1,110 mL.
  • From mg/mL for a protein: 2 mg/mL of a protein of 50,000 g/mol is 2 g/L ÷ 50,000 g/mol = 0.00004 M, or 40 µM. Choose µM or nM for the answer when the molarity is very small.

Dilution, HCl and the molarity of pure water

Dilution (C1V1 = C2V2): to make 250 mL of 0.1 M NaCl from a 1 M stock, the final solution must hold 1.461 g of salt. On the Volume tab, 1.461 g at 1 M is 25 mL of stock, so measure 25 mL of the 1 M solution and add water up to 250 mL. The moles stay the same; only the volume changes.

Molarity of a concentrated acid from its label: molarity = density (g/mL) × 1,000 × mass fraction ÷ molar mass. For a hydrochloric acid label of 37% by mass and 1.19 g/mL, 1 litre weighs 1,190 g and holds 1,190 × 0.37 = 440.3 g of HCl. With the formula HCl (36.458 g/mol), the calculator gives 12.08 M. Always check your own bottle's label, and add acid to water, never water to acid.

Pure water: one litre of water at 20 °C weighs 998.21 g, and water (H2O) is 18.015 g/mol, so pure water is about 55.41 M.

Molarity, molality and normality

  • Molarity (M): moles of solute per litre of solution. Easy to make with a volumetric flask, but it changes slightly with temperature, because liquids expand when warm.
  • Molality (m): moles of solute per kilogram of solvent. It does not change with temperature, so it is used for boiling-point and freezing-point calculations. For dilute water solutions it is close to the molarity.
  • Normality (N): equivalents per litre, molarity × the number of reacting units per molecule. For sulfuric acid, which gives 2 H⁺ ions, 0.5 M H2SO4 is 1 N. IUPAC and NIST discourage the term normality, but older titration methods still use it.
  • Mass concentration (g/L or mg/mL) and ppm: grams of solute per litre. For dilute water solutions 1 ppm is about 1 mg/L, and the calculator converts these to molarity with the molecular weight.

In a titration, the moles of acid and base match at the end point, taking the reaction ratio into account. For a 1:1 reaction, concentration × volume of the titrant equals concentration × volume of the sample, so the unknown molarity is (c × V of titrant) ÷ V of sample.

Tips for making solutions accurately

  • Make up to the final volume. Dissolve the solute in part of the water, then fill a volumetric flask to the mark, because the solute takes up space too.
  • Use the formula you weigh. Hydrates such as CuSO4·5H2O include their water in the molecular weight; anhydrous salts do not.
  • Check case in formulas: Co is cobalt, CO is carbon monoxide. The formula box is case-sensitive for this reason.
  • Watch the units: mL to L is ÷ 1,000 and mg to g is ÷ 1,000, so mixing them up gives an answer 1,000 times off. Let the unit menus do it.
  • Small numbers: express tiny concentrations in mM, µM or nM rather than long decimals in M.

How it works

Choose what you want to find: the concentration of a solution, the mass of solute you need to weigh out, or the volume of solution to make. Enter the other values with their units, and give the molecular weight (molar mass) of the solute, either as a number or by typing its chemical formula. The calculator first turns the mass into moles (mass divided by molecular weight), then divides by the volume in liters to get moles per liter, or works backwards from a concentration. Mass concentrations such as g/L, mg/L or ppm are changed into moles per liter with the molecular weight.

The formula

  • Moles of solute: n = m ÷ M (m in grams, M in g/mol)
  • Molarity: c = n ÷ V (V in liters, c in mol/L = M)
  • Mass to dissolve: m = c × V × M
  • Volume to make: V = m ÷ (M × c)
  • Mass concentration: m ÷ V in g/L = molarity × M; 1 ppm = 1 mg/L and 1 ppb = 1 µg/L for water solutions

A worked example

Dissolve 100 g of table salt (NaCl, 58.44 g/mol) in water to make 2 L of solution: n = 100 ÷ 58.44 = 1.7112 mol, so c = 1.7112 mol ÷ 2 L = 0.8556 M, and the mass concentration is 100 g ÷ 2 L = 50 g/L. To make 500 mL of a 0.1 M glucose solution (180.16 g/mol) you need 0.1 × 0.5 × 180.16 = 9.008 g.

Questions

What is molarity?

Molarity is the number of moles of a substance dissolved in one liter of solution, written mol/L or M. A 1 M salt solution has 58.44 g of NaCl in every liter. Smaller units are millimolar (mM, a thousandth of a mole per liter), micromolar (µM) and nanomolar (nM).

How do I calculate molarity from grams?

Divide the grams by the molecular weight to get moles, then divide by the volume of solution in litres. For 4 g of NaOH (39.997 g/mol) in 250 mL: 4 ÷ 39.997 = 0.1 mol, and 0.1 ÷ 0.25 L = 0.4 M.

What is the molarity formula?

Molarity = moles of solute ÷ litres of solution, or M = mass ÷ (molecular weight × volume). Rearranged, mass = M × volume × molecular weight, which tells you how many grams to weigh out.

How do I calculate molarity with volume in mL?

Either change mL to litres first (divide by 1,000) or use M = mass × 1,000 ÷ (molecular weight × mL). The calculator accepts mL directly and shows the conversion in the steps.

How do I calculate the molarity of HCl from its density?

Multiply the density in g/mL by 1,000 and by the mass fraction to get grams of HCl per litre, then divide by 36.458 g/mol. A label of 37% and 1.19 g/mL gives 440.3 g/L, which is about 12.08 M.

What is the molarity of pure water?

About 55.4 M. A litre of water at 20 °C has a mass of 998.21 g, and dividing by 18.015 g/mol gives 55.41 moles per litre.

What is the difference between molarity and molality?

Molarity is moles per litre of solution; molality is moles per kilogram of solvent. Molality does not change with temperature, while molarity changes a little as the liquid expands or contracts.

How do I convert mg/mL to molarity?

mg/mL is the same as g/L, so divide it by the molecular weight in g/mol. A 2 mg/mL solution of a 50,000 g/mol protein is 0.00004 M, or 40 µM.

Is the volume the water I add or the final solution?

It is the final volume of the solution. In a lab you dissolve the solute in some of the water, then top up to the mark of a volumetric flask, because the solute itself takes up a little space.

Where do I find the molecular weight?

It is printed on the chemical's bottle or data sheet. You can also choose A formula and type the formula, such as NaCl or CuSO4·5H2O, and it is worked out from the IUPAC atomic weights. For a hydrate, use the formula with its water, because that is what you weigh.

How are ppm and mg/L related?

For dilute water solutions they are the same: 1 liter of water weighs about 1 kg, so 1 mg in a liter is one part per million by mass. For other solvents or strong solutions use g/L or mg/L instead.
