What is target heart rate?

Your target heart rate is the range of beats per minute (bpm) that keeps an exercise session in the effort zone you want, hard enough to train your heart and lungs but not so hard that you cannot keep going. This target heart rate calculator works it out from your age or a tested maximum heart rate, and from your resting heart rate if you know it, then splits the range into five training zones.

The American Heart Association (AHA) puts moderate-intensity activity at about 50 to 70% of your maximum heart rate and vigorous activity at about 70 to 85%. The headline result here is that whole 50 to 85% band, the usual target for steady aerobic exercise such as brisk walking, cycling, running or swimming.

These are estimates, not medical advice. If you have a heart condition, take medicine that affects your pulse, or are new to exercise after a long break, ask a doctor what range is safe for you.

How to use the calculator and read the results

  • Maximum heart rate: choose Estimate from my age and enter your age, or I know my maximum and enter a tested value from a supervised exercise test.
  • Resting heart rate (optional): your pulse counted for a full minute after waking up. With it the calculator uses the heart rate reserve (Karvonen) method; without it, the zones are a percent of your maximum.
  • More options: pick the formula for maximum heart rate (220 − age, Tanaka or Nes) and how to show the zones: heart rate reserve, percent of maximum, or the Borg 6 to 20 or CR10 exertion scales.
  • Results: your target range for aerobic exercise, your maximum heart rate, the lower and upper ends of the target and, with a resting rate, your heart rate reserve. Tables list the five training zones in bpm, the maximum from each formula at your age and, if chosen, the Borg scale in beats; a bar chart shows each zone.

Maximum heart rate formulas by age

Your maximum heart rate is the fastest your heart can beat in an all-out effort. It falls with age and differs between people of the same age, so formulas give an average. The calculator offers three: 220 − age, published by Fox and Haskell in 1971 and still the best known; 208 − 0.7 × age from Tanaka, Monahan and Seals (2001); and 211 − 0.64 × age from the HUNT Fitness Study in Norway (Nes and colleagues, 2013).

The Karvonen method and heart rate reserve

The Karvonen formula uses your resting heart rate as well as your maximum. Your heart rate reserve is maximum minus resting, and each target is resting + a percentage of the reserve. This matters because two 40-year-olds with the same maximum can have very different resting rates: a fit person's resting pulse is often lower, so their reserve is larger.

Take a 40-year-old with a resting pulse of 64. The maximum by 220 − age is 180 and the reserve is 116. By Karvonen, the 50 to 85% target is 122 to 163 bpm. By percent of maximum alone it would be 90 to 153 bpm. The Karvonen range starts higher because it never counts beats below your resting rate as exercise.

If you want the Karvonen target formula by hand: target = resting + intensity × (maximum − resting). For 70% intensity in the example: 64 + 0.70 × 116 ≈ 145 bpm.

Perceived exertion: the Borg scales and the talk test

Heart rate is not the only way to judge effort. The Borg rating of perceived exertion asks how hard the work feels: on the 6 to 20 scale, 6 is resting and 20 is maximal, and on the CR10 scale, 0 is resting and 10 is maximal. The calculator spreads each scale evenly across your heart rate reserve, so you can match a feeling to a number.

For a 55-year-old with a resting pulse of 58 and the Tanaka maximum of 170 bpm, a Borg rating of 12 (moderate) maps to about 106 bpm, 13 (somewhat hard) to 114 bpm, 15 (hard) to 130 bpm and 17 (very hard) to 146 bpm. For a tested maximum of 178 and a resting pulse of 55, CR10 rating 3 (moderate) is about 92 bpm and 5 (hard) about 117 bpm.

The CDC's talk test is the simplest check of all: during moderate activity you can talk but not sing, and during vigorous activity you cannot say more than a few words without pausing for breath.

Worked examples

  • Age 40, resting 64, 220 − age: maximum 180 bpm, reserve 116, aerobic target 122 to 163 bpm.
  • Age 25, resting 72, shown as percent of maximum: maximum 195 bpm and a target of 98 to 166 bpm; the light zone is 117 to 137 bpm and the moderate zone 137 to 156 bpm.
  • Age 55, resting 58, Tanaka formula: maximum 208 − 0.7 × 55 = 169.5, rounded to 170 bpm; target 114 to 153 bpm by Karvonen.
  • Age 62, no resting rate, Nes formula: maximum 211 − 0.64 × 62 = 171.3, rounded to 171 bpm; target 86 to 145 bpm as a percent of maximum.
  • Tested maximum 178, resting 55: reserve 123 bpm and a target of 117 to 160 bpm. A runner in Lahore training for a 10 km race could keep easy runs in the light zone, 129 to 141 bpm, and save the hard zone, 153 to 166 bpm, for one session a week.

Target heart rate for weight loss, running and stress tests

Fat burning and weight loss: some machines label a low zone the fat-burning zone. Easy efforts can be kept up for longer, but for weight loss the total energy you use matters most, and harder efforts burn more calories per minute. The Calories Burned Calculator shows the difference between activities, and the best zone is the one you will keep doing most days.

Running and cardio: build most of your weekly running in the light and moderate zones, and add tempo work in the hard zone once you have a base. Pair the zones with pace using the Pace Calculator.

Stress test (TMT): in a treadmill exercise test the usual goal is 85% of the age-predicted maximum heart rate, with the speed and incline raised in stages about every 3 minutes until that target is reached or symptoms appear (MSD Manual). For a 55-year-old, 85% of 220 − 55 = 165 is about 140 bpm, the top of this calculator's percent-of-maximum target. The test is run and stopped by medical staff, not by these numbers.

Resting heart rate, medicines and safety

  • Resting heart rate: the AHA says a resting rate of 60 to 100 bpm is normal for most adults. Measure it in the morning before getting up, on three days, and use the average.
  • Medicines: the AHA notes that some drugs lower your maximum heart rate and target zone. Beta blockers are a common example, so ask your doctor for a safe range if you take them.
  • Heart conditions and symptoms: stop and get help if you feel chest pain, severe breathlessness, dizziness or an irregular heartbeat during exercise.
  • Heat, dehydration, caffeine, stress and illness can raise your heart rate at the same effort, so use the Borg scale or talk test alongside the numbers on those days.
  • Children: the age formulas were made for teenagers and adults, and the calculator warns that estimates for a child under 15 are rough.

How it works

Your maximum heart rate is estimated from your age (or you enter a tested value). If you also enter your resting heart rate, the calculator uses the Karvonen method: it takes the gap between resting and maximum, called the heart rate reserve, and adds a share of it to your resting rate. This fits fit and unfit people better than a plain percent of the maximum, which is used when no resting rate is given. The target for steady aerobic exercise is 50 to 85% of that range. Under More options you can change the age formula or show the zones on the Borg exertion scales, which link how hard an effort feels to a heart rate.

The formula

  • Maximum heart rate: 220 − age (Fox and Haskell), 208 − 0.7 × age (Tanaka), or 211 − 0.64 × age (Nes), rounded to a whole beat
  • Heart rate reserve = maximum − resting
  • Target (Karvonen) = resting + p% × reserve; without a resting rate: target = p% × maximum
  • Borg 6 to 20: heart rate = resting + (rating − 6) ÷ 14 × reserve
  • Borg CR10: heart rate = resting + rating ÷ 10 × reserve
  • All results are rounded to whole beats per minute

A worked example

At 40 with a resting pulse of 64: maximum = 220 − 40 = 180 and the reserve is 180 − 64 = 116. The aerobic target runs from 64 + 0.50 × 116 = 122 to 64 + 0.85 × 116 ≈ 163 beats per minute.

Questions

How do I calculate my target heart rate?

Estimate your maximum as 220 minus your age, then take 50% and 85% of it. At 40 that is 180, so the target is 90 to 153 bpm. If you know your resting pulse, the Karvonen method gives a more personal range, which the calculator works out for you.

The target heart rate formula starts with 220 and you subtract what?

Your age in years. 220 − age gives an estimated maximum heart rate, for example 220 − 30 = 190 bpm; your target zones are then percentages of that number, or of your heart rate reserve.

How do I calculate target heart rate with the Karvonen formula?

Subtract your resting heart rate from your maximum to get the reserve, multiply by the intensity and add the resting rate back. With a maximum of 180 and a resting rate of 64, 70% intensity is 64 + 0.70 × 116 ≈ 145 bpm.

What is the target heart rate for fat burning or weight loss?

There is no special fat-burning number. Any steady effort in the 50 to 85% range helps, and for weight loss the total calories used over the week matter most, so choose a zone you can keep up regularly.

What is the target heart rate for zone 2?

In a five-zone system, zone 2 is usually 60 to 70% of your heart rate reserve or maximum. For a 40-year-old with a resting pulse of 64 that is about 134 to 145 bpm by the Karvonen method.

What is the target heart rate for a stress test (TMT)?

Exercise stress tests usually aim for 85% of the age-predicted maximum heart rate, about 140 bpm at age 55. The test team decides when to stop, based on your heart rate, symptoms and ECG.

How do I measure my resting heart rate?

In the morning, before getting up, count your pulse at the wrist or neck for a full minute. Do it on three mornings and use the average.

Which maximum heart rate formula is best?

220 − age is the simplest and most widely known. The Tanaka and Nes formulas come from newer studies and give higher values after about age 40. Any formula can be 10 or more beats off for one person; a supervised exercise test gives your real maximum.

Is the target heart rate different for women?

This calculator uses the same formulas for women and men, and the AHA chart is the same for both. Your own resting rate and, if you have one, a tested maximum make the result more personal than any formula.

Which zone should I train in?

Most of your training for health and endurance can sit in the light and moderate zones. Short spells in the hard and maximum zones build speed but need more recovery.

What is the Borg scale?

It rates how hard an effort feels. On the 6 to 20 scale, 6 is resting and 20 is an all-out effort; on the CR10 scale, 0 is resting and 10 is maximal. The tables show the heart rate that matches each rating for you.

Do medicines change my heart rate?

Yes. Beta blockers and some other medicines lower the heart rate, so these targets may be too high. Ask your doctor for a safe range.
