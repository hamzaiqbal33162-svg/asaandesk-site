How it works

Every tab turns future pension payments into one value today, using the return you could earn by investing the money yourself and the yearly cost-of-living rise of the pension. The first tab adds up the monthly pension month by month from your retirement age and finds the first age at which it is worth more than the lump sum: live longer than that and the pension wins. The second tab prices the survivor protection of a joint-and-survivor pension: how big a lump sum would replace it, the most a term-life policy can cost to make the single-life pension plus insurance the better deal, and whether investing the extra single-life income beats the survivor pension. The third tab compares retiring earlier on a smaller pension with working longer for a bigger one.

The formula

  • Monthly rate from a yearly return R: r = (1 + R)^(1/12) − 1
  • Tab 1: value of the pension for Y years = Σ P × (1 + COLA)^(whole years) ÷ (1 + r)^m, over months m = 0 … 12Y − 1; break-even = retirement age + the first Y with value ≥ lump sum
  • Tab 2: survivor lump sum = Σ 12 × joint × (1 + COLA)^j ÷ (1 + R)^j for each year of your spouse's life from your retirement
  • Tab 2: difference invested = Σ 12 × (single − joint) × (1 + COLA)^j × (1 + R)^(n − 1 − j), n = your life expectancy − retirement age + 1
  • Tab 3: break-even = the first age L at which Σ 12 × later pension × (1 + COLA)^j ÷ (1 + R)^(j + gap) ≥ Σ 12 × earlier pension × (1 + COLA)^j ÷ (1 + R)^j

A worked example

Offered $800,000 now or $5,000 a month from 65 that rises 3.5% a year, with a 5% return the monthly pension's value passes $800,000 after 16 years of payments. So if you expect to live to 81 or longer the pension is the better deal; if not, the lump sum is.

Questions

Should I take the lump sum or the monthly pension?

The break-even age answers it in money terms: if you expect to live past it, the monthly pension is worth more. A lump sum gives you control and something to leave to heirs; a pension cannot run out and protects you if you live a long time.

What does joint and survivor mean?

A joint-and-survivor pension pays a smaller amount while you are alive and keeps paying your spouse after you die. A single-life pension pays more but stops when you die.

Can life insurance replace a survivor pension?

Sometimes. If a term-life policy that pays out the survivor lump sum costs less each month than the difference between the two pensions, taking the single-life pension plus the policy can leave your spouse just as protected and you with more money.

What return should I enter?

Use what you could realistically earn on the money yourself after fees, such as a balanced fund return of 4% to 6%. A higher return favours the lump sum or retiring earlier; a lower one favours the monthly pension or working longer.

Is the lump sum taxed?

Here it is assumed to be rolled over into an IRA or another untaxed account. If you would cash it out, income tax and possibly a 10% early-withdrawal penalty apply, so enter the amount you would keep.
