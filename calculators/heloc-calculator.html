What a HELOC calculator works out

A HELOC calculator shows what a home equity line of credit will cost you month by month. A HELOC is a revolving credit line secured by your home: the lender approves a limit based on your equity, and you borrow from it when you need to, much like a credit card with your house as security.

The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) describes two stages. During the draw period, which could last 10 years, you can borrow up to the limit. When it ends you can no longer borrow, and the repayment period begins, often over 10 or 20 years, with monthly payments that are usually much higher. Most HELOCs also have a variable rate, so the payment can change from month to month.

This page works out the interest-only payment while you draw, the higher payment once repayment starts, the total interest, the effect of closing costs and yearly fees, and how big a line your equity may allow.

How to use the HELOC calculator, step by step

The Payments tab prices a line you already have in mind; the How big a line can I get? tab estimates the limit.

  • Credit line: the limit you are offered. Interest rate: the yearly rate today. The calculator keeps it the same throughout, so try a higher rate as well.
  • Draw period and Repayment period: the years of each stage from the lender's terms. A draw period of 0 gives an ordinary loan paid off from the first month.
  • Tick Include closing cost and yearly fee to add them. Enter the Closing cost as an amount or a percent of the line, choose whether it is Paid in cash or Taken from the line, and enter the Yearly fee charged in each year of the draw period.
  • Under More options, Amount you use lets you price only part of the line; leave it blank to use the whole line at the start. First payment date sets the dates in the schedule.
  • How big a line can I get? tab: enter the Home value today, the Mortgage still owed (all loans on the home, 0 if it is paid off) and the limit the lender uses in Lender lends up to, from 60% to 90% of the value.

How to calculate a HELOC payment

The HELOC payment formula changes with the stage. In the draw period, an interest-only payment is the balance times the monthly rate: payment = balance × yearly rate ÷ 12. On the default $50,000 at 8%, that is 50,000 × 0.08 ÷ 12 = $333.33, and the balance stays at $50,000 however long you pay it.

In the repayment period the balance is paid off like a normal loan. With r for the monthly rate and m for the number of months, payment = balance × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−m). For $50,000 over 240 months at 0.6667% a month, the payment is $418.22.

The interest is easy to check by hand for the draw years: $4,000 a year, or $40,000 over ten years, without reducing the debt at all. The repayment years add another $50,372.81 of interest, which is why the total interest of $90,372.81 is larger than the amount borrowed.

If you use only part of the line, the payments scale with it. Using $20,000 of the $50,000 line costs $133.33 a month while you draw and $167.29 after that, with $36,149.12 of interest in total.

How much can I borrow with a HELOC?

Lenders cap all the debt on your home with a combined loan-to-value (CLTV) limit. The line you may get is that limit times the home's value, minus what you still owe: line = CLTV limit × home value − mortgage balance. To find your current loan-to-value ratio, divide what you owe by the home's value.

With the defaults, a home worth $500,000 with $250,000 owed has $250,000 of equity, a loan-to-value of 50%. A lender that lends up to 80% in total allows $400,000 of debt, so the line could reach $150,000; at 85% it could reach $175,000.

If you already owe more than the limit, there is no room. A $400,000 home with $330,000 owed is at 82.5%, above an 80% limit, and the calculator warns that you may not qualify with that lender. Lenders also check your credit, income and other debts, and some lend less on rental or second homes. The value they use comes from their own valuation, which can be lower than your estimate, so try a cautious figure too.

In Canada, OSFI's Guideline B-20 expects federally regulated lenders to keep a HELOC to a loan-to-value of 65% or less, while conventional mortgages without insurance go up to 80%. The menu here has 60% and 70%; for 65%, multiply the value by 0.65 and subtract what you owe.

Costs, fees, taxes and your rights

A HELOC can carry closing costs when it is opened and a yearly fee while the line is open; the lender lists them in its disclosures. The CFPB also notes that some plans require minimum draws or a minimum balance, and that a lender may freeze your access to the line if home values drop or your finances change.

Under the US Truth in Lending rules (Regulation Z, 12 CFR 1026.40), the APR disclosed for a credit line is its interest rate, without those costs. That is why the APR row always matches your rate. The Yearly cost with fees row is our estimate of the rate once closing costs and fees are counted: on the default line, a $1,000 closing cost and a $50 yearly fee turn 8% into about 8.27%.

Interest on a HELOC is not always tax-deductible. For 2025 returns, IRS Publication 936 says interest on a loan secured by your home can be deducted only when the money was used to buy, build or substantially improve that home, and only on up to $750,000 of mortgage debt ($375,000 if married filing separately) taken out after 15 December 2017. Using the line for a car, a holiday or credit card debt does not qualify. Check the latest edition of Publication 936 for the year you file.

When you open a HELOC on your main home, Regulation Z (12 CFR 1026.15) gives you a right to cancel it until midnight of the third business day after the plan is opened, you receive the notice of that right and all the required disclosures, whichever comes last.

What happens when the HELOC rate changes

Because most HELOC rates are variable, the rate itself is often the biggest risk. A variable rate is usually an index, such as the prime rate, plus a fixed margin that the lender sets, so when the index rises your payment rises too. HELOC, home equity loan or cash-out refinance?

  • HELOC: borrow as you need, pay interest only on what you use, usually at a variable rate. Suits costs that come in stages, such as a renovation or school fees.
  • Home equity loan: one lump sum at a fixed rate with fixed payments from the start. Suits a single known cost; compare it with the Home Equity Loan Calculator.
  • Cash-out refinance: a new, larger first mortgage that replaces the old one. It can make sense when new mortgage rates are not higher than your current one; see the Refinance Calculator.
  • Personal loan: unsecured, so your home is not at stake, with a fixed payment, but usually at a higher rate and for a shorter term. Price one with the Personal Loan Calculator and compare the totals.
  • Using a HELOC to pay off a mortgage or credit cards moves the debt onto your home. The CFPB warns that if you cannot repay a HELOC on schedule, you could lose your home.

Tips, common mistakes and related calculators

  • Budget for the repayment payment, not the draw payment. The low interest-only figure is temporary.
  • Test a higher rate. Run the calculator again with the rate 2 points higher to see how much room your budget has.
  • Pay principal during the draw years if you can. Every dollar repaid early stops costing interest, and the later payment jump is smaller.
  • Count all the costs. Compare offers by the cost of the line and the yearly cost with fees, not by the rate alone.
  • Borrow only what you need. A large unused limit costs nothing in interest, but a large balance on your home puts it at risk.
  • Remember that the results assume the whole amount is used on day one and the rate never changes; real balances and rates move.

How it works

A HELOC has two stages. During the draw period you can borrow from the line and usually pay only the interest, so the balance does not go down. When the draw period ends, the repayment period starts: the balance is paid off in equal monthly payments of principal and interest, which is when the payment jumps. The calculator assumes you use the whole line (or the amount you type) at the start and that the rate stays the same, then adds up every payment. With costs switched on it adds the closing cost and the yearly fee, and shows the yearly rate that includes them. The second tab shows how big a line your equity may allow: the lender lets all loans on the home together reach a set share of its value, the combined loan-to-value limit.

The formula

  • Draw period: payment = amount used × r, with r = yearly rate ÷ 12; the balance stays the same
  • Repayment period: payment = amount × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−m), with m = repayment years × 12
  • Total paid = draw payment × draw months + repayment payment × m; total interest = total paid − amount used
  • Cost of the line = total interest + closing cost + yearly fee × draw years
  • Yearly cost with fees: the monthly rate at which all payments and fees are worth the amount used minus the closing cost, times 12
  • Line you may get = lender's limit % × home value − mortgage owed

A worked example

The default line of $50,000 at 8% with a 10-year draw and a 20-year repayment: 50,000 × 0.08 ÷ 12 = $333.33 a month for 10 years, then $418.22 a month for 20 years. All 360 payments add up to $140,372.81, of which $90,372.81 is interest.

Questions

How do I calculate a HELOC payment?

During the draw period, multiply the balance by the yearly rate and divide by 12: $50,000 at 8% is $333.33 a month. In the repayment period, use the loan payment formula balance × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−m), which gives $418.22 over 20 years. The calculator shows both at once.

How do I calculate a HELOC interest-only payment?

Multiply what you owe by the yearly rate and divide by 12. The payment covers only the interest, so the balance stays the same; it changes only when you draw more or repay principal.

Why does my HELOC payment go up after the draw period?

During the draw period most HELOCs need only the interest, so none of the balance is paid off. When repayment starts, the whole balance has to be cleared over a shorter time, so each payment carries principal as well as interest. Plan for that jump.

How much can I borrow with a HELOC?

Multiply your home's value by the lender's combined loan-to-value limit and subtract what you owe. A $500,000 home with $250,000 owed and an 80% limit leaves room for a $150,000 line. Your credit and income also count.

How do I calculate my HELOC LTV?

Add all the loans on the home, including the full credit line, and divide by the home's value. A $250,000 mortgage plus a $150,000 line on a $500,000 home is $400,000 ÷ $500,000 = 80%.

Is the HELOC rate fixed?

Usually not. Most HELOC rates are an index, often the prime rate, plus a margin, and change when the index moves. The calculator keeps one rate so you can compare; try a higher rate to see how the payments could change.

What is the APR of a HELOC?

US rules define a credit line's APR as its interest rate, without the closing cost or yearly fees, which is why the two match here. To compare the full cost, look at the yearly cost with fees and the cost of the line.

Do I have to use the whole line?

No. You pay interest only on what you use. Open More options and type the amount you plan to use to see payments on that.

Can I make extra payments on a HELOC?

Usually yes, and paying principal during the draw period lowers both the interest and the later payment. This calculator assumes interest-only payments while you draw; to see the effect of a lower balance, enter it in Amount you use.

Is HELOC interest tax-deductible?

In the US only when the money buys, builds or substantially improves the home that secures the line, under IRS Publication 936 for 2025 returns. Interest on money used for other things is not deductible. Check the current edition for the year you file.

Is a HELOC a good way to pay off a mortgage or credit cards?

It can lower the rate, but it turns unsecured debt into debt secured by your home, and the rate can rise. Compare the full cost with the debt consolidation or refinance calculators before you decide.

Does this HELOC calculator work in Canada?

The payment maths is the same, but the currency is shown in dollars and Canadian rules differ. OSFI expects federally regulated lenders to cap a HELOC at 65% of the home's value, so use that figure for the limit.
