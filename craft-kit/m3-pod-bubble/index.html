Midnight on the Food Street

SISana Iqbal · 21 Nov · 9 min read

Listen to this story12 min · read by Ali Raza
0:00-12:04

The old city does not sleep so much as change its clothes. By eleven the cloth sellers have pulled their shutters down, and the lane outside the fort walls fills with charcoal smoke, folding chairs and the slow traffic of families who came for dinner and stayed for the second one.

Rafiq has run the same tea stall for twenty-six years. He pours from shoulder height so the chai comes down in one amber ribbon, then hands it over without looking, already reaching for the next glass. The trick, he says, is the cardamom. Then he admits the real trick is never letting the pot go quiet.

Two stalls down, the griddle men work in pairs. One chops, one stirs, and the iron plate rings like a bell every time their spatulas meet. Chicken, liver and a fistful of green chillies hit the metal, and the smell reaches the end of the street before the order is even written down.

Nobody comes here for one dish. You come for the street, and the street feeds you in courses.

At half past twelve a boy wheels in a kulfi cart, its brass lid fogged with cold. Children who were asleep on their fathers' shoulders wake up as if someone had called their names.

The best seats are not seats at all but the steps of an old haveli, where you can watch the whole lane at once: the lanterns swinging on their wires, the waiters running trays above their heads, the cats who know exactly which stall drops the most bones.

Around one, the music changes. Someone's phone plays an old film song, a second phone joins a beat late, and for a minute the whole street hums the chorus while naan keeps coming out of the tandoor in soft, blistered sheets.

Ayesha and her sisters drive in from Model Town every Thursday. They order the same four plates in the same order and argue about the same thing every week: whether the fried fish was better last year. It was not, they agree at two in the morning, and order a second plate to be sure.

When the call to prayer drifts over the rooftops before dawn, the stalls start packing up. Chairs fold, the charcoal hisses under a bucket of water and Rafiq rinses his last glass. The walls turn pink, then gold, and the street becomes a road again, as if none of it happened.
